## 1. EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689): which obligations apply to (a) vet-reviewed owner articles, (b) a local-first pet health booklet, (c) an AI documentation assistant for vets — and when (incl. digital omnibus, Code of Practice, Article 50 guidelines)

### Takeaway
As of 18 Oct 2026 the binding AI Act obligations for this product set are Article 50 transparency (applicable since 2 Aug 2026; machine-readable marking grace period for generative systems already on the market until 2 Dec 2026) and Article 4 AI literacy (applicable since 2 Feb 2025, softened by the Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 Jul 2026, to a duty to "take measures to support" literacy). Nothing in Annex III or the MDR route makes veterinary documentation or pet-owner information high-risk, and the omnibus pushed Annex III obligations to 2 Dec 2027 anyway. For vet-reviewed articles, the Article 50(4) human-review exception is available only with genuine expert review including fact-checking, no AI edits after sign-off, and a publicly named person or entity holding editorial responsibility, per the final Commission guidelines of 20 Jul 2026.

### Cited Findings
**Timeline and the Digital Omnibus on AI**
- The Digital Omnibus on AI was adopted as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of 8 July 2026, published in the OJ on 24 July 2026 (OJ L, 2026/1744) and in force on the third day after publication, i.e. 27 July 2026. It amends Regulations (EU) 2024/1689, 2018/1139 and 2023/1230 — [EUR-Lex, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng) (opened 2026-10-18).
- Amended Article 113: Chapters I and II (including Art. 4 AI literacy and Art. 5 prohibitions) apply from 2 Feb 2025. The new prohibitions in Art. 5(1)(ba)/(bb) and 5(1a)/(1b) (nudifier/CSAM-generating systems) apply from 2 Dec 2026. Chapter III Sections 1–3 apply from 2 Dec 2027 for Annex III high-risk systems and 2 Aug 2028 for Annex I systems. Articles 102–110 apply from 27 Jul 2026 — [EUR-Lex 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng).
- New Article 111(4): "providers of generative AI systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026" must "take the necessary steps in order to comply with Article 50(2) by 2 December 2026." High-risk systems used by public authorities must comply by 2 Aug 2030 — [EUR-Lex 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng).
- Article 50(7) is replaced. The Commission must "encourage and facilitate" Union-level codes of practice on detecting, marking and labelling AI-generated content, assess whether adherence ensures compliance with Art. 50(2) and (4), and may adopt an implementing act if a code is inadequate — [EUR-Lex 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng).
- Article 4 is replaced. The new text reads "Providers and deployers of AI systems shall take measures to support the development of AI literacy", and "This obligation does not require providers or deployers to guarantee any specific level of AI literacy of any individual". The Commission and Member States must support this, and the AI Board must adopt recommendations — [EUR-Lex 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng). The original 2024 text required providers and deployers to "ensure, to their best extent, a sufficient level of AI literacy of their staff" — [EUR-Lex AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689).
- Article 99 (penalties) amended. Member States must consider SME/SMC economic viability, and for small mid-caps (SMCs) each fine is capped at the lower of percentage or amount (new 99(6a)). Art. 63(1) gives SMEs simplified quality-management elements — [EUR-Lex 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng).
- Legislative history (secondary): Commission proposal 19 Nov 2025; provisional political agreement 6 May 2026; Council confirmation 13 May 2026 — [Gibson Dunn, 27 May 2026](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/). Cooley (3 Aug 2026) reports the "strict necessity" threshold was kept for processing special-category data to detect and correct bias, now covering non-high-risk systems too. It also reports that GPAI obligations (Arts. 51–56) are unchanged, and that the AI Office gains exclusive supervision of AI systems built on GPAI models where model and system come from the same provider or group — [Cooley](https://cdp.cooley.com/digital-ai-omnibus-delays-key-deadlines-introduces-new-rules/).

**Article 50 text (unchanged in substance by the omnibus, except 50(7))**
- Art. 50(1): providers must design systems that interact directly with natural persons so those persons are informed they are interacting with an AI system, "unless this is obvious from the point of view of a natural person who is reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect". Art. 50(2): providers of systems generating synthetic audio, image, video or text must ensure outputs are "marked in a machine-readable format and detectable". This does not apply where the system performs "an assistive function for standard editing or do[es] not substantially alter the input data". Art. 50(4) second subparagraph: deployers of systems generating or manipulating text "published with the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest" must disclose this, except "where the AI-generated content has undergone a process of human review or editorial control and where a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility for the publication". Art. 50(5): information must be "clear and distinguishable", at the latest at first interaction or exposure, and accessible — [EUR-Lex AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689).
- Penalty: non-compliance with "transparency obligations for providers and deployers pursuant to Article 50" carries fines up to EUR 15 000 000 or 3 % of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. For SMEs and start-ups the lower of the two applies (Art. 99(4)(g), 99(6)) — [EUR-Lex AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689).

**Commission Article 50 guidelines (final, 20 Jul 2026, C(2026) 5054 final; non-binding)**
- The final guidelines were published on 20 July 2026 (document dated "Brussels, 20.7.2026, C(2026) 5054 final") and state that the obligations apply "as from 2 August 2026". They are "non-binding"; only the CJEU can give an authoritative interpretation — [Guidelines PDF](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/131215); [Commission news, 20 Jul 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-publishes-guidelines-transparency-obligations-providers-and-deployers-certain-ai-systems); [library page, updated 31 Jul 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/guidelines-transparency-obligations-providers-and-deployers-ai-systems).
- **Provider vs deployer:** a company offering an interactive or generative AI application "under its own name or trademark" to consumers or professional users is a provider for Art. 50(1)/(2), "regardless of whether the AI system is provided for free or for payment". A company that modifies an existing generative system and puts it into service under its own name becomes a provider of the new system (paras 10–11). Deployers use AI "under their authority"; employees of a legal-person deployer are not separate deployers (paras 12–14) — [Guidelines PDF](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/131215).
- **GPAI interplay:** "Article 50 AI Act does not explicitly apply to GPAI models". Model providers are encouraged to implement model-level marking "to facilitate compliance by downstream AI system providers" (para 27). AI literacy requirements (Art. 4) also apply to providers and deployers in scope of Art. 50 (para 25) — [Guidelines PDF](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/131215).
- **50(1) disclosure form:** notification at the latest at first interaction. Disclosures "contained only in terms and conditions, URLs…" are insufficient on their own (para 38). Recommended techniques: plain-language banners such as "You are interacting with an AI system", first-turn greetings, persistent badges near the input/output field. Child-friendly wording is required where children may interact (paras 33–37) — [Guidelines PDF](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/131215).
- **50(1) "obvious" exception:** to be "interpreted restrictively" and limited to cases with "almost no doubt left". Listed examples where it applies include "Interactive AI systems intended only to be used by properly trained health professionals to support medical diagnosis and suggest related treatments" and internal assistants for trained, AI-literate staff. Examples where it does not apply include "AI chatbots embedded in online platforms or assistance support tools (helpdesks)" and an "AI-powered robotic companion pet". It cannot be relied on where the general public, including vulnerable persons, may use the system (paras 44–45) — [Guidelines PDF](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/131215).
- **50(2) scope and exceptions:** text is in scope, and content "mixed with human-created material also qualifies" (para 59). Outside scope: "AI systems used in internal analytical processes that extract and structure data, but do not summarise it" (para 65), short strings, data labels, source code and JSON (para 68). Exempt as standard editing or minor alteration: grammar and spellchecking, "AI-generated translations of text", formatting, "Transcriptions of conversations" (para 92). Marking required for "AI-generated summaries of text; paraphrasing or rewriting text that changes style, structure and meaning" (para 92). A narrow "business to business applications" exemption, "excluding public and consumer-facing AI systems", requires three cumulative conditions. The output must be strictly technical (e.g. "internal documentation processes… before the output is finalised and made available to other external persons"). It must be perceived only by a limited pre-defined number of professionals within the provider's and deployer's organisation. It must not be intended to be shared outside, with safeguards such as cloud isolation and role-based controls (para 87) — [Guidelines PDF](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/131215).
- **50(4) text on matters of public interest:** "published" means accessible to "an indeterminate, fairly large number of unrelated, potential readers". Private professional correspondence and organisation-internal texts are not published. Public-interest topics expressly include "public health" and "consumer safety". An example that "must be labelled… unless subject to human review/editorial control" is "AI-manipulated parts of a lifestyle-website article comparing the effects of various diets on a particular disease in middle-aged women". Product descriptions are out of scope, but not where they make "claims related to e.g. health, consumer safety" (para 131) — [Guidelines PDF](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/131215).
- **50(4) human-review exception (two cumulative conditions):**
  - **Review:** human review means "deliberate examination of the substance of the content by one or more natural persons possessing relevant knowledge and professional judgement… (e.g. academic peer review or professional validation chains). Fact-checking the accuracy of the content is a minimum requirement" (para 134). "Superficial, solely formal or procedural checks…, the mere existence of an editorial policy, automated review processes or cursory editorial approval" do not qualify (para 135).
  - **No AI changes after sign-off:** "Any substantive AI intervention occurring after the human review or editorial control process has taken place will therefore cause the exception to become void" (para 136).
  - **Editorial responsibility:** a legal or natural person must hold "the ultimate legal responsibility over the publication". "The identity and contact details of the legal person, the natural person or the function with editorial responsibility should be made publicly available on an easily findable location", e.g. "a website's terms and conditions or other user-facing legal information" (para 138). Deployers "may rely on relevant applicable professional or deontological standards to demonstrate compliance" (para 133).

  Source: [Guidelines PDF](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/131215).
- The guidelines refer to "joint Commission and EDPB guidelines on the interplay between the AI Act and EU data protection law (under preparation)" (footnote 37, July 2026) — [Guidelines PDF](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/131215).

**Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI-generated content**
- The Commission published the final Code on 10 June 2026 (page updated 12 June 2026). It is "voluntary and sets out practical steps", with a signing process and "EU Icons for labelling AI-generated content" — [Commission news](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-publishes-code-practice-marking-and-labelling-ai-generated-content).
- According to Faegre Drinker (30 Jul 2026), the Commission and AI Board confirmed the Code as adequate in July 2026. Labels must be visible at first exposure, and the Commission's standard icon satisfies this. The editorial exception is described as narrow. Signatories must have watermark-detection interoperability in place by 2 Feb 2027 — [Faegre Drinker](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/7/eu-ai-act-commission-confirms-transparency-code-of-practice-as-adequate-and-publishes-final-version-of-its-guidelines-on-transparency-obligations). A search snippet mentioned ~190 signatories, but I did not verify it on an opened page.

**High-risk (Annex III / Annex I) and GPAI**
- Annex III areas: biometrics; critical infrastructure; education; employment and workers' management; access to essential services (public benefits, creditworthiness, "risk assessment and pricing in relation to natural persons in the case of life and health insurance", emergency calls and "emergency healthcare patient triage systems"); law enforcement; migration; justice and democracy. No veterinary or animal-health area is listed — [EUR-Lex AI Act, Annex III](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689).
- The MDR defines a medical device as something intended "for human beings" for medical purposes — [EUR-Lex MDR 2017/745 consolidated](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:02017R0745-20250110).
- A "downstream provider" is a provider of an AI system "which integrates an AI model, regardless of whether the AI model is provided by themselves… or provided by another entity based on contractual relations" (Art. 3) — [EUR-Lex AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689).
- GPAI provider obligations "entered into application" on 2 Aug 2025. The Commission's enforcement powers, including fines, start on 2 Aug 2026. Models placed on the market before 2 Aug 2025 must comply by 2 Aug 2027. "only those making significant modifications to AI models need to comply with the obligations for providers of GPAI models" (page updated 28 Apr 2026) — [Commission GPAI guidelines page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/guidelines-gpai-providers).

### Inferences
- **Vet documentation assistant (text input, EU hosting, vet approves every document):** offered under the site's own name and built on a third-party model via API, it makes the operator an AI-system *provider* and downstream provider, not a GPAI-model provider, unless it significantly modifies a model. Clinics are deployers. Obligations that apply now:
  - Art. 4 (support AI literacy: user training material, documented guidance).
  - Art. 50(1) disclosure if any part is conversational. The "trained health professionals" obviousness example may cover a vet-only tool, but the guidelines read it restrictively and a disclosure costs little.
  - Possibly Art. 50(2) marking of generated text from 2 Aug 2026, or 2 Dec 2026 if the system was on the market before 2 Aug 2026.

  It is ambiguous whether structured records drafted from a vet's own text are "extract and structure… but do not summarise" (out of scope), a "summary" (marking required), or covered by the B2B/internal-documentation exemption. That exemption likely fails for client-facing outputs such as discharge notes sent to owners. Lawyer to confirm. A low-cost hedge is embedding provenance metadata in exports.
- **Owner articles:** pet health content touches "public health" and "consumer safety" topics, so treat AI-assisted article text as in scope of Art. 50(4) (my inference; the guidelines do not mention animals). Either label AI involvement visibly, or meet the exception:
  - a named vet reviewer who fact-checks the substance;
  - a re-review after any AI edit;
  - an editorially responsible person or entity with contact details published, e.g. in the imprint or an "editorial policy" page.

  Keeping a review log (reviewer, date, version hash) helps to evidence "genuine" review.
- **Local-first booklet:** if it contains no AI interaction or generation, Art. 50 is not triggered. Any owner-facing chatbot or summary feature would trigger Art. 50(1) disclosure in plain, child-appropriate language at first interaction, not only in the T&Cs.
- **High-risk:** none of the three products appears to be high-risk. Animal health is not an Annex III area, and the MDR (the main Annex I route for health software) covers only human-use devices. Edge cases to keep out of scope: staff performance monitoring (Annex III 4(b)) and pricing of human life/health insurance (5(c)). Lawyer to confirm.

### Gaps
- I did not open the GPAI guidelines PDF, so the quantitative "significant modification" threshold (reported elsewhere as one-third of original training compute) is unverified.
- The exact date of the Code of Practice adequacy decision and the signatory count were not verified on an opened Commission page.
- Whether a vet-record generator falls under Art. 50(2) marking is not resolved by the guidelines (see Inferences).

## 2. GDPR as it applies (owner data in pet records, vet notes with names/addresses, Art. 28 processors, transfers to US model providers and DPF status, DPIA triggers, EDPB Opinion 28/2024 and 2025–2026 guidance)

### Takeaway
Pet records and vet notes are personal data of the owner (name, address, contact, and anything linkable to them), so GDPR applies fully. The site acts as an Art. 28 processor for clinics using the documentation tool, with sub-processors such as the model API and hosting, and as a controller for its own owner accounts. As of Oct 2026 the EU–US Data Privacy Framework remains in force: the General Court upheld it on 3 Sep 2025, but the appeal C-703/25 P is pending and the US Supreme Court's 29 Jun 2026 *Trump v. Slaughter* ruling has renewed doubts. EU-region processing plus SCCs and a transfer impact assessment as fallback is therefore the defensible posture. The EDPB's 2026 AI-related guidelines (anonymisation, web scraping) are still in consultation until 30 Oct 2026.

### Cited Findings
- **Definitions:** "'personal data' means any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person… directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier such as a name… location data" (Art. 4(1)). Art. 9(1) special categories include "data concerning health… of a natural person"; the provision is framed around natural persons — [EUR-Lex GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679).
- **Art. 28(3) mandatory processor-contract terms:** processing "only on documented instructions… including with regard to transfers"; confidentiality of authorised persons; Art. 32 security; conditions for engaging sub-processors; assistance with data-subject rights and Arts. 32–36; deletion or return at end of service; audit and information rights — [EUR-Lex GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679).
- **Art. 35(1) DPIA:** required where processing, "in particular using new technologies", is "likely to result in a high risk". Art. 35(3) lists systematic and extensive automated evaluation with legal or similarly significant effects, large-scale special-category processing, and large-scale systematic monitoring of public areas. Art. 35(4) requires supervisory authorities to publish lists — [EUR-Lex GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679).
- **EDPB Opinion 28/2024 (adopted 18 Dec 2024)** — [EDPB page](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/opinion-board-art-64/opinion-282024-certain-data-protection-aspects_en); [Opinion PDF](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2024-12/edpb_opinion_202428_ai-models_en.pdf):
  - AI models trained with personal data "cannot, in all cases, be considered anonymous". Anonymity requires an insignificant likelihood both of extraction and of obtaining personal data through queries.
  - Legitimate interest requires the three-step test (legitimate interest, necessity, balancing), with data subjects' reasonable expectations relevant.
  - Scenario 2: supervisory authorities should consider whether a controller *deploying* a model developed by another "conducted an appropriate assessment… to ascertain that the AI model was not developed by unlawfully processing personal data". That assessment should be "less or more detailed depending on the risks raised by the processing in the deployment phase".
- **EDPB plenary of 8 July 2026:** guidelines on anonymisation and on web scraping for generative AI were adopted for public consultation until 30 October 2026; final blockchain guidelines were also adopted. The scraping guidance states "processing special categories of personal data is in principle prohibited" absent an Art. 9(2) exception. The news page announces no joint AI Act–GDPR guidelines — [EDPB news, 8 Jul 2026](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/edpb-sheds-light-on-anonymisation-and-web-scraping-for-generative-ai-and-adopts-final-version_en). Secondary sources number these Guidelines 02/2026 and 03/2026; I did not verify the numbering on the EDPB page.
- **DPF litigation:** the General Court (Tenth Chamber, Extended Composition) upheld the EU–US Data Privacy Framework in *Latombe v Commission*, T-553/23, on 3 Sep 2025. The appeal C-703/25 P was filed on 31 Oct 2025 on four grounds of law, and the judgment assessed adequacy only as of the 2023 adoption — [EDPL 2026/1, Corrales Compagnucci](https://edpl.lexxion.eu/article/EDPL/2026/1/15).
- **DPF status, 2 Jul 2026** — [Gawel Legal, 2 Jul 2026](https://gawel.ch/insights/64-transatlantic-data-transfers-dpf/):
  - The framework "has not lapsed" and no court has struck it down.
  - *Trump v. Slaughter* (29 Jun 2026, 6-3) allows at-will removal of FTC members, the agency that enforces DPF commitments.
  - The PCLOB has been below quorum since removals on 27 Jan 2025.
  - noyb wrote to the Commission on 30 Jun 2026 asking for an orderly repeal.
  - The Swiss–US DPF has been adequate for certified organisations since 15 Sep 2024.
  - The article suggests reinstating SCCs as a fallback and updating TIAs.
- Search snippets (not verified on an opened page; the DataGuidance page did not load) state that a 4 Jun 2026 order admitted Microsoft as intervener in C-703/25 P, and that no hearing date was known as of Jul–Aug 2026.
- The Commission's EU–US transfers page still presents the 10 July (2023) adequacy decision (Decision 2023/1795) without mentioning the litigation (accessed 2026-10-18) — [Commission](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/international-dimension-data-protection/eu-us-data-transfers_en).
- **Market practice on model providers:** Provet Cloud's published sub-processor list names Anthropic (Claude API/Enterprise) with location "USA / EU". It names OpenAI Ireland Limited as "EU if EU residency is enabled for the project; otherwise United States (SCCs apply)", and Speechmatics as "Primarily UK" (no date shown; accessed 2026-10-18) — [Provet third-party processors](https://www.provet.com/legal/third-party-data-processors).
- **Italian Garante, 30 Jul 2025 press release** on uploading medical reports to generative AI chatbots — [Garante docweb 10154670, "Roma, 30 luglio 2025"](https://www.garanteprivacy.it/home/docweb/-/docweb-display/docweb/10154670):
  - Warns of "perdita di controllo su dati sanitari" and urges users to check whether providers retain uploads for training.
  - Notes tools not released as medical devices after the necessary testing may err, and that answers "dovrebbero sempre essere verificate con un professionista medico".
  - Calls for "un'intermediazione umana qualificata nell'elaborazione dei dati sanitari tramite sistemi di IA".
  - Recalls the October 2023 decalogue for AI health services, which requires a legal basis, a prior impact assessment, transparency and security.

### Inferences
- **Roles and legal bases:** owner identity and contact data in a pet record are personal data. The pet's clinical data becomes personal data once linked to an identifiable owner (Art. 4(1) "indirectly"). It is generally not Art. 9 "health data" because it concerns an animal, but it can reveal owner facts: an assistance-dog record may imply a disability, and costs reveal financial data. Treat it as sensitive in design (my inference; no DPA statement on pet data was found). For the documentation tool the clinic is controller and the site is processor, needing an Art. 28 DPA, a sub-processor list and change notification. For owner accounts the site is controller.
- **DPIA:** none of the Art. 35(3) cases clearly applies, but "new technologies" (LLMs) plus free-text clinical notes about identifiable owners make a DPIA prudent and cheap. Several national authority lists (not opened) reportedly include innovative-technology criteria. Lawyer to confirm.
- **Local-first booklet:** storing data only on the owner's device reduces controller exposure (minimal server processing). Any sync or backup feature reintroduces controller/processor duties and transfer questions.
- **Transfers:** with a US-headquartered model provider, the strongest posture as of Oct 2026 is EU-region processing where offered (e.g. OpenAI's EU data residency per Provet's list; Anthropic listed as "USA / EU"), plus the DPF (if the provider is certified), plus SCCs and a TIA as fallback. Also document the Opinion 28/2024 "appropriate assessment" of the model provider: training-data sources, any infringement findings, and API no-training terms.

### Gaps
- The status of the separate "Digital Omnibus" proposal amending the GDPR (Nov 2025) as of Oct 2026 could not be verified; the Commission page returned HTTP 500.
- National DPIA "must-lists" (Garante list, German DSK Muss-Liste) were not opened.
- No supervisory-authority statement specifically on whether animal/veterinary data is personal data was found.

## 3. Italy: Law 132/2025 (AI law), duties for intellectual professions (Art. 13), health/veterinary rules, FNOVI and Garante positions, veterinary teleconsultation

### Takeaway
Italy now has both its national AI law (Law 132/2025, in force 10 Oct 2025) and an AI Act adaptation decree (D.Lgs. 7 Oct 2026 n. 179, GU 8 Oct 2026, entering into force 23 Oct 2026, after today's date). Art. 13 of Law 132/2025 confines AI in intellectual professions to "instrumental and support" activities with human intellectual work prevailing, and requires informing the client in clear, simple, exhaustive language. This most likely reaches vets, but the law does not name them. D.Lgs. 179/2026 makes ACN the general AI market-surveillance authority and requires professional orders to add AI training, including "obblighi informativi verso il cliente". FNOVI insists responsibility "non può essere delegata" to AI, and its 2019 deontological code forbids replacing the clinical visit with an exclusively virtual relationship.

### Cited Findings
- Law 23 Sep 2025 n. 132, "Disposizioni e deleghe al Governo in materia di intelligenza artificiale", was published in GU Serie Generale n. 223 of 25 Sep 2025 with "Entrata in vigore del provvedimento: 10/10/2025" — [Gazzetta Ufficiale](https://www.gazzettaufficiale.it/eli/id/2025/09/25/25G00143/sg). The article text itself did not load (connection resets); flagged.
- **Art. 13 (as described by secondary sources, official text not opened):**
  - AI use in intellectual professions is allowed only for "attività strumentali e di supporto", with "prevalenza del lavoro intellettuale".
  - The professional must inform the client about AI systems used "con linguaggio chiaro, semplice ed esaustivo", to preserve the "rapporto fiduciario".

  Sources: [ecnews](https://www.ecnews.it/intelligenza-artificiale-e-professioni-intellettuali-lobbligo-di-trasparenza-verso-il-cliente/); [diritto.it](https://www.diritto.it/legge-italiana-intelligenza-artificiale-professione/).
- **Assolombarda (undated, 2026) on Art. 13** — [Assolombarda](https://www.assolombarda.it/servizi/diritto-di-impresa/informazioni/intelligenza-artificiale-art-50-ai-act-e-art-13-legge-132-2025-a-confronto):

## 4. Germany / Austria / Switzerland: professional rules (documentation, confidentiality), telemedicine, recording conversations, national AI-Act implementation, chamber statements on AI, Swiss FADP/FDPIC

### Takeaway
- **Germany:** vets are expressly bound by criminal professional secrecy (§ 203 StGB), which allows disclosure to IT service providers only where necessary and with a secrecy undertaking. Recording consultations without consent is a crime (§ 201 StGB). The new TÄHAV (in force 1 Jan 2025) allows electronic records only if they stay readable and "unveränderlich". Germany's AI Act implementation law KI-MIG has been in force since 29 Jul 2026, with the Bundesnetzagentur as market-surveillance authority. The BTK's AI leaflet demands vet final control, owner information about AI use, labelling of AI content in vet statements, and training.
- **Austria:** the Tierärztegesetz requires personal and direct practice, imposes secrecy, and lists "Fernbehandlung" as a forbidden treatment form that may not be advertised.
- **Switzerland:** the FDPIC holds the FADP directly applicable to AI, including telling users when they are talking to a machine.

## 5. Advertising and health-information rules for owner articles (EU Reg. 2019/6 Arts. 119–121, German HWG/TAMG, Austrian TÄG, Italy) — what an information site must avoid

### Takeaway
Under Reg. 2019/6, advertising prescription-only veterinary medicines is allowed only to vets and persons permitted to supply them. "Advertising" is any representation made to promote supply, sale, prescription or use, including sponsorship. Germany applies Arts. 119–121 directly via TAMG § 33, and HWG still covers advertising of veterinary treatments and procedures. An owner-facing site should therefore keep content non-promotional: no brand-name recommendations of POM-V products, no dosing instructions, no sponsor or affiliate tie-ins next to prescription-drug content, and a "talk to your vet" framing.

### Cited Findings
- **Definition:** "'advertising of veterinary medicinal products' means the making of a representation in any form in connection with veterinary medicinal products in order to promote the supply, distribution, sale, prescription or use of veterinary medicinal products and comprising also the supply of samples and sponsorships" (Art. 4) — [EUR-Lex Reg. 2019/6](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32019R0006).
- **Art. 119** (all VMP advertising): only authorised or registered products; must "make it clear that it aims at promoting…"; must comply with the SPC; no misleading information or anything that could "lead to incorrect use"; must "encourage the responsible use… without exaggerating"; no antimicrobial samples — [EUR-Lex Reg. 2019/6](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32019R0006).
- **Art. 120(1):** advertising of POM-V products "shall be allowed only when made exclusively to… veterinarians; persons permitted to supply veterinary medicinal products". A Member State may allow advertising of immunologicals to professional keepers, with an express invitation to consult the vet (120(2)). Art. 121 bans gifts and inducements to prescribers beyond inexpensive items. Art. 122 lets Member States lay down implementing procedures — [EUR-Lex Reg. 2019/6](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32019R0006).
- **Germany:**
  - TAMG § 33 "Werbung": for veterinary medicinal products and veterinary-technical products, "sind die Artikel 119 bis 121 der Verordnung (EU) 2019/6… anzuwenden" — [TAMG § 33](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/tamg/__33.html).
  - HWG § 1(1): HWG applies to advertising for medicinal products "im Sinne des § 2 des Arzneimittelgesetzes", medical devices, and "Verfahren und Behandlungen, soweit sich die Werbeaussage auf die Erkennung, Beseitigung oder Linderung von Krankheiten… beim Tier bezieht" (Nr. 3). § 1(5) excludes correspondence and documents not serving advertising purposes that answer a specific inquiry — [HWG § 1](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/heilmwerbg/__1.html).
  - HWG § 10(1): prescription-only medicines may be advertised only to "Ärzten, Zahnärzten, Tierärzten, Apothekern" and lawful traders — [HWG § 10](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/heilmwerbg/__10.html).
- **Austria:** TÄG § 31 restricts vets' own advertising, banning "reklamehaftes" self-promotion, comparative references to colleagues, and announcing forbidden treatment forms such as Fernbehandlung — [RIS TÄG](https://www.ris.bka.gv.at/GeltendeFassung.wxe?Abfrage=Bundesnormen&Gesetzesnummer=20011642).
- **Article 50 overlap:** the Commission guidelines treat AI-manipulated product text containing "claims related to e.g. health" as potentially public-interest text that must be labelled unless human-reviewed (para 131) — [Guidelines PDF](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/131215).

### Inferences
- **Classification:** whether an information article is "advertising" turns on promotional purpose. Educational content naming an active substance or drug class generically is lower-risk. Naming a prescription-only brand, giving doses, ranking products, or placing affiliate links or sponsor logos next to such content can make it look like public advertising of POM-V products, which Art. 120 prohibits. For Germany, HWG § 10 is then a further hurdle; whether HWG § 10 still applies to Tierarzneimittel after TAMG/Reg. 2019/6 is unclear from the texts read, since TAMG § 33 points to the EU rules. Lawyer to confirm.
- **Safe editorial pattern:**
  - describe conditions, signs and when to see a vet;
  - mention treatment classes only generically ("your vet may prescribe an anti-inflammatory");
  - no doses and no POM-V brand names;
  - a visible "not a substitute for an examination" note;
  - a disclosed sponsorship/affiliate policy, separated from medicine content.
- Petleo's own website privacy policy lists Amazon PartnerNet and Awin affiliate tracking, and its vet platform includes a Purina PRO PLAN recommendation tool (section 6). This shows commercial tie-ins are common in this market, which is a reason to publish a clear conflict-of-interest policy.

### Gaps
- Italian national rules on veterinary medicine advertising (D.Lgs. 218/2023 implementing Reg. 2019/6, any ministerial authorisation regime) were not opened.
- No German or Italian authority guidance on online pet-health information sites was found.

### Inferences
- **Table stakes for an AI documentation tool selling into EU clinics, Oct 2026:**
  1. A security page with named controls (TLS 1.2+/AES-256, access control/SSO, logging, pen testing).
  2. An explicit "no training on your data" statement covering both the vendor and its model providers, worded precisely. VetRec's "anonymised, aggregated" carve-out and Vetster's "de-identify… training" show that buyers need to read the fine print.
  3. A public sub-processor list with locations, especially LLM and speech providers (Provet's is the benchmark).
  4. A DPA/AVV available up front.
  5. Stated retention, with zero audio retention where audio is used.
  6. A "draft, vet decides" human-review statement.
  7. Recording-consent templates where audio is captured (Curala, Scribenote).
- **Differentiators (few or no vendors have them):**
  - A third-party attestation (SOC 2 Type II or ISO 27001; ISO 42001 now emerging via Scribenote).
  - EU data residency stated per component, including the LLM call. US vendors mostly do not state it, and CoVet's sub-processors are US/CA.
  - A published evaluation or accuracy report with methodology. None was found; HappyDoc's "99.8%" has no stated method.
  - An EU AI Act page: provider role, Art. 50 approach, AI-literacy materials, editorial-responsibility statement. CoVet only lists the question.
  - DACH/IT-specific items: a § 203 StGB secrecy undertaking, Italian Art. 13 client-notice templates, and a TÄHAV-compatible immutable audit trail.
- A text-input, EU-hosted, vet-approves-everything design can credibly claim several of these at low cost: no audio, so no § 201 StGB or consent burden; an EU residency map; immutable sign-off log; Art. 50 statement; a published evaluation of extraction accuracy on synthetic cases. Formal certifications (SOC 2 or ISO 27001) take time and money and are the main gap versus US leaders.

### Gaps
- Scribenote's trust centre, HappyDoc's privacy policy, and VetRec's full security-page text (only bundle strings extracted) could not be fully read.
- Digitail and Provet hosting regions for AI features are not stated on the pages read.
- Model providers for Curala, Petleo, VetRec, Scribenote, HappyDoc and Digitail are not named publicly.
- I found no vendor publishing an accuracy or evaluation methodology, an Art. 50 transparency statement, or an AI-literacy programme.
- FirstVet's and Vetster's use of AI in owner-facing services was not established from the pages read.
