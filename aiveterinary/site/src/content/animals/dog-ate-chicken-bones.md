## First: go to a vet now if you see these signs

Has your dog stolen the roast chicken carcass or found a bone on a walk? Stay calm, take away what's left and **phone your vet**, even if your dog seems perfectly well. Go **straight away** to your clinic, or an [emergency vet](/emergency) out of hours, if your dog:

- is **coughing, gagging**, retching without bringing anything up, or seems to be choking
- is **drooling** heavily, swallowing over and over, or struggling to swallow
- **brings food or water back up** soon after eating or drinking
- is **vomiting** repeatedly, or can't stop
- has a **swollen**, hard or painful **tummy**, is hunched, or doesn't want to be touched
- is **dull**, weak, won't eat, or **collapses**
- is straining or struggling to **poo**, or has **blood** in their poo

These are signs that a bone may be stuck in the gullet (oesophagus), stomach or intestines, or has damaged them.

## Should I make my dog sick after eating chicken bones?

No: cooked bones are sharp and can injure the gullet again on the way back up, so leave that decision to your vet.

It's the first thing many people think of, but **don't try to make your dog vomit** with salt water, hydrogen peroxide or other remedies from the internet.

A University of Illinois veterinary page on the medicines vets use to make dogs sick lists **sharp objects**, along with corrosive substances and petroleum products, among the cases where vomiting should **not** be induced, because it can injure organs or make things worse.

Whether to make your dog sick, and how, is your vet's call. Avoid other home "tricks" to help the bone pass unless your vet has agreed to them; they can cost you valuable time.

## Why are cooked chicken bones dangerous?

Splinters can damage the digestive tract, and bigger pieces can lodge in the gullet, block the stomach or bowel, or pierce the gut wall.

The PDSA, a UK veterinary charity, warns of digestive tract damage caused by splinters, "particularly with cooked chicken bones", and says larger pieces can cause **blockages in the throat or bowel, which is life-threatening**. It describes a seven-month-old puppy who swallowed a chicken bone whole and needed emergency surgery to remove it from her stomach.

There are three main risks.

**1. A bone stuck in the gullet.** The MSD Veterinary Manual says bones are the **most common foreign body in the oesophagus** in dogs. Typical signs are **drooling, gagging, difficulty swallowing, bringing food back up and repeated attempts to swallow**. Once it's diagnosed, the manual says, the object should be **removed right away**, because **perforation** of the gullet is a serious complication.

**2. A blocked stomach or intestine.** The Cornell Riney Canine Health Center lists **vomiting, loss of appetite, tummy pain, diarrhoea, dehydration and lethargy** as common signs, and calls these blockages **emergencies** that often need surgery. The MSD Veterinary Manual adds that the belly may swell and that a dog may be unable to stop vomiting.

**3. Perforation.** A splinter can make a hole in the gut wall. Cornell explains that if gut contents leak into the abdomen they can cause **peritonitis** (inflammation of the abdominal lining) and **sepsis**, a potentially life-threatening infection.

The MSD Veterinary Manual also notes that hard, poorly digestible material such as **hair or bones** can cause **constipation**.

## What should I do right now?

Take the leftovers away, work out what and how much your dog ate, and phone your vet, without making your dog sick.

1. **Take away the leftover bones** calmly, without a tug-of-war that might make your dog gulp them down.
2. **Work out what your dog ate**: raw or cooked bones, from what animal, roughly how many, and when.
3. **Phone your vet** and follow their advice. They'll tell you whether to come in now or watch your dog at home.
4. **Don't make your dog sick**, and don't give anything to "help the bone slide down" unless your vet says so.
5. If your vet says to watch at home, **check your dog carefully** over the next few days: appetite, any vomiting, their tummy, and whether they're passing normal poo.

## Can I give my dog a painkiller for tummy ache?

No: don't give painkillers, anti-inflammatories or other medicines meant for people, and let your vet choose any treatment after examining your dog.

Tummy pain after eating a bone is a reason to phone your vet, not to treat at home. See our guide to [human painkillers and pets](/animals/nsaid-painkillers-dogs-cats) for why medicine cabinets are risky.

## What will the vet do?

Examine your dog, usually with x-rays, ultrasound and blood tests, then remove the bone with an endoscope, operate, or admit your dog for fluids and monitoring.

Your vet will start with what you tell them and a careful examination, gently feeling the tummy. According to Cornell, the most common tests are **x-rays** and **ultrasound** of the abdomen, with **blood tests** to check for dehydration, salt imbalances and infection.

What happens next depends on where the bone is and how your dog is:

- in the **gullet**, the MSD Veterinary Manual says most objects can be removed through the mouth with an **endoscope** (a flexible tube with a camera); surgery is needed if that isn't possible or the gullet is perforated
- in the **stomach**, Cornell says the vet may try to retrieve it with an endoscope first
- for a **blocked intestine**, Cornell says most cases need **surgery**; small pieces that don't cause a complete blockage may be managed with hospital care, fluids and monitoring

The MSD Veterinary Manual says dogs with a foreign-object blockage **tend to do well if diagnosed and treated quickly**. That's why it's worth phoning straight away rather than waiting. Our [visit brief](/visit-brief) helps you note what was eaten and when.

## How can I stop it happening again?

Don't give cooked bones, put leftovers in a bin your dog can't open, tell guests and children not to share scraps, and offer dog-safe chews.

- **Don't give cooked bones**, even as a treat. Cornell advises avoiding items that are easily swallowed, such as small bones.
- Put chicken carcasses, rib bones and leftovers in a **lidded bin** your dog can't get into, and don't leave plates unattended.
- Ask guests and children **not to feed scraps** from the table.
- For chewing, the PDSA recommends **dog-friendly chew toys**.
- The MSD Veterinary Manual notes that many dogs who have eaten things they shouldn't **keep doing it**. If yours is one of them, talk to your vet.

Remember: a dog who seems fine after eating a bone isn't necessarily out of danger. If in doubt, **call your vet**.
