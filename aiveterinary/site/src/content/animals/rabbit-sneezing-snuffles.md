## First: go to a vet now if your rabbit is struggling to breathe

Rabbits breathe through their nose. The Rabbit Welfare Association & Fund (RWAF) says a rabbit that is **breathing through its mouth** "must see a vet at once", and the PDSA says to contact your vet immediately if your rabbit is struggling to breathe.

Call a vet who treats rabbits, or an emergency clinic, and go straight in if your rabbit:

- is **breathing through its mouth**, or breathing very fast, heavily or with effort
- is wheezing or has very noisy breathing
- has **stopped eating** or is passing fewer or no droppings
- is very quiet, hunched, hiding or floppy

If your rabbit is sneezing, has a runny nose or weepy eyes but is otherwise bright and eating, book a vet appointment **today or as soon as you can**. The PDSA warns that without treatment a rabbit with snuffles is likely to become very poorly and could even die, and that rabbits often hide illness until they are very unwell.

## What is snuffles in rabbits?

Snuffles is the everyday name for a bacterial infection of a rabbit's nose, throat or chest. It is not a harmless cold.

The PDSA and the RWAF both name *Pasteurella* as the most common cause, with *Bordetella* as another. The RWAF adds *Pseudomonas*. According to the Merck Veterinary Manual, *Pasteurella* is "highly contagious", most adult rabbits are thought to be infected, and 30 to 90% of healthy-looking rabbits in colonies may carry it without signs. The PDSA explains that the bacteria usually cause trouble during stress, illness or poor housing, such as a hutch that is too small, poorly ventilated or not cleaned often enough. Merck notes that snuffles appears "when the resistance of the rabbit is low" and can progress to pneumonia.

## What are the signs of snuffles?

Sneezing with a runny nose, weepy eyes and crusty or stained fur on the inside of the front legs.

The PDSA lists:

- sneezing
- discharge from the nose, and weepy eyes
- fast, difficult, wheezy or noisy breathing
- low energy, being quiet or hiding
- eating less and losing weight
- stained fur on the front legs, from wiping the eyes and nose

Merck describes the discharge as thin and clear at first, later becoming thick (pus-like), and adds that affected rabbits often sneeze and cough. The RWAF notes that a blocked nose makes breathing and swallowing hard, which is one reason appetite drops.

## Can snuffles cause other problems?

Yes. The same bacteria can spread to the eyes, ears and other parts of the head.

The PDSA says *Pasteurella* can also cause eye infections, swollen or blocked tear ducts, head tilt, and abscesses in the head. Merck describes the same forms, including middle and inner ear infection that makes the rabbit twist its head. If your rabbit's head has started to tilt, see our article on [head tilt in rabbits](/animals/rabbit-head-tilt). The RWAF notes that some rabbits with snuffles also have dental problems; see [overgrown teeth in rabbits](/animals/rabbit-overgrown-teeth).

## Is it contagious to my other rabbits or pets?

Yes, between rabbits, and *Bordetella* is a particular worry for guinea pigs.

The PDSA says the infection spreads through direct contact, shared items such as food bowls, and the air. The RWAF advises keeping a bonded pair together, because separation is stressful, while giving the sick rabbit its own food and water bowls, removing any discharge and keeping everything "scrupulously clean". It also advises washing your hands after handling the sick rabbit. Ask your vet whether your other rabbits should be checked.

Merck warns that rabbits and guinea pigs should not be housed together, because guinea pigs are very susceptible to a severe pneumonia caused by *Bordetella*. If you have a guinea pig that is sneezing, see [guinea pig sneezing](/animals/guinea-pig-sneezing).

## What will the vet do?

Examine your rabbit, decide whether tests are needed, and treat the infection and anything that came with it.

Merck says snuffles should not be diagnosed from the signs alone; vets may use swabs for culture, blood tests and, in severe cases, imaging. The PDSA says severely ill rabbits may need to stay in hospital, sometimes starting with oxygen, anti-inflammatories and antibiotics, followed by fluids and support for breathing and gut movement. Blocked tear ducts may need flushing, and abscesses draining or removing. Your vet may suggest a nebuliser.

Only use medicines your vet has prescribed for your rabbit. The RWAF notes that some medicines used in rabbits are not licensed for them, so your vet may ask you to sign a consent form.

## Will my rabbit get better?

Many do, but snuffles can be stubborn and can come back.

The PDSA says some rabbits recover in a few days, others take weeks, some relapse later in life and some do not survive despite treatment. Merck says treatment "will most likely not eradicate the organism", and that the next period of stress may cause a relapse. Give all the medicine your vet prescribes, and tell your vet if you are struggling to medicate your rabbit.

## How can I lower the risk of snuffles?

Keep stress low and housing clean, roomy and well ventilated.

- **Space and air.** The PDSA recommends a spacious, clean, well-ventilated home. Merck stresses good ventilation and low ammonia from urine.
- **Clean litter.** Remove wet, dirty litter often, because ammonia irritates the airways.
- **Company.** The PDSA advises keeping rabbits in pairs or groups and keeping them stress-free.
- **Diet.** Hay, leafy greens and, if needed, a small amount of pellets, with fresh water in both a bowl and a bottle (PDSA).
- **Separate species.** Don't house rabbits and guinea pigs together (Merck).

## When should I take my rabbit to the vet for sneezing?

Straight away if breathing is difficult or your rabbit stops eating; otherwise book an appointment for any sneezing with nasal discharge, weepy eyes or dirty front paws.

The RSPCA lists wheezing or noisy breathing, a snotty nose and runny eyes as signs of snuffles and says to speak to your vet immediately. If your rabbit has stopped eating, read [my rabbit is not eating](/animals/rabbit-not-eating) now, because that is an emergency in its own right. If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know about my rabbit's sneezing?

How long it has been going on, what the discharge looks like, whether your rabbit is eating and pooing, and how it is housed.

- when the sneezing started, and whether it is getting worse
- whether there is discharge from the nose or eyes, and its colour (clear, white, yellow)
- whether you have noticed noisy or fast breathing, or breathing through the mouth
- whether your rabbit is eating, drinking and passing normal droppings
- any head tilt, loss of balance or swelling on the face or jaw
- your rabbit's age, and any previous snuffles, dental or eye problems
- housing (indoors or outdoors, size, ventilation, type of litter, how often it is cleaned)
- other rabbits or guinea pigs in the home, and whether any are ill
- any recent stress, such as a move, a new companion or a loss
- any medicines your rabbit takes
