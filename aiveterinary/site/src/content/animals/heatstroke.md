## First, the signs that mean go now

A dog with heatstroke needs cooling *and* a vet. Start cooling immediately, and get to a clinic as soon as you can, if your dog has been in heat or exercising and:

- is panting heavily, with a wide, flat tongue, and cannot settle
- is drooling thick, sticky saliva
- has bright red, dark or purple gums, or very pale gums
- is wobbly, confused, or struggling to stand
- vomits or has diarrhoea, especially with blood
- is trembling, has a seizure, or collapses
- stops panting and goes quiet while still very hot

Flat-faced breeds (pugs, French bulldogs, bulldogs), overweight dogs, thick-coated dogs, older dogs and dogs with heart or breathing problems are at highest risk and can overheat in conditions other dogs tolerate.

## How do I cool down a dog with heatstroke?

Stop all activity, move the dog into shade, pour cold water over its body, get air moving, and call the vet while you keep cooling.

Vets and researchers now advise "cool first, transport second": start cooling straight away, before you set off for the vet, because delayed cooling leads to poorer outcomes. If someone can come with you, they can keep cooling the dog on the way.

1. **Stop all activity** and move the dog into shade or an air-conditioned room.
2. **Gently pour cold water over the dog's whole body.** Water from the cold tap is fine; a hose, buckets or bottles all work. Keep the water away from the nose and mouth, and keep going. Do not cover the dog with wet towels and leave them; they trap heat once they warm up.
3. **Get air moving**: a fan, an open car with air conditioning, or a breeze.
4. **Offer small amounts of water to drink** if the dog is alert and able to swallow. Do not force water into the mouth of a dog that is collapsed or confused.
5. **Call the vet while cooling** and tell them you are coming with a heatstroke case.
6. **Travel with the air conditioning on or the windows open**, on a wet towel the dog is lying on, not under.

The old worry that cold water makes overheating worse is not supported by the evidence; delayed cooling is the bigger danger. If your dog is calm and used to water, you can put its body in water, but keep its head out and be extremely careful that it does not breathe any water in. This risk is greatest in flat-faced dogs and dogs that are not fully conscious, so stay with the dog and support its head.

## What should I not do if my dog has heatstroke?

Do not wait to see whether the dog improves, do not skip the vet because it seems better, and do not give any medication.

- Do not wait to see whether the dog improves on its own.
- Do not skip the vet because the dog "seems better" after cooling. Heatstroke can damage the kidneys, gut, blood clotting and brain, and some of that shows hours later.
- Do not give any medication, including human painkillers.
- Do not leave a dog in a parked car, even for minutes, even with the windows open.
- Do not walk a dog on hot pavements in the heat of the day. If you cannot comfortably hold the palm of your hand on the ground for 7 seconds, it is too hot for paws.

## Does my dog need a vet after heatstroke?

Yes: every dog you have had to actively cool should be seen by a vet the same day, even if it looks normal afterwards.

Every dog you have had to actively cool should be seen by a vet the same day, even if it looks normal afterwards. Whether blood tests, fluids or monitoring are needed is a vet decision, and it cannot be made from the outside.

Also see a vet today if your dog, after being hot:

- is quiet, off food, or drinking much more than usual
- has diarrhoea or dark urine
- has small bruises or bleeding gums

## What will the vet want to know about my dog's heatstroke?

What your dog was doing, where and for how long, the conditions, which signs you saw and when, what cooling you did, and its breed, age, health and medication.

- What the dog was doing, where, and for how long
- The temperature and conditions
- When you noticed signs, and which ones
- What cooling you did and for how long
- Whether the dog has vomited, had diarrhoea or collapsed
- Breed, weight, age and any existing conditions
- Any medication

## Can cats and rabbits get heatstroke?

Yes: cats can overheat when trapped in hot enclosed spaces, and rabbits are very sensitive to heat, so both need cooling and a vet.

**Cats** rarely get heatstroke from exercise but can from being trapped in a greenhouse, shed, conservatory or car. Signs include panting and fast breathing, vomiting, diarrhoea, weakness and confusion, then collapse or seizures. Move the cat somewhere cool, slowly pour cold tap water over its body (away from the nose and mouth), use a fan, and call your vet as soon as possible.

**Rabbits** are very sensitive to heat, and heatstroke can be fatal quickly, so keep hutches and runs out of the sun. Signs include panting or rapid breathing, weakness or confusion, wetness around the nose and mouth, and lying stretched out and unresponsive. Open-mouthed breathing is an emergency. Move the rabbit to a cool, shaded place, dampen its ears and feet with cool (not cold) water, and call a vet who treats rabbits immediately. Do not cool a rabbit too quickly.

## How can I prevent heatstroke in my dog?

Walk early or late in warm weather, carry water and rest in shade, take extra care with flat-faced and overweight dogs, and never leave an animal in a car.

- Walk early morning or late evening in warm weather.
- Carry water, and rest in shade.
- Be especially careful with flat-faced and overweight dogs; for some, a hot day means no walk at all.
- Never leave any animal in a car or sunlit enclosed space.

If your dog has had heatstroke before, ask your own vet what precautions make sense for it.
