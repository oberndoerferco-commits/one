## First: if a cat has had a dog flea treatment, call a vet now

**Never put a dog flea treatment or a household flea spray on a cat.** The PDSA warns that these often contain **permethrin**, which is extremely poisonous to cats. International Cat Care says even a tiny amount can be dangerous, because a cat's liver can't break it down.

Call your vet **immediately** if your cat:

- has had a **dog spot-on, spray or other dog flea product** put on it, even a small amount
- has been in close contact with a **dog treated with permethrin**, for example grooming it or sharing its bed
- is **twitching, trembling, drooling, unsteady**, has wide pupils, **seizures** or trouble breathing

The PDSA says signs usually start within a few hours but can take up to 36 hours, and that untreated permethrin poisoning can be fatal. If the product is still on the coat, the PDSA advises washing it off straight away with warm water and diluted washing-up liquid, if your cat will let you, and then calling your vet. Bring the packaging.

Also call your vet today if your pet has **sore, infected or extremely itchy skin**, or if a **young kitten or puppy**, or an old or ill pet, has a lot of fleas: the PDSA says heavy flea burdens can cause anaemia (blood loss).

## How do I know my dog or cat has fleas?

Look for scratching and nibbling, especially at the back end, and for flea dirt in the coat; you often won't see a live flea.

The PDSA lists scratching, bites or a rash, bald patches or rough fur, sore or infected skin, and nibbling at the back end. Flea dirt is often found on the lower back and above the base of the tail. ESCCAP Germany says fleas aren't always visible, especially in dark, dense or long coats.

To check, ESCCAP describes combing the coat close to the skin with a very fine-toothed **flea comb** and putting what comes out onto a **damp tissue**. If it turns reddish around the specks, that's flea dirt: fleas pass small amounts of blood in their droppings.

## Why are there still fleas after I treated my pet?

Because most of the problem is in your home, not on your pet: eggs, larvae and pupae keep hatching into new fleas.

ESCCAP says these development stages make up over 95% of the whole flea population, mainly in sleeping and resting places. The PDSA puts it this way: 50 fleas on your pet can mean up to a thousand fleas and eggs in your home, and eggs can survive for up to a year. That's why the PDSA says clearing a flea problem can take a few weeks, and why treating only the pet often fails.

ESCCAP adds other common reasons for failure: not every animal in the household was treated, washing or swimming reduced how well the product works, or the gaps between treatments were too long.

## What should I do today?

Get a suitable product from your vet, treat every dog and cat in the home on the same day, and start on the home.

1. **Call your vet** for a flea product for each pet. The PDSA advises a prescription product from your vet where possible, and says products bought off the shelf often contain less effective ingredients. If your pet was checked recently, you may be able to collect one without an appointment.
2. **Treat every pet on the same day**: all dogs, cats and rabbits (PDSA). Use only products made for that species and that weight, as your vet advises.
3. **Keep cats away from dog products.** If you use a permethrin product on your dog, International Cat Care says to keep dog and cat apart for 72 hours. It's simpler to ask your vet for a dog product without permethrin if you have both.
4. **Worm your pets.** Fleas can pass on tapeworm, so the PDSA recommends worming at the same time. Ask your vet which product.
5. **Don't rely on shampoos.** The PDSA says flea shampoo generally isn't good at killing fleas, and to speak to your vet before buying a flea collar.

Follow the product instructions and your vet's advice on how often to repeat. Don't add a second product on top without asking.

## How do I get rid of fleas in my home?

Vacuum often, hot-wash bedding and blankets, and with a heavy or repeated infestation use a household flea spray as your vet advises.

- **Vacuum** regularly, especially around pet beds and under furniture (PDSA).
- **Wash bedding and blankets** at 60°C, as both the PDSA and ESCCAP Germany recommend.
- **Clean every sleeping and resting place** your pet uses: ESCCAP names these as where the development stages gather.
- **Household flea spray.** ESCCAP says that with a heavy or repeated infestation the home should be treated too, with sprays or foggers. The PDSA says household sprays are available from vets or online, but warns that many contain permethrin, which is extremely toxic to cats, fish and birds. Read the label, follow it exactly, and keep cats, fish and birds away as it says.

## Can fleas bite me?

Yes: ESCCAP Germany says fleas move on to people when there are so many on the animal that they run out of room.

If you are being bitten, ESCCAP recommends treating all the animals in the household and the home. Fleas can also carry other infections, including the dog and cat tapeworm and the bacterium behind cat scratch disease, which can infect people too (ESCCAP). If you have bites that worry you, speak to your doctor.

## How do I stop fleas coming back?

Keep up regular treatment for every pet, as often as your vet recommends, and keep bedding and floors clean.

The PDSA recommends treating all pets as often as your vet advises, checking regularly for flea dirt, vacuuming and washing bedding regularly, and only letting flea-free pets into your home. For pets that are allergic to flea bites, ESCCAP Germany recommends consistent year-round flea control. For parasite prevention in general, see [fleas, ticks and worms](/animals/fleas-ticks-worms).

## When should I take my pet to the vet?

Straight away for any sign of permethrin poisoning, and soon if the skin is sore, infected or very itchy, or if fleas keep coming back.

Book an appointment if your pet has:

- sore, broken, crusty or infected skin, or bald patches
- intense itching all over, which ESCCAP says can mean an allergy to flea saliva
- a lot of fleas on a young, old or ill animal, which the PDSA says can cause anaemia
- fleas despite treatment, so your vet can check the product and the plan

For itchy dogs in general, see [dog itching](/animals/dog-itching). If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know about the fleas?

Which pets you have, what you have used and when, and how the skin looks.

- every animal in the household, including rabbits, and each pet's species and weight
- every flea product used, with the name from the packaging and the date, and on which animal
- whether a dog product could have reached a cat, and any signs since
- where you found fleas or flea dirt, and since when
- whether your pet is scratching, has sore skin or bald patches
- whether your pet bathes or swims often
- whether people at home are being bitten
- when your pets were last wormed
