## First, the signs that mean go now

Ferrets love to chew, and the MSD Veterinary Manual warns that a blocked gut "can be fatal and usually requires emergency surgery". A ferret that is eating less needs a vet the same day; some signs mean it can't wait even that long.

Go to a vet that treats ferrets **now**, and phone ahead, if your ferret:

- has eaten **nothing**, or drunk nothing, for 24 hours
- has passed **no poo** or much less than usual, or strains without passing anything
- is **vomiting** severely or forcefully
- has **black, tarry** poo, or blood in its poo
- is extremely lethargic or seems to be in a lot of pain
- isn't eating and is drooling heavily, grinding its teeth or pawing at its mouth
- is coughing or choking
- is weak, wobbly or has collapsed
- has swallowed, or may have swallowed, rubber, foam, plastic or part of a toy

Don't wait for vomiting. VCA points out that although you might expect vomiting to be the main sign of a blockage, that is not always the case, and the MSD Veterinary Manual says vomiting is more common with stomach inflammation (gastritis) than with swallowed objects. A ferret that has simply gone quiet and stopped eating can still have a blockage: a 2002 review by vets at the University of Zurich says a swallowed object must be considered in every ferret that isn't eating and is lethargic, with or without vomiting and diarrhoea. See also our [emergency page](/emergency).

## How long can a ferret go without eating?

Not long. Ferrets need to eat often, and the MSD Veterinary Manual says a ferret that hasn't eaten or drunk for 24 hours should see a vet immediately.

MSD explains that ferrets have a fast metabolism and a short digestive tract, so they need to eat frequently. Its guide to ferret emergencies says to see a vet **within 24 hours** if your ferret has lost its appetite but shows no other signs of illness, and **immediately** if it has eaten or drunk nothing for 24 hours.

With a blockage, time matters. VCA says that if a blockage has been there for more than 24 hours, the blocked part of the bowel may lose its blood supply, and then a much more complicated operation is needed to remove that piece of bowel. If your ferret has an insulinoma, eating matters even more: the MSD Veterinary Manual's chapter for vets says owners must make sure such a ferret eats throughout the day. See [weak, wobbly or collapsed](/animals/ferret-weak-wobbly-collapsed).

## What are the signs of a blockage in a ferret?

Loss of appetite, little or no poo, lethargy, drooling or teeth grinding, belly pain, and sometimes vomiting, diarrhoea or black, tarry poo.

The MSD Veterinary Manual lists loss of appetite, clenching or grinding the teeth, heavy drooling, sharp belly pain, diarrhoea and tarry black poo as signs of a swallowed object. VCA names severe lethargy, a reduced appetite with or without vomiting, and less poo than usual as the key signs, and says objects stuck in the small intestine often cause severe pain. MSD adds that a ferret with a blockage stops eating and passing poo, and may cough, choke or start vomiting as it gets worse.

VCA points out that these signs are shared with many other illnesses, so only a vet can tell. According to VCA, X-rays find most objects, and sometimes the vet gives barium, a liquid that shows up on X-rays, and takes a series of pictures. The MSD Veterinary Manual's chapter for vets names an ultrasound scan as another way to find them.

## What do ferrets swallow?

Mostly soft rubber, foam and plastic, and sometimes hairballs. VCA's rule of thumb: if it fits in a ferret's mouth, it can be swallowed.

- The MSD Veterinary Manual says swallowed objects are usually soft rubber or plastic items, but can also be hairballs.
- VCA says swallowing objects is common, especially in ferrets under a year old, and names shoe inserts, earplugs, children's and pet toys, erasers, rubber bands, balloons, foam from speakers and headphones, and swimming goggle liners.
- A 2002 review by vets at the University of Zurich describes balloons, earplugs and cleaning sponges among the objects that blocked ferrets' small intestine.
- **Hairballs:** MSD notes that ferrets moult every spring and autumn, and that hairballs can cause vomiting, a poor appetite or a blockage. In older ferrets, VCA adds, a hairball or a tumour can also block the gut.
- **Raw meat:** the PDSA doesn't recommend it, partly because of a higher risk of problems such as gut blockages.

The best protection is a ferret-proof home. MSD advises putting away anything small enough to be swallowed.

## Is there a home remedy for a blockage in a ferret?

No. A blockage needs a vet: the MSD Veterinary Manual says surgery or endoscopy is usually needed to remove the object.

After the object is out, MSD says the ferret should also be treated for inflammation of the stomach. VCA says that if a blockage is diagnosed and treated early, most ferrets recover well from the operation. While you arrange the visit:

- **Don't wait for the object to pass.**
- **Don't give laxatives, oil or other remedies, and don't syringe-feed,** unless your vet tells you to.
- **Don't give human medicines or medicines meant for other animals.** The RSPCA warns that prescribed medicines for other animals "can be very dangerous to ferrets", and VCA that ferrets can be "easily poisoned or killed with common human medications".
- **Don't treat at home without a diagnosis.** VCA advises against it because many ferret illnesses have similar signs.

## Why else might my ferret stop eating or vomit?

Stomach inflammation, hairballs, gut infections, dental problems, low blood sugar and illnesses such as kidney disease can all do it, which is why the vet looks for the cause.

- **Stomach inflammation and ulcers:** VCA links them to a bacterium, *Helicobacter mustelae*, that almost all ferrets carry from weaning, and names stress as an important trigger: a move, a change of diet, an overbearing older ferret, a playful cat or dog, or a cage that isn't cleaned.
- **Hairballs,** especially around the spring and autumn moult (MSD).
- **Infections:** VCA names, among others, epizootic catarrhal enteritis (ECE), a coronavirus that typically affects very young ferrets. Ferrets can also catch human flu, and VCA lists severe lethargy among its signs. VCA also lists loss of appetite among the signs of canine distemper, which it says is usually fatal in ferrets.
- **Dental disease,** which VCA says can affect ferrets from two years of age.
- **Low blood sugar from an insulinoma:** MSD's chapter for vets lists drooling, pawing at the mouth and signs of nausea.
- **Kidney disease, heart disease or cancers such as lymphoma:** MSD and VCA list a poor appetite among their signs.

## When should I take my ferret to the vet?

Today if your ferret is eating less, even with no other signs; immediately if it has eaten nothing for 24 hours or shows any of the signs at the top.

- The MSD Veterinary Manual says to see a vet within 24 hours for loss of appetite with no other signs, and for vomiting or diarrhoea that lasts more than 24 hours, "because ferrets can quickly become dehydrated and weak".
- The RSPCA says that if your ferrets' eating or drinking habits change, you should ask your vet for advice straight away.
- VCA says any diarrhoea, vomiting or other gut problem in a ferret should be brought to your vet's attention as soon as possible.

An occasional soft or irregular poo on its own is not usually a problem, according to MSD.

## What will the vet want to know about my ferret?

When your ferret last ate, drank and passed poo, any vomiting, anything missing or chewed, its diet and age, recent stress, and earlier illnesses and medicines.

- When your ferret last ate and drank normally, and how much less it is eating now
- When it last passed poo, and what it looked like: black and tarry, bloody, or very little
- Whether it has vomited, how often, and what came up
- Anything missing or chewed: toys, earplugs, foam, rubber bands, balloons, shoe inserts
- Its age, diet and any recent change, including raw meat
- Whether it is moulting
- Any recent stress: a move, a new ferret or another new pet
- Earlier illnesses, such as an insulinoma, and all medicines
- Its vaccinations, and whether anyone at home has flu
