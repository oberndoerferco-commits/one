## First, the signs that mean go now

Knowing your horse's temperature, pulse and breathing helps you spot change early, but measuring must never delay help. The British Horse Society (BHS) and University of Nottingham guide says that if your horse is showing signs of discomfort or illness, get veterinary help immediately and **don't waste time measuring its values first**.

Call your vet now, without stopping to measure, if your horse:

- shows signs of colic: pawing, looking round at its flank, kicking at its belly, lying down and getting up, rolling, sweating for no reason, eating less, or passing fewer droppings or none
- breathes fast or with effort while at rest
- has pale, dark red or dry gums
- has a strong, bounding pulse at the back of the fetlock, hot feet, or does not want to move
- is dull and withdrawn, or unusually restless

These signs come from the BHS and University of Nottingham REACT campaign on colic, the MSD Veterinary Manual, Penn State Extension, World Horse Welfare and the Swiss Institute of Equine Medicine (ISME) at the University of Bern. Penn Vet's New Bolton Center adds that a heart rate that stays **above 60 beats per minute** can be telling in a horse that shows few other signs.

## What are normal vital signs for an adult horse?

At rest, 28 to 44 heartbeats and 8 to 16 breaths a minute in both sources below, and a rectal temperature of 37.5 to 38.5 °C (BHS) or 37.2 to 38.0 °C (ISME). Your own horse's normal matters more than any table.

| At rest | BHS and University of Nottingham | ISME Avenches and University of Bern |
|---|---|---|
| Heart (pulse) rate | 28–44 beats per minute | 28–44 per minute |
| Breathing rate | 8–16 breaths per minute | 8–16 per minute |
| Rectal temperature | 37.5–38.5 °C | 37.2–38.0 °C |

Sources differ a little. Penn State Extension, for example, gives 10 to 24 breaths per minute as normal, and notes that larger horses often have lower rates and smaller horses higher ones.

The BHS and University of Nottingham guide explains why your horse's own values matter:

- each horse has its own normal, which depends on its age, size and fitness
- heart rate and breathing rate rise with stress, anxiety, excitement and exercise, and may also rise when a horse is in pain or ill
- some horses are always slightly outside the usual range without anything being wrong, but it is worth having the vet check that there is no underlying problem

## How do I take my horse's temperature?

With a digital thermometer in the rectum, standing to the side of the hindquarters, with a helper holding the horse.

The BHS and University of Nottingham guide describes these steps:

1. Switch on the digital thermometer and check the display works.
2. Put lubricant on the tip.
3. Ask a helper to hold your horse.
4. Stand at the side of your horse, near the hindquarters, to reduce the risk of being kicked.
5. Hold the tail and move it gently to the side.
6. Gently insert just the narrow tip of the thermometer into the anus, angled to one side so that it rests against the wall of the rectum rather than in a ball of droppings, which would read colder.
7. Hold it for at least 30 seconds, or until it beeps. Never let go of the thermometer, as it can disappear inside.
8. Remove it, read it, then clean and disinfect it.

Safety comes first. The guide says some horses resent a rectal thermometer, so wear a hard hat and stay out of range of the hind legs. If you don't feel comfortable doing it, ask someone more experienced or your vet to show you.

## How do I take my horse's pulse or heart rate?

Listen with a stethoscope behind the left elbow, or feel the facial artery under the jaw, count for 15 seconds and multiply by four.

- **With a stethoscope:** the BHS and University of Nottingham guide says to place it on the left side of the chest, just behind the elbow. In a broad-chested or heavier horse the heart is harder to hear; moving the front leg forward can help. Each heartbeat has two parts, and finding the best spot takes practice.
- **Without a stethoscope:** feel the facial artery, which runs over the jawbone at the bottom of the cheek muscle, roughly between where a noseband and a throatlash would sit. The guide describes it as about 1 cm wide and tube-like. Rest your fingers on it from the inside of the jawbone with your thumb outside, because you would feel your own pulse in your thumb.
- **Counting:** count the beats for 15 seconds and multiply by four (BHS and University of Nottingham). Penn State Extension suggests counting for 30 seconds and doubling, or 15 seconds times four if your horse won't stand still.

## How do I count my horse's breathing rate?

Watch the flank rise and fall. One breath in plus one breath out counts as one breath.

The BHS and University of Nottingham guide says to watch the belly and flank, count the breaths for 15 seconds and multiply by four. Penn State Extension advises looking at the flank rather than the nostrils.

## What should my horse's gums look like?

Moist, smooth and pink, with the colour coming back within about 2 seconds after you press on them.

- **Colour and moisture.** Penn State Extension describes normal gums as wet, pink and shiny, and says pale or dark red gums are abnormal and a reason to call the vet. ISME and the University of Bern describe healthy membranes as pink and moist, and pale, red or dry ones as a warning sign.
- **Capillary refill time.** Press a finger on the gum until the colour disappears, then count how long the pink takes to return. Penn State Extension gives 1 to 2 seconds as normal; ISME counts over 2 seconds as a warning sign, and Penn Vet says over two seconds suggests dehydration.
- **Skin tent.** Penn State Extension describes pinching a fold of skin on the shoulder or neck: normally it flattens in 1 to 3 seconds, and if it takes longer than 4 seconds, your horse is dehydrated and you should contact your vet.

Penn State Extension also warns to be careful your horse doesn't bite you while you check its gums.

## What else can I check?

Gut sounds, the digital pulse, the glands under the jaw, and your horse's face.

- **Gut sounds.** Penn Vet's New Bolton Center says that normally you hear several gurgles a minute, with a louder rumble every minute or two. Silence is not good, and neither is excessive noise.
- **Digital pulse.** Penn State Extension names the digital artery, just below the fetlock, as a place to feel a pulse. World Horse Welfare lists a strong or bounding digital pulse at the back of the fetlock among the signs of laminitis, so it helps to know how it normally feels.
- **Glands under the jaw.** ISME and the University of Bern include feeling the lymph nodes between the jawbones in a health check. Normally they are not enlarged, not painful and move under the fingers; enlarged, painful nodes that don't move are a warning sign.
- **The face.** The ISME poster, based on research on the equine pain face, describes a horse in pain as having stiff ears turned back that react little, a tense look, tense, widened nostrils, an angular muzzle with pressed lips and tense facial muscles. A comfortable horse has mobile ears that react to sounds, a relaxed face and a rounded, relaxed muzzle.

## How often should I check my horse's vital signs?

Learn the normal values while your horse is healthy, and check your horse over every day.

- Penn State Extension recommends assessing vital signs at least daily or weekly, and says all you need is a stethoscope, a thermometer and a watch.
- Penn State Extension also advises working out each horse's own averages before illness is suspected, and the BHS and University of Nottingham guide says regular checks tell you what is normal for your horse.
- Redwings recommends checking every horse carefully at least twice a day.
- ISME and the University of Bern suggest asking yourself at every visit: does my horse seem calm and alert? Has it eaten and drunk? Has it passed droppings and urine? Then take a quick look at the eyes, nose, mouth, body, legs and hooves, and watch it move for any lameness.

## When should I call the vet?

At once if your horse seems ill or uncomfortable; for advice if a reading has changed for no clear reason.

The BHS and University of Nottingham guide advises:

- **straight away** if your horse shows signs of discomfort or illness, without measuring first
- **for advice** if temperature, pulse or breathing have changed and there is no reason such as recent stress or exercise
- **for advice** if your horse seems ill or uncomfortable even though its values are normal, or whenever you are worried
- **for a check-up** if your horse's values are always outside the usual range, to make sure there is no underlying condition

The MSD Veterinary Manual adds that a horse that is not eating, has diarrhoea, dry or fewer droppings, is coughing or sneezing, has discharge from the eyes or nose, or is limping or reluctant to move for more than a day or two should be examined by a vet.

## What will the vet want to know about my horse's vital signs?

Your horse's usual values, today's readings and when you took them, what your horse was doing, and any other signs.

- your horse's normal resting temperature, pulse and breathing rate, if you know them
- today's readings, and the time you took each one
- whether your horse was resting, or had just worked, travelled, been upset or been out in the heat
- whether it is eating and drinking, and passing droppings and urine normally
- any other signs, such as a cough, nasal discharge, swelling, heat in the legs or lameness
- whether other horses on the yard are unwell, since the ISME poster asks whether a problem affects one horse or may be contagious
- any medicine it has had, and its passport: before treating, the vet must check whether your horse is recorded as intended for the food chain (Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/963, Article 39)
