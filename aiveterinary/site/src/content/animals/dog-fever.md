## First, the signs that mean go now

Go to a vet straight away, and call ahead, if your dog has a high temperature or feels hot and:

- is weak, wobbly, collapsed, or unusually confused or drowsy
- is breathing fast or with difficulty
- has pale, yellowish, very red or blue-tinged gums
- is vomiting repeatedly or has bloody diarrhoea
- has a seizure
- has been in the heat, in a car or exercising hard: this may be **heatstroke**, which needs cooling and a vet immediately
- is a young puppy, elderly, or already has a serious illness
- has a rectal temperature of around **41 °C or more**, or **below 37.5 °C**

## Normal temperature for a dog

According to the MSD Veterinary Manual, the normal **rectal** temperature of a dog is **37.5–39.2 °C** (99.5–102.5 °F).

Most dogs with a true fever have a temperature somewhere between about 39.5 and 41 °C. Exercise also raises body temperature, so if your dog has just been active, let them rest quietly before you measure.

A temperature **below** the normal range can also be a sign of illness and is worth a call to your vet.

## Fever or overheating?

These are not the same, and the difference matters:

- **Fever** is the body deliberately raising its "thermostat", usually in response to infection or inflammation. It is a sign that something is wrong, not a disease in itself.
- **Overheating (hyperthermia)**, such as heatstroke, happens when a dog takes on or produces more heat than it can lose, for example in a hot car or on a hot day. The body is not in control, and the temperature can climb to dangerous levels.

If your dog may have overheated, follow heatstroke first aid and get to a vet. For a dog with a true fever, vets advise against cold baths and other cooling at home, because they work against the body's own temperature control.

## How to take your dog's temperature

Touch isn't reliable. Vets regard a **rectal** reading as the most accurate.

You need:

- a **digital thermometer**, kept only for your pet, ideally with disposable covers
- a **water-based lubricant**
- a **helper** to hold and calm your dog

Steps:

1. Ask your helper to hold your dog gently but securely, standing or lying on one side.
2. Put a cover on the thermometer and lubricate the tip well.
3. Lift the tail and gently slide only the tip of the thermometer into the bottom. Never force it.
4. Hold it still until it beeps, then remove it and read it.
5. Clean the thermometer, wash your hands and write down the reading and the time.

Some practical points:

- Don't use a family ear thermometer. Ear readings in pets often don't match rectal temperatures, and armpit readings give inconsistent results.
- If your dog is in pain, very distressed or tries to bite, stop. Take them to the vet instead.
- Ask your vet nurse to show you how to do it next time you visit. It's much easier once you've seen it done.

## What can cause a fever

Many conditions can cause a fever. In dogs, the common groups are:

- **infections**: bacterial, viral, fungal, or infections carried by ticks
- **inflammation**, including immune-mediated diseases, where the immune system attacks the body
- **cancer**

Sometimes a fever has an obvious cause, such as an infected wound or a recent operation site. Sometimes it needs a series of tests to find, and when no cause is found straight away vets call it a "fever of unknown origin".

A fever can make dogs lose their appetite, become low in energy and get dehydrated, so a dog with a fever should always be checked by a vet.

## Never give your own fever medicines

**Do not give your dog paracetamol, ibuprofen, aspirin or any other human medicine for a fever or for pain.**

- **Ibuprofen** has a narrow safety margin in dogs. It can cause stomach ulcers, perforation of the stomach and kidney damage.
- **Paracetamol** can damage a dog's liver and red blood cells.
- **Aspirin** and any other human medicine should only ever be given if your vet prescribes it.

Fever itself probably helps the body fight some infections. Whether and how to treat it is your vet's decision, after finding out what is causing it.

If your dog has already swallowed any human medicine, call your vet now, with the packet in your hand.

## When to see a vet today

Book an appointment the same day if your dog's temperature is above the normal range and:

- they are quieter than usual or off their food
- they have any other sign of illness, such as vomiting, diarrhoea, coughing, limping or a painful area
- they have a wound, a recent operation or a recent tick bite
- the temperature is still raised when you re-check after rest

## What the vet will want to know

- The readings you took, when, and how (rectal or otherwise)
- What your dog was doing before (resting, exercising, in the heat)
- Appetite, drinking, vomiting, diarrhoea, coughing
- Any wounds, recent surgery, tick bites or travel abroad
- Any medicines your dog is taking, and anything you have already given
