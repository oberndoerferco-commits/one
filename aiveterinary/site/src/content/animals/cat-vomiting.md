## First, the signs that mean go now

Go to a veterinary clinic immediately, and call ahead, if your cat:

- is retching repeatedly but bringing nothing up, or seems to be choking
- vomits blood, or material that looks like coffee grounds
- is very weak, flat, hiding, or breathing with an open mouth
- has pale or yellow-tinged gums
- may have eaten something poisonous: lilies (any part of the plant, including pollen), antifreeze, human painkillers, flea products meant for dogs
- may have swallowed string, ribbon, a hair tie or thread (do not pull on anything hanging from the mouth or bottom)
- is a kitten, very old, or already has a condition such as kidney disease, diabetes or an overactive thyroid
- vomits several times in a few hours and cannot keep water down

## Hairball or vomiting?

A hairball is a sausage-shaped clump of hair, usually with a little fluid. The cat typically coughs and retches, brings it up, and is perfectly normal afterwards. Once or twice a month in a long-haired cat is common.

Vomiting is different. Food, foam, yellow or green fluid, or liquid with only a few hairs in it is vomit, even if your cat retches in the same way. Vomiting more than once or twice a month, or hairballs coming up every few days, is a sign that something else is going on. Many vets now regard frequent "hairballs" as a reason to look at the gut, not just the coat.

## What can I do at home if my cat has vomited once?

A single episode in a cat who then eats and behaves normally can be watched at home.

- Leave water available. Do not withhold it.
- Offer a small amount of your cat's usual food a few hours later. If it stays down, go back to normal meals.
- Do not withhold food from a cat for more than a few hours. Cats that stop eating can develop liver problems quickly.
- Note what came up, how often, and when in relation to meals. A photo helps.
- Check that litter tray use is normal. No urine for a day is an emergency in its own right, especially in male cats.
- Never give human medication. Paracetamol in particular is highly toxic to cats.

## When should I take my vomiting cat to the vet?

Today if your cat vomits more than two or three times in a day, keeps vomiting for more than 24 hours, is off food for more than a day, or vomits most weeks.

Book an appointment today if:

- vomiting happens more than two or three times in a day
- it continues on and off for more than 24 hours
- your cat is off food for more than a day
- there is diarrhoea as well, or no stool at all
- your cat is drinking much more than usual, or losing weight
- your cat is vomiting most weeks, even if seemingly well in between
- your cat is lethargic, hiding, or less interested in you than usual

Cats hide illness well. A cat that is "a bit quiet" and vomiting is worth a call.

## What will the vet want to know about my cat's vomiting?

When it started, how often, what the vomit looked like and when it happens, plus appetite, drinking, litter habits, access to hazards and any treatments.

- When it started and how often
- What the vomit looked like: hair, food, foam, bile, blood
- Whether it happens straight after eating or at random times
- Appetite, drinking, weight and litter tray habits
- Access to plants, string, toys or anything new in the house
- Whether your cat goes outdoors and could have hunted or scavenged
- Any medications, flea or worm treatments
- Other cats in the house and whether they are affected

## Why is my cat vomiting?

Hairballs, eating too fast, food changes, mild stomach upsets, worms and grass eating are likely causes; swallowed objects, kidney, thyroid, bowel, pancreas or liver disease and poisoning are less common but important.

| Likely | Less common but important |
|---|---|
| Hairballs | Swallowed string or other foreign body |
| Eating too fast | Kidney disease |
| Sudden food change or food intolerance | Overactive thyroid |
| Mild stomach upset | Inflammatory bowel disease |
| Intestinal worms | Pancreatitis or liver disease |
| Grass eating | Poisoning, especially lilies |

Your vet decides which of these is likely for *your* cat, based on the examination and any tests. This list is to help you understand the conversation, not to diagnose.

If your cat vomits regularly, even if each episode seems minor, talk to your own vet rather than putting it down to hairballs.
