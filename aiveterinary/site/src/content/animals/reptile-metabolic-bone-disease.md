## First, the signs that mean go now

In bearded dragons with metabolic bone disease, VCA vets describe muscle twitching, seizures, severe lethargy and a loss of appetite. Go to a vet who treats reptiles now, and call ahead, if your reptile:

- is **twitching, trembling or having fits**
- is so **weak** that it cannot walk or push its body up off the ground
- may have a **broken bone**: a leg or the spine suddenly looks bent, swollen or out of place, even without a fall
- has tissue **sticking out of its vent** (a prolapse)
- has **stopped eating** and is very lethargic

The MSD Veterinary Manual lists fractures of the spine or legs, cloacal prolapse and muscle spasms among the signs, and VCA vets note fractures that appear to have happened without any injury. The RSPCA advises seeking urgent vet advice if you see signs of metabolic bone disease. And VCA vets are clear: "DO NOT ATTEMPT TREATMENT without guidance from your veterinarian."

## What is metabolic bone disease?

A group of bone problems in which the skeleton becomes weak, soft or misshapen, most often because the reptile is short of calcium or vitamin D3, which usually means missing UVB light or the wrong diet.

- The MSD Veterinary Manual calls the most common form, nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism, "the most common bone disease seen in pet reptiles". The RSPCA calls metabolic bone disease one of the most common problems affecting captive reptiles.
- **Why UVB matters.** VCA vets explain that reptiles need UV light to make vitamin D3, which the intestines need to absorb calcium from food. The RSPCA puts it the same way: UVB allows reptiles to make vitamin D3, vital for storing and using calcium.
- **Not only day-active reptiles.** VCA vets note that even nocturnal reptiles such as leopard geckos raise their vitamin D levels after UV exposure in captivity.
- **Why it matters early.** VCA vets warn that metabolic bone disease can be fatal if it is not recognised and treated early.
- **A kidney form.** The MSD Veterinary Manual also describes a related kidney disorder in adult reptiles, secondary renal hyperparathyroidism.

## What are the signs of metabolic bone disease?

They depend on the species: a soft or misshapen shell and slow growth in tortoises, a swollen, soft jaw and swollen legs in lizards, and in all reptiles weakness, trembling and a poor appetite.

- **Tortoises (VCA):** a misshapen shell or deformed bones in one or all four legs, slow growth, often never reaching full adult size, and a shell that is often flimsy or pliable beyond the first 6 months of age.
- **Bearded dragons (VCA):** swelling of the lower jaw, softening of the jaw and facial bones, swelling of the hind legs, legs that tremble as the lizard tries to walk, weakness, and fractures without any injury.
- **Bearded dragons and leopard geckos (RSPCA):** muscle twitching, swollen legs, fragile bones and, eventually, permanently deformed limbs, jaws, spine or tail.
- **Terrapins (RSPCA):** swellings around the mouth or eyes and an uneven shell.
- **All reptiles (MSD Veterinary Manual):** poor appetite, weakness, an inability to walk normally, swollen or distorted jaw or leg bones, fractures of the spine or legs, cloacal prolapse and muscle spasms.

## Why does a reptile get metabolic bone disease?

Usually because of its set-up and diet: no UVB lamp, an old one or glass in the way, too little calcium and too much phosphorus in the food, or an enclosure that is too cold.

- **Diet.** The MSD Veterinary Manual names a poor diet with the wrong ratio of calcium to phosphorus and a lack of vitamin D3. In bearded dragons, VCA vets name a diet high in phosphorus and low in calcium and/or vitamin D3.
- **Light.** The MSD Veterinary Manual names a lack of ultraviolet B light. Glass gets in the way: the British Chelonia Group (BCG), a UK tortoise and turtle charity, reminds keepers that glass filters out all of the sun's ultraviolet rays, and the vet Frances Baines, writing for the BCG, notes that most glass and plastics block UVB.
- **Temperature.** The MSD Veterinary Manual also lists inadequate temperature control in the enclosure.
- **Too much protein in tortoises.** The BCG says soft shell (osteodystrophy) "can result from a combination of calcium deficiency, incorrect lighting and excessive protein in the diet", and warns that high-protein foods such as dog and cat food can be harmful, especially in young tortoises. The German veterinary association for animal welfare (TVT) agrees that too much protein makes tortoises grow fast but usually leads to a lumpy shell, soft bones and kidney damage.

## How much UVB does my reptile need?

It depends on the species and the lamp, so follow a care sheet for your species and your vet's advice; for bearded dragons, the RSPCA gives a UV Index of 3.0–5.0 in the basking zone.

- **Measure it.** For bearded dragons, the RSPCA advises: "Check the UVB output regularly using a UV Index meter, to ensure the UVB gradient ranges from 3.0–5.0 in the basking zone to zero in the shade."
- **Replace it.** VCA vets say UVB bulbs should be replaced every 9–12 months or as directed by the manufacturer. The BCG explains that many lamps need to be close to the tortoise and replaced frequently, even if they still seem to work, because their UV output falls over time and with distance.
- **Nothing in between.** VCA vets say there must be no glass or plastic between your pet and the light.
- **Over the basking spot.** Frances Baines explains that vitamin D3 is only made in warm skin exposed to UVB, so the UVB lamp belongs over the basking spot.
- **Not too close.** VCA vets warn that a bulb placed too close can cause eye inflammation (photokeratitis) and skin damage, so follow the manufacturer's instructions.
- **Every day.** For tortoises, the BCG says the UVB lamp should be on every day, mimicking the natural day length.
- **Sunshine.** VCA vets encourage regular exposure to natural, direct sunlight outdoors, unfiltered through glass, and the BCG calls unfiltered summer sunlight ideal for tortoises.

## Can I treat metabolic bone disease at home?

No: VCA vets say not to attempt treatment without your vet's guidance, because the right treatment depends on x-rays and blood tests, and getting supplements wrong can do harm.

- **Diagnosis.** The MSD Veterinary Manual says diagnosis needs x-rays showing loss of bone throughout the skeleton, and blood tests showing low vitamin D and an imbalance of calcium and phosphorus.
- **Treatment.** Correcting the diet and the set-up, including UV light, are the most important parts of successful treatment, according to the MSD Veterinary Manual. Critical cases need treatment from the vet, such as fluids, nutritional support and calcium injections.
- **No supplements on your own.** The TVT says that for tortoises fed mainly on meadow grasses and herbs, a vitamin supplement is unnecessary and can do harm, and VCA vets warn that an overdose of vitamin A can occur in tortoises. Ask your vet which supplements your reptile needs, if any.
- **Outlook.** VCA vets say not all cases can be resolved, and that severe cases may leave a tortoise with misshapen bones or a permanently deformed shell. The RSPCA notes that the disease can end in permanently deformed limbs, jaws, spine or tail, which is why early treatment matters.

## How can I prevent metabolic bone disease?

With the right UVB lamp, replaced on time and with no glass in the way, a diet suited to the species with enough calcium, the right temperatures, and regular vet checks.

- **UVB light,** as described above.
- **Calcium.** The RSPCA recommends a small bowl of plain calcium powder available at all times for leopard geckos to lick. For tortoises, the BCG suggests calcium carbonate scattered on the ground, especially for egg-laying females, which need far more calcium, and the TVT says calcium should always be on offer, for example as cuttlefish bone or the shell of boiled eggs.
- **Diet.** The BCG says tortoises need a diet high in fibre, vitamins and minerals but low in fat and protein. For bearded dragons, avoid diets high in phosphorus and low in calcium (VCA). Ask your vet which supplements to use and how often.
- **Temperatures** right for the species, with a warm basking spot and a cooler end.
- **Check-ups.** The MSD Veterinary Manual recommends an annual health check, which helps make sure your reptile is well nourished.

## When should I take my reptile to the vet?

Today if you notice any sign of metabolic bone disease, such as a soft shell, a swollen jaw or legs, limping, trembling or a poor appetite, and straight away for the signs at the top of this page.

- The RSPCA advises urgent vet advice for signs of metabolic bone disease.
- VCA vets say any change from normal in a bearded dragon should be evaluated by your vet immediately.
- A young tortoise whose shell is still flimsy or pliable after 6 months of age needs a check (VCA).
- Even without signs, an annual health check is worth it (MSD Veterinary Manual).

Not every vet treats reptiles: the MSD Veterinary Manual advises asking about a vet's experience with reptiles. If your reptile has also stopped eating, see [my reptile is not eating](/animals/reptile-not-eating).

## What will the vet want to know about my reptile?

The species and age, how it has grown, its UVB lamp and its age, temperatures, diet and supplements, time in the sun, and when you first noticed the change.

- The species, age, and how fast it has grown
- The UVB lamp: type, age, how far it is from the basking spot, and whether there is glass, plastic or mesh in between
- The basking and cool-end temperatures, and how long the lights are on each day
- Its diet, and for insect-eaters what the insects are fed
- Which supplements you use, and how often
- How much time it spends outdoors in unfiltered sunlight
- For a female, any egg-laying
- When you first noticed the change; photos taken over time help

If your reptile is twitching, too weak to walk or may have a broken bone, stop reading and call a vet who treats reptiles now.
