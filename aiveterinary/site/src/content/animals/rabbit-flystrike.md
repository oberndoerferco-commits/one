## First: if you see even one maggot, call a vet and go now

Flystrike is an emergency, day or night. The PDSA says to contact a vet immediately for an emergency appointment **if you find even one maggot**, because flystrike can cause death quickly. The Rabbit Welfare Association & Fund (RWAF) calls it "a true emergency – day or night – and treatment cannot wait".

Right now:

1. **Phone your vet**, or the nearest emergency clinic that sees rabbits, and say it is flystrike.
2. **Put your rabbit in a carrier** and go. The PDSA suggests bringing its bonded companion if you can.
3. **Don't bath your rabbit or dunk it in water.** The PDSA says this won't drown the maggots and can cause shock; the RWAF adds that wet fur clogs the clippers the vet needs.
4. **Don't try to treat it at home.** The PDSA advises against pulling maggots off yourself, as this causes pain and could lead to shock. The RWAF says you can pick off visible maggots with tweezers only if it doesn't delay getting to the vet. The safest choice is to leave them and go.

Also go straight in if you see **fly eggs** in the fur, wet or matted fur with a bad smell around the bottom, or a rabbit that is suddenly quiet, not eating or collapsed.

## What is flystrike?

Flies lay eggs on a rabbit's fur; the eggs hatch into maggots that eat into the skin and flesh.

The PDSA describes it as "a painful, life-threatening condition caused by maggots burrowing under the skin and feeding on flesh". The eggs need only a few hours to hatch, and the PDSA says maggots can cause extensive damage in as little as one day. The RWAF says they can eat into a rabbit's flesh within 24 hours. Blowflies (bluebottles and greenbottles) are the most dangerous, according to the RWAF.

## What are the signs of flystrike?

Maggots or eggs in the fur, wet or missing fur around the bottom, a bad smell, and a rabbit that is quiet or not eating.

The PDSA lists:

- maggots in the fur or under the skin
- wet patches of fur and fur loss, often around the back end
- open wounds or sores with eggs or maggots in them
- low energy
- eating less
- a bad smell
- collapse

The RWAF notes that flystrike can also happen above the tail and elsewhere on the body, not only at the bottom, and that affected rabbits are usually in pain and in shock. Rabbits hide pain, so don't wait for obvious distress.

## Which rabbits get flystrike?

Any rabbit, but especially one with a dirty or wet bottom, a wound, or a reason it can't groom.

- **Season.** It is most common in warm summer months but, the RWAF says, can happen at any time of year.
- **A dirty bottom.** The RWAF says any rabbit with a dirty bottom is at very high risk. Causes include [diarrhoea or uneaten caecotrophs](/animals/rabbit-diarrhoea), urine problems, a poor diet, and dental pain, arthritis or obesity that stop a rabbit cleaning itself (PDSA, RWAF). See also [overgrown teeth in rabbits](/animals/rabbit-overgrown-teeth).
- **Wounds.** Flies are attracted to wounds (PDSA).
- **Dirty bedding.** Soiled litter and bedding attract flies and soil the fur (RWAF).
- **Long fur.** The PDSA advises trimming the fur around the rear of long-haired breeds such as Angoras and Lionheads, especially in summer.

## What will the vet do?

Assess your rabbit, then usually clip the fur, remove the maggots and treat pain, shock and infection.

According to the PDSA, if flystrike is treatable the vet clips the fur, cleans the skin and removes as many maggots as possible, often under anaesthetic, and gives pain relief and medication to kill any remaining maggots or eggs. A drip may be needed for shock or dehydration, and antibiotics for infection. The RWAF notes that anaesthesia carries more risk in a rabbit in shock.

## Can a rabbit survive flystrike?

Yes, if it is found early and treated quickly; severe cases often do not survive.

The PDSA says the sooner your rabbit is seen, the better its chance of survival, and the RWAF says rabbits can recover fully if it is caught and treated quickly. When maggots have already caused extensive damage, both say recovery is unlikely and the vet may recommend putting the rabbit to sleep to end its suffering.

## How do I prevent flystrike?

Check your rabbit's bottom every day, twice a day in warm weather, and keep it and its home clean and dry.

- **Check.** The PDSA advises a daily check for cleanliness, eggs and maggots, paying particular attention to the bottom. The RSPCA and the RWAF go further: in warm weather, check the fur and skin around the bottom and tail **at least twice a day**. We suggest following the stricter advice.
- **Clean and dry.** If there is urine or poo on the fur, clean and dry the area (RWAF), and find out why it is there.
- **Clean housing.** Remove urine, droppings, dirty litter and wet bedding at least once a day, especially in summer (PDSA).
- **Diet.** Feed mainly hay, with leafy greens and a small amount of quality nuggets, to keep droppings normal (PDSA).
- **Weight and exercise.** Overweight rabbits struggle to groom their bottom (PDSA, RWAF).
- **Health checks.** Have dental problems, arthritis and urine problems treated, because they stop rabbits cleaning themselves (PDSA, RWAF).
- **Fly-repellent products.** Ask your vet whether to use one in summer and how often. The RWAF stresses that these products are not a substitute for regular checks and prompt cleaning.

## When should I take my rabbit to the vet?

Immediately if you find maggots or fly eggs. Book an appointment the same day if your rabbit's bottom is repeatedly dirty or wet, even without maggots.

A dirty bottom is the main warning sign, so treat the cause before flies find it. The PDSA also says to contact your vet whenever you are worried, even if your rabbit has none of the listed signs. If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know about the flystrike?

What you found and where, when you last checked, and anything that could have made your rabbit's bottom dirty.

- what you saw (maggots, eggs, wounds, smell) and where on the body
- when you last checked your rabbit and found it clean
- whether you removed any maggots or wetted the fur
- whether your rabbit is eating, drinking and passing droppings
- any recent diarrhoea, soft droppings stuck to the fur, or urine soiling
- any known dental problems, arthritis, weight problems or wounds
- your rabbit's age, and whether it lives indoors or outdoors
- what fly-prevention product, if any, has been used, and when
- any medicines your rabbit takes
