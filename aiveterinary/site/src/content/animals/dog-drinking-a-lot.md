## First: go to a vet now if you see these signs

Drinking and weeing more is rarely an emergency on its own, but it can be. Go to a vet **straight away**, and call ahead, if your dog is drinking a lot and:

- is an **unspayed female** with **pus or blood coming from her vulva**, or is vomiting, has a swollen tummy, is low in energy, off her food or collapses. This can be **pyometra**, an infected womb, which the PDSA describes as an emergency. Signs usually appear four to eight weeks after a season. In a "closed" pyometra there may be little or no discharge, which makes it harder to spot and more dangerous
- is **weak, vomiting, off food or breathing with difficulty**. In a dog with diabetes, these can be signs of **diabetic ketoacidosis**, a medical emergency that needs immediate treatment and a stay in hospital
- already has diabetes and becomes weak, wobbly, has a seizure or collapses: dangerously low blood sugar is also an emergency
- collapses, is very flat or is getting worse quickly

Otherwise, if you think your dog is drinking or weeing more than normal, **book an appointment**. The PDSA advises always contacting your vet when you notice this.

## Should I take water away from a dog that drinks too much?

No: always leave fresh water out, because restricting it can cause dehydration and dangerous salt imbalances in the blood.

It's tempting, especially if your dog has started having accidents indoors. **Don't.** The PDSA says you should never restrict your dog's water to control the problem. Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center explains that until your dog has been checked, you should assume they really need that water: restricting it can cause dehydration and dangerous electrolyte problems.

In many common causes, the body is making too much urine first and the dog drinks more to keep up. Taking the water away doesn't stop the urine.

In the meantime, Cornell suggests keeping bowls clean and full, putting out extra bowls if you have more than one pet, and **taking your dog out more often**.

## How much water should a dog drink a day?

Most healthy dogs drink about 40 to 60 ml per kilogram of body weight a day, and more than 100 ml per kilogram is considered too much.

According to Cornell, that means a 10 kg dog normally drinks roughly 400 to 600 ml a day. Drinking more than about 1 litre a day would count as excessive thirst (polydipsia).

These are only a guide. Size, health, activity and diet all make a difference, and a dog can drink more for harmless, temporary reasons:

- more exercise than usual
- hot weather
- being a puppy
- a change of diet, especially to dry or saltier food (wet food contains more water)

More useful than any number is the question: **is your dog drinking more than they used to?**

## How can I tell if my dog is drinking and weeing more?

You're filling the bowl more often, your dog drinks from taps or puddles, asks to go out more, has accidents indoors or leaks urine while asleep.

Signs the PDSA and Cornell list include:

- the bowl needs filling more often, or is always empty
- drinking from taps, puddles or streams more than usual
- drinking so much they vomit
- weeing more often, or larger amounts
- stopping to wee more on walks
- weeing indoors after being house-trained
- asking to go out more often
- leaking urine while sleeping

## How do I measure how much my dog drinks?

Measure the water you put in the bowl, including top-ups, and after 24 hours subtract what's left.

Your vet may ask how much your dog drinks in **24 hours**, and the PDSA advises taking a **urine sample** to the appointment. Use a measuring jug, write down every top-up, and measure what's left after a day. Ask your practice how they'd like the urine sample collected.

## Why is my dog drinking so much?

Common causes include diabetes, kidney disease, Cushing's disease, pyometra, urinary infections, liver disease and some medicines.

There are many possible causes, and only your vet can tell them apart with an examination and tests. They include:

- **Diabetes mellitus.** Typical signs are increased thirst and urination with increased appetite and **weight loss**. It can come on gradually, so early signs are easy to miss. It mostly affects middle-aged dogs, more often females, and obesity raises the risk.
- **Kidney disease.**
- **Pyometra** in unspayed females. Spaying prevents it.
- **Cushing's disease** and other adrenal gland problems, including Addison's disease.
- **Urinary tract infections** and other bladder problems.
- **Liver disease.**
- **Leptospirosis**, a bacterial infection.
- **Some cancers**, such as lymphoma.
- **Some medicines**, such as steroids, some anti-seizure drugs and some heart drugs. If your dog is on treatment, **don't stop it yourself**. Talk to your vet.

Less often, thirst itself is the starting point, for example with pain, stress or heat, or rarely as a behavioural problem.

## Does weeing indoors always mean my dog is weeing more?

Not always: incontinence, incomplete house-training, submissive weeing, pain and dementia can look similar.

The PDSA lists things that are easy to confuse with weeing more:

- **urinary incontinence**: leaking or dribbling, wet patches on the bed
- **incomplete toilet training** in puppies
- **submissive urination**, when a dog is scared, anxious or greeting people. Punishment won't help
- **pain** that makes it hard to go outside or squat
- **dementia** in older dogs

## Can I give my dog painkillers while we wait?

No: never give your dog human painkillers, especially as kidney disease is a common cause of extra thirst.

Ibuprofen can cause stomach ulcers, bleeding and kidney damage in dogs, and paracetamol can damage a dog's liver. Only give medicines your vet has prescribed.

## What will the vet do?

Examine your dog, ask about diet and medicines, and usually test urine and blood, sometimes with X-rays or ultrasound.

Initial tests usually include a **urine test**, a **complete blood count** and a **biochemistry profile**, sometimes with X-rays or ultrasound. Treatment depends entirely on the cause.

## What will the vet want to know?

When the extra drinking started, how much your dog drinks in 24 hours, appetite and weight, her last season if unspayed, and any medicines.

- when you first noticed it, and whether it came on suddenly or gradually
- how much your dog drinks in 24 hours, if you've measured it
- changes in appetite or weight, and any vomiting
- for unspayed females, when her last season was
- all medicines, including flea and worm treatments and supplements
