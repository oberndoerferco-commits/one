## First: see your vet today if you notice these signs

Phone your vet and get your dog seen **today** if your dog:

- suddenly holds their head tilted, is wobbly, walks in circles or falls over
- has uncontrolled, flicking eye movements
- has a drooping lip on one side of the face, or can't close one eye
- starts shaking their head hard after a walk through long grass or fields and won't let you touch one ear (a grass seed may be stuck)
- yelps in pain when you touch the ear, or scratches it until it bleeds
- has a soft, puffy swelling on the ear flap

A head tilt, loss of balance and a drooping face can mean the infection has spread to the middle or inner ear. The PDSA says a head tilt or loss of balance needs a **same-day appointment**, and the MSD Veterinary Manual says early treatment improves the chance of a good outcome. Out of hours, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What is an ear infection in dogs, and how do I spot it?

Usually it's inflammation of the outer ear canal, showing as head shaking, scratching, redness, discharge, a bad smell and a sore ear.

When people talk about an ear infection in a dog, they usually mean inflammation of the outer ear canal, called **otitis externa**. The MSD Veterinary Manual says it's the most common ear problem in dogs. One or both ears can be affected, and signs can start suddenly or go on for a long time.

Typical signs, from the MSD Veterinary Manual and the PDSA:

- frequent head shaking and scratching at the ear
- rubbing the face along the floor or furniture
- red, swollen or scaly skin inside the ear
- discharge
- a bad smell
- pain or itching when the ear is touched

## Why does my dog get ear infections?

Common triggers include allergies, ear mites, grass seeds or other objects in the canal, bacteria and yeast, and wax.

An ear infection almost always has an underlying cause, and that's what needs finding. Possible causes include:

- **allergies**, especially skin allergies
- **parasites**, such as ear mites
- **something stuck in the ear canal**, such as a grass seed
- **bacteria and yeast**, which multiply in an inflamed canal
- **wax**

Some dogs are more prone because of the shape of their ear flaps or canals. Water in the ears after swimming or bathing can also lead to infection.

Worth knowing: the MSD Veterinary Manual says that unless every cause is treated, the infection often comes back.

## What can I do at home for my dog's ear infection?

Leave the ear alone, don't clean it yourself, and don't use home remedies, old ear drops or human medicines.

Until your appointment:

- **Leave the ear alone.** Have a look, but don't clean inside the canal yourself.
- **No cotton buds.** The MSD Veterinary Manual says they shouldn't be used for cleaning, and the PDSA advises against pushing anything into the ear.
- **No home remedies** such as vinegar mixtures. The MSD Veterinary Manual says they can make inflammation worse.
- **No over-the-counter ear cleaners or wax removers** unless your vet has approved them.
- **No leftover ear drops** from a previous problem or another pet. Until your vet has examined the ear, nobody knows what's causing it.
- **No human medicines**, including painkillers. Your vet decides whether your dog needs pain relief and which. See our guide to [human painkillers and pets](/animals/nsaid-painkillers-dogs-cats).
- Stop your dog scratching the ear until it bleeds. A cone from your vet can help.

## When should I take my dog to the vet with an ear infection?

As soon as you suspect one: ear infections are painful and don't get better on their own.

Even without the warning signs above, the PDSA advises booking an appointment as soon as you suspect an ear infection. Go sooner if:

- your dog is in obvious pain
- discharge, smell or swelling are getting worse quickly
- your dog keeps getting ear infections (tell your vet if they've had several)
- your dog seems to be hearing less well

## How is a dog ear infection treated?

Your vet examines the ear canal, usually checks a sample of discharge, cleans the ear and prescribes drops or tablets that you give until the end of the course.

Your vet will look down the ear canal with an otoscope. If the ear is very painful, this may need sedation or an anaesthetic. They will usually look at a sample of the discharge under a microscope, and sometimes send it to a lab. For long-running cases, extra tests such as allergy tests or x-rays may be needed.

According to the MSD Veterinary Manual, the vet cleans and dries the ear before applying medicine, sometimes under anaesthetic. This deep cleaning belongs in the clinic. Your vet decides which drops or tablets your dog needs and for how long. **Finish the full course**, even if the ear looks better after a few days, as the PDSA advises, and go to the check-ups. Long-running cases can take months, and some dogs need lifelong care. The PDSA notes that severe, long-standing infections may need surgery.

## What is an ear haematoma in dogs?

A soft, fluid-filled swelling on the ear flap that forms when constant shaking or scratching bursts a blood vessel.

If a dog keeps shaking or scratching, a blood vessel in the ear flap can burst. The MSD Veterinary Manual describes the result, an **aural haematoma**, as a soft, fluid-filled swelling "like a water balloon". It's usually treated with surgery, and treating the cause behind it, often an ear infection, is essential.

## How can I prevent ear infections in my dog?

Check the ears regularly, keep them dry after swimming, and ask your vet whether and how to clean them.

- Check your dog's ears regularly for redness, wetness or a change in smell.
- Dry the ears after swimming or bathing.
- After walks in long grass, check your dog for grass seeds, as the PDSA suggests.
- If your dog is prone to ear infections, ask your vet whether you should clean the ears regularly, how, and with what.
- Only pluck or trim hair in the ear canal after talking to your vet.

## What will the vet want to know?

How long and which ear, past ear infections and treatments, anything you've put in the ear, itching elsewhere, swimming or long grass, and diet.

- how long it's been going on, and which ear
- whether your dog has had ear infections before, and what was used then
- whether you've put anything in the ear yourself
- itching elsewhere, such as paws or tummy (a possible sign of allergy)
- swimming, bathing or walks through long grass
- their food and any recent changes
