## First, the signs that mean go now

In ferrets, sudden weakness, wobbly back legs or collapse can mean dangerously low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia). The usual cause is an **insulinoma**, a tumour of the pancreas that releases too much insulin. The MSD Veterinary Manual says ferrets with signs of low blood sugar "should be seen by a veterinarian immediately".

Go to a vet that treats ferrets **now**, and phone ahead, if your ferret:

- has **collapsed**, is unresponsive, or is very hard to wake
- is having a **seizure** (fit)
- is **staggering**, dragging its back legs, or can't stand
- is **drooling**, pawing at its mouth, or staring into space with a glazed look
- is extremely lethargic
- has white or bluish gums, or is struggling to breathe
- has collapsed in **hot weather** or is breathing with its mouth open, which can be heatstroke

Don't wait to see whether it passes. The MSD Veterinary Manual's chapter for vets notes that the signs often settle once the ferret has eaten something, so an episode is easy to dismiss. MSD also warns that ferrets may hide illness until it is advanced, so a sick ferret "can rapidly become critically ill".

Not every practice treats ferrets. MSD advises finding, before you need it, an out-of-hours emergency clinic that treats ferrets, in case your ferret becomes ill at night. See also our [emergency page](/emergency).

## What can I do on the way to the vet?

Only what the MSD Veterinary Manual describes: a weak ferret may be offered a little honey or corn syrup, nothing goes in its mouth during a seizure, and you keep going to the vet.

The MSD Veterinary Manual's pages for pet owners say: "If your ferret shows signs of hypoglycemia (weakness or collapse), you can try offering a little honey or corn syrup. For safety, do not put anything in the mouth of a ferret having a seizure. Ferrets with signs of hypoglycemia should be seen by a veterinarian immediately."

Beyond that:

- **Keep going, even if your ferret perks up.** A ferret that seems better after eating still needs the vet.
- **Use a secure carrier.** MSD says a plastic cat carrier is enough for the journey.
- **Don't give any medicine of your own.** The RSPCA advises giving ferrets only medicines a vet has recommended for them, and warns that medicines prescribed for other animals can be very dangerous to them.
- **If it may be heatstroke,** start cooling straight away and then go to the vet. PDSA's first aid for heatstroke, which it says can affect any animal that gets too hot, is to move the animal somewhere cool or into the shade, gently wet its body with water from the cold tap, keeping water away from the nose and mouth and taking care not to stress small animals that aren't used to water, and create a breeze. Don't cover it with a wet towel, which PDSA says makes it hotter. LafeberVet warns vets not to put exotic pets such as rabbits and chinchillas into cold water or ice baths.
- **Note what happened:** when it started, how long it lasted, and when your ferret last ate. A short video can help the vet.

## What is an insulinoma in a ferret?

A tumour of the pancreas that makes too much insulin and drives the blood sugar down; it is very common in middle-aged and older ferrets.

The MSD Veterinary Manual describes insulinomas as very common in ferrets older than 3 years. A 2002 review by vets at the University of Zurich describes them as common in middle-aged ferrets of either sex. The tumour makes more insulin than the body needs, and the extra insulin pushes the blood sugar too low; MSD calls this "the opposite of diabetes". The low blood sugar is what causes the signs: weakness, wobbliness, a blank stare and, at worst, seizures.

## What are the signs of low blood sugar in a ferret?

Weakness, tiredness, weak or wobbly back legs, drooling, pawing at the mouth and staring into space; in severe cases, seizures and collapse. The signs often come and go.

The MSD Veterinary Manual lists:

- weakness and lethargy
- weak back legs, or partial paralysis of the back legs
- drooling, and clenching or grinding the teeth
- pawing at the mouth and signs of nausea
- "stargazing" (staring into space) or a glazed look in the eyes
- weight loss
- in severe cases, collapse, seizures and coma

The episodes are easy to miss. The 2002 Zurich review describes signs ranging from episodes of tiredness to coma, and MSD's chapter for vets says the signs often settle after the ferret has eaten.

## Why is my ferret trembling or shaking?

Trembling on its own doesn't tell you the cause, so look for other signs: together with weakness, drooling or a blank stare, it needs a vet now.

If your ferret is trembling and also weak, wobbly, drooling, pawing at its mouth or staring blankly, those are signs of low blood sugar described by the MSD Veterinary Manual: go to the vet now. If it is trembling but otherwise its usual self, phone your vet for advice today: MSD's guide lists a sudden change in behaviour among the signs to see a vet about within 24 hours.

## What else can make a ferret weak or wobbly?

Heart disease, falls and injuries, heatstroke and anaemia can all cause similar signs, which is why a vet check is always needed.

The MSD Veterinary Manual says many problems in ferrets can weaken the back legs, and that a vet should be consulted any time this is seen. Besides an insulinoma, your vet will think about:

- **Heart disease (dilated cardiomyopathy):** MSD says it usually occurs in ferrets over 4 years old and that its signs can look like an insulinoma, so the vet should test for both. MSD lists lethargy, weakness and trouble breathing among its signs.
- **A fall or injury:** MSD notes that poor eyesight and curiosity make ferrets prone to falls, and that limping, bent limbs or trouble coordinating the back legs may mean a broken bone or another problem.
- **Heatstroke:** the MSD Veterinary Manual says ferrets are sensitive to heat and humidity because they have no sweat glands, and that they easily overheat.
- **Anaemia in an unspayed female who stays in season:** the MSD Veterinary Manual explains that very high oestrogen levels can suppress the bone marrow. See [female ferret in heat](/animals/female-ferret-in-heat).

## Can an insulinoma in a ferret be treated?

Yes, with medication, surgery or both, but the MSD Veterinary Manual says there is no cure, and medical treatment is lifelong.

The diagnosis is made with a blood test that shows low blood sugar. MSD says you will be asked to withhold food for about 4 hours before the appointment; do this only when your practice tells you to, and exactly as they say.

MSD explains that surgery removes the tumour or the affected part of the pancreas, but because some insulinomas are many tiny tumours, surgery may not remove them all, and most ferrets still need medication afterwards. Medical treatment is lifelong and controls the effects of the tumour without shrinking it, and ferrets with an insulinoma need blood sugar checks every few months and after any change in their medication. Which treatment suits your ferret is a decision for you and your vet.

## How should I feed a ferret with an insulinoma?

Make sure it has food available and eats throughout the day, and follow your vet's advice on diet.

- The MSD Veterinary Manual's chapter for vets says any ferret with an insulinoma should have food freely available, and owners must make sure it is eating throughout the day.
- The honey or corn syrup in MSD's advice is for a weak ferret on the way to the vet, not for everyday feeding.
- A ferret with an insulinoma that stops eating needs a call to the vet the same day. See [my ferret won't eat or is throwing up](/animals/ferret-not-eating-vomiting).

## When should I take my ferret to the vet?

Now for collapse, seizures, staggering or a ferret you can't wake properly; within 24 hours if it is sleeping more, playing less, drooling or behaving differently.

The MSD Veterinary Manual's guide to ferret emergencies says to see a vet:

- **immediately** for seizures, staggering or other problems walking, extreme lethargy, white or bluish gums, or difficulty breathing
- **within 24 hours** for sleeping more than usual and not wanting to play, drooling, a sudden change in behaviour, sudden weight gain or loss, or loss of appetite with no other signs

MSD recommends a vet visit whenever a ferret seems lethargic or its behaviour suddenly changes.

## What will the vet want to know about my ferret?

What you saw and for how long, when your ferret last ate and whether eating helped, its age and history, its diet, and anything you gave on the way.

- What happened, when it started and how long it lasted; a video if you have one
- When your ferret last ate, and whether it seemed better afterwards
- Any earlier episodes: hard to wake, staring, drooling, pawing at the mouth, wobbly back legs
- Age, sex, and whether it is neutered or has a hormone implant
- Diet, treats and any recent change of food
- Whether you gave honey or syrup on the way, and when
- Previous illnesses, such as adrenal disease or heart problems, and all medicines
- Recent heat, a fall, or anything it could have chewed or swallowed
