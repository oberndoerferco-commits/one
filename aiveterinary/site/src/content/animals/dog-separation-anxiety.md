## First: see your vet soon if any of these apply

Separation anxiety is not usually an emergency, but book a vet appointment **promptly** if:

- your dog **hurts themselves** when left alone, for example licking or biting themselves until they have wounds, or getting injured trying to escape from a crate, door or window
- the behaviour has **changed suddenly** for no obvious reason. The PDSA advises contacting your vet if you notice a sudden change in your dog's behaviour
- an **older dog** has recently started having accidents indoors, wandering or seeming confused
- you notice other signs as well, such as repeated **vomiting**, diarrhoea, extra thirst, pain, limping or weight loss

The reason is simple. The RSPCA explains that **health problems can cause or worsen** separation-related behaviour, and the MSD Veterinary Manual says medical causes must be ruled out first. House soiling, for instance, can be caused by **pain, failing senses or cognitive dysfunction** (a kind of dementia in older dogs) rather than anxiety.

## What is separation anxiety in dogs?

It's when a dog panics at being left alone, although not every dog that struggles alone is actually anxious.

The MSD Veterinary Manual describes it as a condition in which a dog **panics when left alone**. The PDSA explains that vets now often talk about **"separation-related problems"**, because some dogs aren't anxious but scared, frustrated, bored or over-excited. Working out which applies to your dog changes how you help them.

## What are the signs of separation anxiety?

When alone, your dog may bark or whine, chew and destroy things, wee or poo indoors, drool, pant, pace or ignore their food and toys.

According to the PDSA, the RSPCA and the MSD Veterinary Manual, when your dog is alone (or away from their favourite person) they may:

- **bark, howl or whine**
- **chew or destroy** things, scratch or dig, often near **doors and windows**
- **wee or poo indoors**, even though they are house-trained
- **drool** heavily, **pant** or tremble
- pace, circle or be unable to settle
- **vomit**
- **ignore** food, treats or toys left for them
- lick or bite themselves excessively

Some signs start **before you leave**, as you pick up your keys or put on your shoes. The MSD Veterinary Manual notes that signs are often worst **in the first 15 to 30 minutes** after you go.

Watch for quiet dogs too. The RSPCA warns that many dogs show no obvious signs, so the problem goes unnoticed, and the PDSA notes that a dog who seems **flat or low** may also be struggling.

## How can I find out what my dog does when I'm out?

Film your dog with a camera or phone while you're out, and show the footage to your vet.

Both the PDSA and the RSPCA suggest a camera, and the MSD Veterinary Manual calls video an invaluable tool for telling separation problems apart from other issues such as noise fear or incomplete house-training.

## Why does my dog have separation anxiety?

Possible causes include a bad experience while alone, never learning to be alone as a puppy, strong attachment to one person, insecurity, boredom and fear of noises.

The PDSA lists:

- **bad experiences** while alone, such as a fright or an injury
- not being **taught as a puppy** to spend time alone, bit by bit
- **over-attachment** to one person
- **insecurity**, for example after time in kennels or a rescue centre, or several moves
- a home with **too little to do**
- **noise phobias**, such as thunder or fireworks, happening while your dog is alone. The MSD Veterinary Manual notes that noise phobia and separation problems often occur together

## Should I punish my dog for damage while I was out?

No: telling your dog off or showing you're angry can frighten them and make things worse.

This is the most important rule. The RSPCA says raising your voice may scare your dog and make the situation worse. A dog who "looks guilty" when you get home is reacting to your anger now; they don't link it to something they did hours earlier. The MSD Veterinary Manual warns that methods causing fear can lead to lasting fearfulness and anxiety, and even to **fear of the owner**.

## What can I do at home to help?

Leave your dog alone as little as possible, practise very short absences, keep comings and goings calm and give them a safe place to settle.

These steps from the PDSA, RSPCA and MSD Veterinary Manual help many dogs, but in serious cases they don't replace a plan made with a professional.

- **Leave your dog alone as little as possible** while you work on the problem. The MSD Veterinary Manual explains that each time they're left, a dog can become more sensitive, making treatment harder. A dog sitter, friend or day care can help. The PDSA and RSPCA advise that dogs generally **shouldn't be left alone for more than 4 hours**, and puppies for much less.
- **Train in tiny steps.** The PDSA suggests starting by walking a short distance away and rewarding calm behaviour, then leaving your dog in a room for **a few seconds**, and building up very gradually. The MSD Veterinary Manual stresses that practice absences must be **short enough not to trigger anxiety**, and that this can take months.
- **Keep departures and returns low-key**, without big greetings.
- **Create a safe space** where your dog can relax, perhaps with an item of your clothing.
- **Before you go out**, the RSPCA suggests a walk, making sure your dog isn't hungry, and leaving a long-lasting chew or food toy. If your dog doesn't touch it while you're away, that's a sign they're worried. The PDSA cautions that treats and toys alone only help in a small number of cases.

Before using **pheromone diffusers or collars**, the PDSA advises talking to your vet.

## When should I get professional help?

If you see no improvement after a few weeks, or your dog is very distressed, see your vet first and then a behaviour professional they refer you to.

The RSPCA recommends this route: **your vet first**, to rule out health problems, then a referral to a **clinical animal behaviourist**. Across Europe you can ask your vet to refer you to a veterinary behaviourist or qualified behaviourist; in the UK, the PDSA points to behaviourists certified by the ABTC (Animal Behaviour and Training Council).

According to the PDSA, a treatment plan usually includes:

- an **assessment** of your dog, often using video
- **behavioural therapy**: desensitisation (gradually getting used to being alone) and counterconditioning (linking being alone with something pleasant)
- **changes at home** and predictable **routines**
- **medication**, which the PDSA says is often needed alongside behavioural therapy and is usually stopped once your dog copes alone

Anxiety medicines for dogs are prescription-only, and your vet chooses the product and dose. **Never give your dog human tranquillisers, sleeping tablets or calming medicines**: they can be dangerous and don't solve the problem.

## How can I prevent separation anxiety in a puppy?

Teach independence early, with short spells alone that you lengthen gradually, rewarding calm behaviour.

The PDSA also suggests low-key departures and returns and a safe den. With patience, most dogs improve, and the first step is understanding what your dog is feeling, with your vet's help.
