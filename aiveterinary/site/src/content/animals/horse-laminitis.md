## First, the signs that mean go now

World Horse Welfare calls laminitis **a veterinary emergency**, and the MSD Veterinary Manual explains why: in acute laminitis the coffin bone (pedal bone) inside the hoof can move out of place quickly. Redwings, the horse charity, says that even if you only suspect laminitis you should call a vet, because it does not get better on its own.

Call your vet now if your horse, pony or donkey:

- is reluctant to walk forward or to move at all
- finds it hard to make a tight turn
- walks short, stiff or stilted, or is lame, especially on hard ground
- keeps shifting its weight from foot to foot when standing, or tries to take the weight off some feet
- has a strong, bounding pulse at the back of the fetlock (the digital pulse)
- has hot feet, especially around the coronary band at the top of the hoof
- looks dull, goes off its food or stands reluctantly
- has a pained expression on its face

These signs come from World Horse Welfare, Redwings and the MSD Veterinary Manual. They can be subtle: in a British study of horses and ponies whose owners reported laminitis (Pollard and colleagues, Equine Veterinary Journal, 2019), the most common signs, seen in at least 70% of episodes, were **difficulty turning and a short, stilted or lame walk**.

## What is laminitis?

Inflammation of the laminae, the tissue that holds the pedal bone inside the hoof. It can be a sudden attack or a long-term problem.

The MSD Veterinary Manual explains that the word means "inflammation of the laminae". It can be a short, sudden (acute) inflammation, or the long-term disease left by repeated attacks, and it can affect one foot, all four, or only the hind feet. World Horse Welfare describes how the tissue that holds the pedal bone inside the hoof weakens, so the bone becomes unstable, with inflammation, pain and lameness. It can affect any horse, pony, donkey or mule.

World Horse Welfare also stresses that laminitis is not a disease in itself but a clinical sign, which is why the vet will look for what caused it.

## Why is laminitis an emergency?

Because the damage inside the hoof can progress quickly and does not heal by itself.

- The MSD Veterinary Manual calls acute laminitis a medical emergency because the coffin bone can be displaced quickly.
- Redwings describes laminitis as very painful, and says the damage is progressive, worsens over time and does not resolve without veterinary and farriery treatment.
- The MSD Veterinary Manual says that even with prompt treatment the outlook for recovery is guarded.

## What causes laminitis?

Most often an underlying hormonal disease, such as equine metabolic syndrome or PPID (Cushing's). Too much grain or rich grass, severe illness, and overloading one foot can also trigger it.

- **Hormonal (endocrine) disease.** Redwings says the majority of cases are caused by an underlying disease of the hormone system, and World Horse Welfare names the two main ones: equine metabolic syndrome (EMS) and pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction (PPID, also called equine Cushing's disease).
- **Too much grain or rich grass.** The MSD Veterinary Manual lists eating too much grain and grazing lush pasture, especially in ponies or overweight horses, among the most common causes. World Horse Welfare mentions gorging on spring grass.
- **Severe illness elsewhere in the body**, such as a retained placenta after foaling or complications after surgery (World Horse Welfare, MSD Veterinary Manual).
- **Overloading one foot**, when pain or injury stops a horse using the leg on the other side (MSD Veterinary Manual, World Horse Welfare).
- **Hoof balance and workload** can also damage the laminae, according to Redwings.

World Horse Welfare names recent weight gain, a previous episode of laminitis and foot soreness after shoeing or trimming as key risk factors. And there is no safe season: the study by Pollard and colleagues concluded that laminitis remains a considerable year-round welfare problem in horses and ponies.

## What should I do while I wait for the vet?

Keep your horse still, don't give any medicine yourself, and get the passport and the facts ready.

- **Don't ride, lunge or exercise your horse**, and don't walk it to "see how it goes". World Horse Welfare lists restricted movement as part of the first stage of treatment. Ask your vet on the phone how to keep your horse until they arrive.
- **Don't give painkillers or other medicines** unless your vet tells you to. Pain relief is part of the vet's treatment, and which medicines your vet may use also depends on your horse's passport (see below).
- **Note when you first noticed the signs**, and any recent change in grazing, feed, weight, work, shoeing or trimming.
- **Have your horse's passport ready.** In the EU, horses are deemed to be intended for the food chain unless their passport irreversibly excludes them (Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/963, Article 38). The vet must check this status before treating (Article 39), and it decides which rules on medicines apply (Regulation (EU) 2019/6, Article 112(4)).

## Can laminitis be cured?

There is no quick fix: treatment needs the vet and the farrier, then long-term management, and the outlook is guarded.

World Horse Welfare describes treatment in three stages: first restricted movement, pain relief and support for the feet; then therapeutic trimming or shoeing, together with treatment of any hormonal disorder; then long-term management of diet, exercise and foot care. Redwings says a horse that has had laminitis needs careful management to avoid future episodes. The MSD Veterinary Manual notes that horses with chronic laminitis are usually extremely lame and may spend a lot of time lying down.

## How can I lower the risk of laminitis?

Keep your horse at a healthy weight, manage rich grazing all year round, keep feed out of reach, and keep up regular, skilled foot care.

- **Healthy weight.** Redwings says most cases can be prevented through careful weight management to prevent obesity. World Horse Welfare advises avoiding unintentional weight gain and checking weight regularly with a weight tape or by fat scoring.
- **Careful access to rich, sugary grazing throughout the year** (Redwings).
- **No access to the feed store**, since too much grain is one of the most common causes (MSD Veterinary Manual).
- **Routine, skilled trimming and farriery**, which Redwings says removes the risk of laminitis caused by poor hoof balance.
- **Hormonal disease under control.** Because most laminitis has a hormonal cause, ask your vet whether your horse should be checked for EMS or PPID, especially if it has had laminitis before.

## When should I call the vet about laminitis?

As soon as you suspect it, even if the signs seem mild. Also call for advice after a known trigger, before any signs appear.

- Redwings and World Horse Welfare both say: if you suspect laminitis, call your vet first.
- Call about **any new lameness, stiffness or difficulty turning**. These were the signs owners reported most often in the study by Pollard and colleagues.
- Call for advice if your horse has **got into the feed store and eaten a lot of grain**, if a mare **has not passed the placenta after foaling**, or if your horse **cannot bear weight on one leg**, since these are recognised triggers (MSD Veterinary Manual, World Horse Welfare).

Feeling the digital pulse and checking the hooves for heat is easier if you know what is normal for your horse: see [horse vital signs](/animals/horse-vital-signs).

## What will the vet want to know about my horse's laminitis?

When the signs started, which feet seem sore, grazing and feed, weight, earlier episodes, recent hoof care, any hormonal disease or recent illness, and any medicine given.

- when you first noticed the signs, and which feet seem affected
- what your horse grazes and eats, any recent change, and any chance it got into the feed store
- whether it has put on weight recently
- whether it has had laminitis before
- when it was last trimmed or shod, and whether it was footsore afterwards
- whether it has been diagnosed with EMS or PPID (Cushing's)
- any recent illness, injury to another leg, or, in a mare, a recent foaling
- any medicine it has had, and its passport with its food-chain status
- who your farrier is, as the vet and the farrier often work together on treatment
