## First: never your own painkillers

If your dog or cat is in pain, the medicine cabinet is the wrong place to look.

- **Paracetamol (acetaminophen)** is poisonous to cats at any dose. Cats lack the liver enzyme that breaks it down; the red blood cells lose the ability to carry oxygen, the gums turn muddy brown, the face may swell, and the liver fails. In dogs paracetamol has a narrow margin and also damages the liver.
- **Ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and similar** cause stomach ulcers, bleeding and kidney failure in both species. Cats are affected by roughly half the dose that harms a dog, and one human tablet can be enough for a small dog.
- **Aspirin** is broken down ten times more slowly by cats than by people and is not a home remedy.

If your pet has already swallowed any of these, call a clinic now, with the packaging in your hand. Do not wait for signs and do not try to make the animal vomit at home.

## What are veterinary NSAIDs and what do they treat?

They are licensed, species-specific medicines that reduce pain, inflammation and fever, and are the most common painkillers vets prescribe.

NSAID stands for non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug. The veterinary versions are licensed, species-specific medicines that reduce pain, inflammation and fever by blocking the enzymes that make inflammatory messengers. They are the most common painkillers vets prescribe, for:

- osteoarthritis and other chronic joint pain, in dogs and increasingly in cats
- pain after surgery, including neutering, usually for a few days
- injuries, sprains and soft tissue inflammation
- dental pain, some bladder conditions in cats, and fever

They come as tablets, flavoured chews, liquids measured with a syringe, and injections given at the clinic. The prescription is calculated for your animal's weight and health; a product for dogs is not automatically safe for cats, and vice versa.

## Does my pet need tests before starting an NSAID?

Often yes: for an older animal or long treatment, expect a blood and urine check first, because NSAIDs are cleared by the kidneys and liver.

NSAIDs are cleared by the kidneys and liver and can irritate the gut, so your vet will want to know that those organs are healthy. For an older animal or for treatment that will go on for weeks, expect a blood and urine check first and periodic rechecks. Tell your vet about every other medicine or supplement your pet takes, any past stomach upsets, and any kidney, liver or heart disease.

## How should I give my pet an NSAID?

With food, with water available, exactly as prescribed and at the lowest effective dose your vet sets.

- **With food.** Give the dose with or straight after a meal. Food protects the stomach lining and is the single most useful thing you can do to prevent side effects. If your pet is not eating, do not give the tablet; call the clinic.
- **With water available.** A dehydrated animal is at much higher risk of kidney damage. In hot weather, after vomiting or diarrhoea, or in a cat that drinks little, pause and ask.
- **Exactly as prescribed.** Once a day means once a day. Never give a second dose because the first "did not seem to work", and never give a dose early.
- **Lowest effective dose.** For chronic pain, your vet will often reduce the dose over time to the smallest amount that keeps your pet comfortable, sometimes with pain-free days built in.
- **Missed dose?** Ask your vet what to do with this particular product. Do not double up.

## What NSAID side effects mean I should stop and call the vet?

Vomiting, diarrhoea or black stools, loss of appetite, drinking more, tiredness, yellow gums or new skin problems mean stop the NSAID and call your vet the same day.

Stop the NSAID and phone your vet the same day if you notice any of the following. Most problems reverse fully when the drug is stopped early.

- vomiting, especially more than once or with blood
- diarrhoea, or **black, tarry stools** (digested blood)
- loss of appetite, drooling, or lying with a hunched back
- drinking or urinating noticeably more
- unusual tiredness or weakness
- yellow tinge to the gums, eyes or skin
- skin redness, itching or scabs that appeared after the medicine started

Go immediately, calling ahead, if your pet collapses, has pale gums, or vomits blood.

## Can I give an NSAID with other medicines?

Never two NSAIDs at once, never an NSAID with a steroid, and never a human painkiller on top, unless your vet has planned it.

- **Never two NSAIDs at once**, for example your dog's arthritis tablet plus a product left over from another animal or bought online.
- **Never an NSAID together with a steroid** (cortisone-type tablets, drops or injections are common for allergies and itching). The combination multiplies the risk of a bleeding stomach ulcer. If your pet is switched from one to the other, your vet will leave a gap of several days.
- **Never add a human painkiller "on top"** because the pain still seems bad. Tell your vet instead; there are other classes of painkiller that can be combined safely, and the plan can be changed.

## Is it safe for my pet to take NSAIDs long term?

Many arthritic dogs and cats take an NSAID for years, with food at every dose, water available, watching appetite and stools, and regular blood tests.

Many arthritic dogs and cats take an NSAID for years and are transformed by it: they climb stairs, jump on the sofa and play again. The price is a routine: food with every dose, water everywhere, a watchful eye on appetite and stools, and blood tests at the interval your vet sets. Keep a note of the day's dose so no one in the household gives it twice, and store the medicine out of reach, because flavoured chews are attractive and an overdose is serious.

Whether an NSAID is the right painkiller for your animal, at what dose and for how long, is a decision for the vet who has examined it; take any of the signs above, or any doubt, to your own vet rather than adjusting the treatment yourself.
