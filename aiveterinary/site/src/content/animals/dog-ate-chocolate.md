## First: call your vet now, before any signs appear

If your dog has eaten chocolate, **call your vet or an emergency vet now**, even if your dog seems completely normal. Signs of chocolate poisoning usually appear 6 to 12 hours after eating it, and by then much of the toxin has been absorbed. The best time for your vet to act is while your dog still looks well.

Go straight to the nearest clinic, and ring on the way, if your dog already has:

- repeated vomiting or diarrhoea
- restlessness, agitation or heavy panting
- trembling, stiff muscles or a seizure
- a very fast or irregular heartbeat
- collapse

Have these four things ready when you call: **what kind of chocolate** (keep the wrapper), **roughly how much** is missing, **when** it happened, and **your dog's weight**. If you can't reach your own practice, call an emergency vet. In the UK, the Animal PoisonLine also gives advice to owners.

## Why is chocolate poisonous to dogs?

Chocolate contains theobromine and caffeine, which dogs clear slowly and which overstimulate the heart, muscles and nervous system.

Cocoa contains **theobromine** and, in smaller amounts, **caffeine**. Dogs break theobromine down far more slowly than people do, so it stays in their body for a long time. It acts on the heart, the muscles and the nervous system. That is why the signs include restlessness, a racing heart, tremors and, in severe cases, an abnormal heart rhythm and seizures.

## Is dark chocolate worse than milk chocolate for dogs?

Yes: the darker the chocolate, the more theobromine it contains, and cocoa powder is the most concentrated of all.

From most to least dangerous:

- **Cocoa powder** and **unsweetened baking chocolate**: the highest concentration. Small amounts matter, even for large dogs.
- **Dark chocolate**: much more than milk chocolate, and the higher the cocoa percentage, the more it contains.
- **Milk chocolate**: much less, but enough of it can still poison a dog, especially a small one.
- **White chocolate**: almost no theobromine. Its fat and sugar can still upset the stomach and, in some dogs, trigger **pancreatitis**.

Think about what else was in it, too. Chocolate cakes, biscuits and selection boxes can contain raisins, nuts or the sweetener xylitol, and foil or wrappers can be swallowed. Tell your vet about all of it.

Cocoa shell **garden mulch** is a less obvious source of the same toxin.

## How much chocolate is dangerous for a dog?

It depends on the type of chocolate, how much was eaten and how much your dog weighs, and your vet should do that calculation.

The same piece that gives a big Labrador a mild stomach upset can be dangerous for a 4 kg terrier. Our [chocolate dose check](/chocolate) shows you the working, using figures from the MSD Veterinary Manual, but it never tells you your dog is safe. Cocoa content varies between products, owners often underestimate how much has gone, and elderly dogs, puppies and dogs with heart problems may cope less well. Use it to prepare for the call, not instead of it.

## What should I do straight away if my dog ate chocolate?

Move your dog away from what's left, keep the wrapper, call your vet with your dog's weight, and don't make your dog sick at home.

1. **Take away any remaining chocolate** and put everything else out of reach.
2. **Find the packaging** or work out what it was: type, cocoa percentage if shown, the size of the bar and how much is missing.
3. **Call your vet** or the emergency vet, with your dog's weight to hand.
4. **Go in if your vet tells you to.** Take the packaging with you and put your dog on a lead, even if they still seem lively.

## Should I make my dog sick at home?

No: don't try to make your dog sick yourself, because your vet can do it safely.

- **Don't use salt, salt water, washing-up liquid or other home remedies.** Large amounts of salt can cause dangerously high salt levels in the blood, which can lead to wobbliness, tremors, seizures or coma.
- **Don't give hydrogen peroxide** unless a vet has specifically told you to. Leave it to your vet.
- **Don't give milk, oil or other foods** to "neutralise" the chocolate. They don't help.
- **Don't give human medicines**, including painkillers or sedatives.
- **Don't wait to see what happens.** Treatment is simplest before signs appear.

If your dog gets to the clinic soon after eating the chocolate (the MSD Veterinary Manual describes making a dog sick when it is within about two hours), your vet can give an injection to make your dog vomit. Depending on the amount, they may also give activated charcoal to reduce absorption, put your dog on a fluid drip, control tremors, and monitor the heart.

## What are the signs of chocolate poisoning in dogs?

Restlessness, vomiting, diarrhoea, extra thirst and weeing, a racing heart and tremors, usually within 6 to 12 hours, and in severe cases seizures or collapse.

- restlessness, hyperactivity, panting
- vomiting, diarrhoea, a swollen or painful tummy
- drinking and weeing more than usual
- trembling, twitching, stiff muscles, wobbliness
- a very fast heartbeat
- in severe cases, seizures, collapse and coma

Because theobromine leaves the body so slowly, signs can last up to 72 hours in severe cases. A dog that is "just a bit restless" in the evening can get much worse overnight.

## How do I watch my dog at home if the vet says it's OK?

Keep your dog calm with water available, check on them through the night, and call back at the first new sign.

Your vet may decide that the amount was very small for your dog's size. In that case:

- keep your dog quiet, with fresh water available
- watch them closely for the next day, including overnight, for vomiting, restlessness, panting, thirst, extra weeing or trembling
- call back, or go in, if **any** sign appears

## What will the vet want to know?

Your dog's weight and health, what chocolate and how much, when it was eaten, anything else swallowed, and whether your dog has vomited.

- your dog's weight, age and any health problems (heart, epilepsy, kidneys)
- the type and brand of chocolate, and the cocoa percentage if you know it
- how much is missing (grams, squares, pieces)
- when it was eaten
- whether your dog also swallowed wrappers, foil, raisins, nuts or sugar-free sweets
- whether your dog has already vomited, and what came up
- any medicines your dog takes

## How can I stop my dog eating chocolate?

Keep chocolate high up or locked away, especially at Christmas, Easter and birthdays, and watch bags, parcels and children's treats.

Most cases happen around Christmas, Easter, Halloween and birthdays, when chocolate is in advent calendars, Easter eggs, stockings and gift bags. Store it in high or locked cupboards, make sure children and visitors know not to share it, keep handbags and shopping off the floor, and keep your dog away from post and parcels that may hold chocolate gifts. Avoid cocoa shell mulch in the garden.
