## First: see a vet today if any of these apply

Phone your vet and take your cat in **as soon as possible** if your cat has diarrhoea and:

- is a **kitten**, especially one not fully vaccinated, or an **older cat**. The PDSA advises getting prompt advice for kittens and elderly cats, because they **dehydrate** more easily
- is **vomiting**
- is **lethargic**, hiding or "not themselves"
- has **blood or mucus** in their poo
- has a **sore tummy** when touched
- **won't eat** or drink
- might have **eaten something poisonous**, such as plants, medicines or household products

The Cornell Feline Health Center advises seeing a vet if diarrhoea lasts **more than a day or two**, especially with **poor appetite, lethargy or vomiting**. The PDSA adds that you should contact your vet if mild diarrhoea **lasts longer than 24 hours** or gets worse. Out of hours, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## Why is diarrhoea dangerous in a kitten?

Because one cause to rule out quickly is panleukopenia, a highly contagious virus that is most likely to kill kittens under 5 months old.

If a kitten has diarrhoea, vomiting, a fever, is lethargic and won't eat, one of the first causes to rule out is **feline panleukopenia** (also called feline infectious enteritis or feline distemper). According to the MSD Veterinary Manual:

- **kittens under 5 months** are the most likely to die from it
- severe cases can become dangerously **dehydrated quickly**, and some kittens die with almost no warning
- severe cases need **hospital care** with fluids into a vein and supportive treatment
- the virus can survive **more than a year in the environment** unless strong disinfectants are used, and cats that recover can shed it in their poo for **up to 6 weeks**
- **vaccines** give strong, long-lasting protection

That's why a kitten with diarrhoea should never just be "watched" at home for days.

## What is diarrhoea in cats, and why does it matter?

It's passing watery poo too often, and it matters because the cat can't absorb enough water, nutrients and salts and may become dehydrated.

The Cornell Feline Health Center describes it as the abnormally **frequent passage of watery stools**, which may look grey or yellow, smell unusually bad, or contain mucus or blood. Food moves through the gut too quickly, so your cat **can't absorb enough water, nutrients and electrolytes**. The PDSA notes that severe or untreated diarrhoea can lead to dehydration and serious illness.

## Why does my cat have diarrhoea?

Often a food change, stress, something that upset the stomach or parasites, but sometimes a more serious illness that needs investigating.

Many episodes are short and settle on their own. Cornell lists these causes of short-lived diarrhoea:

- a **sudden change of food**
- **stress**, such as a long car journey or a stay at the vet's
- in **kittens**, getting used to solid food

The PDSA lists other common causes: eating something that upset the stomach, **worms**, other gut parasites such as **giardia, cryptosporidium and tritrichomonas**, and **pancreatitis**.

Less common but more serious causes include gut infections, viruses such as **panleukopenia, feline leukaemia virus (FeLV) and feline infectious peritonitis (FIP)**, poisoning, **inflammatory bowel disease**, blockages, **liver or kidney disease** and cancer. Cornell adds that diarrhoea that goes on or keeps coming back can be a sign of illness elsewhere in the body, such as an **overactive thyroid** or **lymphoma**.

In an adult cat, Cornell says, diarrhoea that isn't explained by a change of diet most often comes from a problem in the gut itself, and needs investigating.

## What can I do if the diarrhoea is mild?

If your adult cat has had loose poo for less than a day and is otherwise bright, offer water, let them rest and ask your vet about small, bland meals.

If your cat is a healthy adult, has had loose poo for **less than 24 hours** and is otherwise eating, drinking, playing and as lively as usual, the PDSA says you may be able to manage it at home for a short time:

- always leave **fresh water** out and encourage drinking: cats with diarrhoea often drink more to replace what they lose
- let your cat **rest**, but get advice if they seem very tired
- ask your vet about **small, bland, frequent meals**; the PDSA gives boiled chicken or white fish without skin or bones as examples, and advises avoiding rich or fatty food
- once their poo is back to normal, return to their usual food **gradually** over a few days

If the diarrhoea **hasn't improved within 24 hours**, your cat refuses food, or any warning sign above appears, call your vet without waiting longer.

## What shouldn't I do if my cat has diarrhoea?

Don't give human medicines or leftover antibiotics, don't keep switching foods, and don't worm your cat at random.

- **Don't give human medicines**, whether for diarrhoea or pain. Cornell warns that some over-the-counter medicines can harm cats. The Merck Veterinary Manual explains that cats are **especially sensitive to paracetamol**, which can damage their blood and liver, and more sensitive than dogs to ibuprofen. See our guide to [human painkillers and pets](/animals/nsaid-painkillers-dogs-cats).
- **Don't use leftover antibiotics.** The PDSA says antibiotics are rarely needed for diarrhoea and can make it worse by killing helpful gut bacteria. Your vet prescribes them only when they're really needed.
- **Don't keep switching foods** to see what works: sudden changes are themselves a cause of diarrhoea.
- **Don't worm your cat at random** with whatever product you have: the type of parasite matters, and your vet can check a poo sample.

## What will the vet want to know?

How long it has been going on and what it looks like, whether your cat is vomiting, eating and drinking, what has changed, their lifestyle, vaccinations and worming, and what they could have got into.

- how long the diarrhoea has lasted, how often, and what it looks like (colour, consistency, blood, mucus)
- whether your cat is vomiting, eating and drinking
- any change of food, treats or litter
- whether your cat goes outdoors or hunts, and whether there are other cats at home or new arrivals
- whether they are vaccinated and wormed, and when
- whether they could have got into plants, medicines or chemicals

If you can, bring a **fresh poo sample** in a clean container: it often helps the search for parasites. Our [visit brief](/visit-brief) helps you get this down quickly.

## How can I prevent diarrhoea in my cat?

Change food gradually, keep vaccinations up to date, follow a parasite plan agreed with your vet and keep the litter tray clean.

- **Change food gradually**, every time.
- **Keep vaccinations up to date**, starting as a kitten.
- Follow a **parasite control plan** that suits your cat's lifestyle, agreed with your vet.
- **Keep the litter tray clean**: it also helps you spot any change in their poo straight away.

A single bout of diarrhoea in an adult cat who is otherwise well is often a passing upset. A kitten, an older cat, or a cat who is also lethargic, vomiting or off their food should be **seen without delay**.
