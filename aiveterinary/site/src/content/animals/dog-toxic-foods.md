## Go now if your dog

- is shaking, staggering, having a seizure, or has collapsed
- has a very fast or irregular heartbeat, pale gums, or is panting hard at rest
- has eaten xylitol (sugar-free gum, some peanut butters), a large amount of dark chocolate, grapes or raisins, or raw bread dough, even if he looks fine

Call the clinic while you are on your way. If no vet answers, call a poison line: in the UK, Animal PoisonLine (run by VPIS) on 01202 509 000, 24 hours, for a fee; in the US, ASPCA Animal Poison Control on (888) 426-4435. Your local clinic is always the first call.

## The common kitchen poisons

| Substance | Why it is dangerous | What to do first |
|---|---|---|
| Chocolate, cocoa, coffee, tea, energy drinks | Theobromine and caffeine overstimulate heart, muscles and nerves: vomiting, restlessness, tremors, abnormal heart rhythm, seizures. The darker the chocolate, the more concentrated. | Keep the wrapper, estimate how much is missing, call now even if your dog seems well. |
| Grapes, raisins, sultanas, currants | Can cause kidney injury. Tartaric acid is the suspected toxin, and there is no known safe amount. | Call now. Early treatment protects the kidneys; waiting for signs is too late. |
| Xylitol (sugar-free gum, mints, some peanut butters, baked goods, toothpaste) | Triggers a sudden drop in blood sugar, sometimes within 10 to 15 minutes, and can damage the liver. | Treat as an emergency. Take the packaging; the ingredient list matters. |
| Onions, garlic, leeks, chives (raw, cooked, powdered) | Damage red blood cells and cause anaemia. Signs can take days: weakness, pale gums, dark urine. | Call now, even if the meal was days ago. |
| Macadamia nuts | Weakness, wobbliness, vomiting, tremors and a raised temperature, usually within 12 hours. | Call; your vet will decide whether to see him. |
| Alcohol (drinks, but also fermenting dough and some mouthwashes) | Vomiting, poor coordination, low body temperature, coma. Absorbed quickly. | Call now. |
| Raw yeast dough | Rises in the warm stomach, stretching it painfully and risking a twist; the fermentation also produces alcohol. | Go now. Do not wait to see if it passes. |
| Salt, salty snacks, play dough, salt-water | Thirst, vomiting, then tremors and seizures as blood sodium climbs. | Offer fresh water, call. |
| Fatty scraps, nuts, bones | Vomiting, diarrhoea and in some dogs pancreatitis; bones can block or perforate the gut. | Call if vomiting, pain or no stools. |

Avocado flesh is not toxic to dogs, but the stone can block the gut and the fat upsets the stomach. Milk is not a poison, but most adult dogs digest it poorly.

## What not to do

- **Do not make your dog vomit at home.** Salt, hydrogen peroxide, mustard and the other things you will find online can burn the gullet, cause salt poisoning, or send vomit into the lungs. Your vet can induce vomiting safely, and only when it is the right call.
- **Do not give milk, oil, bread or "something to soak it up".** None of them neutralise a poison, and some add a second problem.
- **Do not wait for symptoms.** With grapes, xylitol and onions, the damage is done before anything shows.
- Do not use a dose table from the internet to decide to stay home. Your vet works out the risk from your dog's weight, the substance and the amount, and that calculation is theirs to make.

## What the vet will ask

1. **What** exactly: product name, type of chocolate, whether the gum lists xylitol. Keep the packaging.
2. **How much**: grams, number of squares, how many pieces are missing from the bowl.
3. **When**: minutes or hours ago, and whether he has vomited since.
4. **Weight**, age, breed and any illness or medication.

Having these ready, even as a note on your phone, turns a long call into a short one.

## Prevention

Most kitchen poisonings happen at Christmas, Easter, barbecues and parties, when food sits at dog height. Keep chocolate, raisin cakes and gum in closed cupboards, not in bags on the floor. Check the label of any peanut butter before you use it in a treat. Keep bins closed and dough rising out of reach. Tell children and guests that "just a little" is not a safe rule for a small dog.

If your dog has eaten something on this list, phone your own vet now; they know your dog and can tell you whether to come in.
