## First: go to a vet now if your dog suddenly can't use its back legs

**Go to your vet or an emergency vet now**, and ring on the way, if your dog:

- **suddenly** has weak, wobbly or dragging back legs, or keeps tripping or falling
- **can't stand or walk**, or its back legs seem paralysed
- is in **severe pain**: crying out or yelping, a hunched back, or a stiff neck it doesn't want to move
- has lost control of its **bladder or bowels** (incontinence)

The PDSA says to contact your vet immediately if your dog is in severe pain or the signs came on suddenly. The MSD Veterinary Manual says dogs with severe neurological signs, such as paralysis, need surgery promptly to take the pressure off the spinal cord. UFAW's genetic welfare database says the speed with which a dog gets treatment "can greatly affect the outcome".

**Moving your dog:** the PDSA advises carrying a dog with a suspected slipped disc on a board or blanket. A dog in pain may bite, even a gentle one (Cornell), so use a towel or muzzle if needed.

## What is a slipped disc in a dog?

The discs that cushion the bones of the spine lose their springiness and can bulge or burst out, pressing on the spinal cord. Vets call this intervertebral disc disease (IVDD).

The PDSA explains that in IVDD the discs between the backbones become hard and fragile, and can slip out of place. A slipped disc pressing on the spinal cord causes pain, leg weakness, and sometimes paralysis or incontinence. Cornell describes how each disc has a tough outer ring around a gel-like centre; with age the discs can harden, and a disc can rupture and push into the spinal cord.

A problem in the **neck** mainly causes neck pain and stiffness, and can weaken all four legs. A problem in the **middle of the back** causes back pain and weakness of the back legs, up to paralysis and incontinence (MSD). Cornell says about 65% of cases are in the middle to lower back and about 18% in the neck alone.

## Are Dachshunds and French Bulldogs more at risk?

Yes. Both are named among the breeds most prone to slipped discs, because of the shape of their spine.

- The PDSA names Dachshunds and French Bulldogs as prone to IVDD, along with Shih Tzus, Bulldogs, Pugs, Pekingese, Beagles, Basset Hounds and Cocker Spaniels.
- **Dachshunds:** UFAW says around 25% of Dachshunds develop IVDD badly enough to need veterinary treatment. Cornell cites estimates that Dachshunds make up 40 to 75% of all IVDD cases, and that they may be affected from 3 to 6 years of age.
- A 2026 study of 4,295 Dachshunds in the UK, Norway and Denmark found that **smooth-coated** Dachshunds had worse spinal health than the other coat types (Hopper et al., Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine).
- Any dog can get a slipped disc. The MSD Veterinary Manual describes a slower form that tends to affect large breeds after the age of 5.

## What are the early signs of a disc problem?

Pain usually comes first: not wanting to jump or use stairs, a hunched back, yelping for no clear reason, or holding the head low.

UFAW says signs of pain are often the first sign of IVDD in Dachshunds, and lists reluctance to climb stairs or jump, a hunched posture and yelping without an obvious cause. Cornell adds that a dog with a neck problem may avoid turning its head, or only eat and drink when the bowl is raised. The PDSA says signs can appear slowly or very quickly.

Even mild signs need a call to your vet the same day. Cornell advises contacting your vet at the first sign of back discomfort.

## Should my dog have rest or surgery?

That is your vet's decision, based on an examination of how well the nerves are working, and often a scan.

- **Rest and pain relief** may be enough for mild cases, where the dog is in pain but can still walk. The PDSA says "strict rest for several weeks is essential for most cases": a crate or small pen, and out on a lead only for short toilet trips. The MSD manual says extra activity can push more of the disc out and make things worse. Your vet will set how long the rest lasts and which pain relief your dog needs.
- **Surgery** is likely for more severe cases, such as severe pain or a dog that cannot walk (PDSA), and when signs come back despite treatment (MSD). Cornell says: "If surgery is recommended as a treatment, don't wait", because delay increases the risk of permanent damage to the spinal cord. The PDSA says surgery can be very successful, especially within a few days of the signs starting.

The PDSA says signs can develop very quickly, and only an examination shows how badly the nerves are affected, so your vet needs to see your dog even if it is still walking. **Don't give human painkillers**: they can be dangerous for dogs (see [painkillers for dogs and cats](/animals/nsaid-painkillers-dogs-cats)).

## What will the vet do?

A careful examination of the legs, nerves and spine, then often an MRI or CT scan at a specialist centre.

The PDSA says MRI is the best test, because it shows the spinal cord, nerves and discs, but it is usually only available at specialist hospitals. X-rays show some changes but not the spinal cord itself, and blood tests, a spinal tap or a CT scan may also be needed. Cornell says imaging also helps to rule out other problems, such as tumours.

In surgery, bone is removed over the affected part of the spine so the disc material pressing on the cord can be taken out (Cornell).

## Will my dog walk again?

Many dogs do, especially when treated early, but dogs with severe signs may be left with weakness, pain or incontinence.

- The PDSA says most dogs with mild signs have a good outlook and can lead a fairly normal life after recovery, while dogs with severe signs have a worse outlook.
- The MSD manual says recovery after surgery is good if the dog can still feel pain in its legs, and that the chances fall if surgery is delayed more than 24 hours after that feeling is lost. It says signs come back in 30 to 40% of cases.
- UFAW reports that in one study of 238 dogs treated with surgery, about 85% did well.
- Recovery can take months (Cornell), with rest, controlled exercise and often physiotherapy or hydrotherapy. Some dogs need help to wee and must be checked for pressure sores. Some are so badly affected that putting them to sleep may be the kindest choice (PDSA).

## Can I prevent a slipped disc?

Not completely, but keeping your dog slim and limiting jumping and stairs can reduce the strain on its back.

Cornell advises keeping your dog trim and fit, minimising stairs and jumping on and off beds and sofas, and choosing sensible activity: walking is fine, leaping for a thrown toy is not. The PDSA adds a healthy weight and non-slip floors. If you are buying a Dachshund puppy, UFAW suggests asking whether the parents were screened with spine X-rays.

## When should I take my dog to the vet?

Now for any sudden weakness, paralysis or severe pain; the same day for milder back or neck pain.

- **Go now:** any of the signs at the top of this page.
- **Call the same day:** your dog doesn't want to jump or use the stairs, yelps for no clear reason, walks with a hunched back, or seems stiff in the neck.
- **During recovery:** call your vet if your dog gets weaker, stops weeing, or seems in more pain.

## What will the vet want to know about my dog's back?

When the signs started, how quickly they got worse, whether your dog can walk and wee, and any past back problems.

- when you first noticed the signs, and whether they came on suddenly or slowly
- whether your dog can stand and walk, and whether it is getting worse
- whether it has weed or pooed since, and whether it seems to know when it is going
- any fall, jump or knock before the signs started
- whether it has had back or neck problems, or disc surgery, before
- its breed, age and weight
- any medicines it has had, including anything given at home
