## First: go to a vet now if you see these signs

Go to a vet **immediately**, and phone on the way, if after a sting your dog:

- has **difficulty breathing**, noisy breathing or unusually fast breathing
- has a **face, lips, throat or neck that is swelling** quickly
- was stung **in the mouth or throat**, or swallowed the insect, and is drooling or struggling to swallow
- is **vomiting** or has **diarrhoea**
- is wobbly, very weak or **collapses**
- has **pale gums**
- has **hives** (raised bumps) or a severe rash
- has a seizure
- was stung **several times**, for example after disturbing a wasps' nest or a hive
- has had a bad reaction to a sting before

These can be signs of a **severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)** or of poisoning from many stings. Don't wait to see if things improve: the PDSA says "never wait to see if their symptoms improve". Anaphylaxis can be life-threatening, and although first reactions are often mild, the PDSA notes they can occasionally be severe. If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What usually happens when a bee stings a dog?

Usually a local reaction, with pain, swelling and redness, often on the face, nose or mouth.

Most stings cause a **local reaction**: sudden pain (your dog may yelp or jump), then swelling and redness at the spot. The MSD Veterinary Manual says stings in dogs and cats are most common **on the face and in the mouth**, often because dogs snap at insects. The PDSA lists licking, biting or pawing at the area, whining, holding up a paw and drooling as signs of a mild reaction.

The difficulty is that, early on, a local reaction and the start of a severe one can look alike. The Animal Emergency Service notes that severe reactions can get worse very quickly.

## What should I do if my dog has been stung?

Move away from the insects, scrape out any sting, apply something cold, keep your dog calm and watch them closely.

1. **Get away from the insects.** If your dog disturbed a nest or hive, call them away and leave the area. Several stings mean a vet visit.
2. **Look for the sting.** The MSD Veterinary Manual explains that only honeybee stings are barbed and stay in the skin, torn off with the venom sac; wasps and hornets don't leave one behind. If you can see it, the PDSA advises placing the edge of a **credit card** under the venom sac (the yellowish blob) and very gently **scraping it out**. Don't squeeze it with tweezers or your fingers, which could release more venom.
3. **Apply something cold**, such as a cold, damp cloth, to soothe the skin and reduce swelling. An ice pack wrapped in a tea towel also works, the PDSA says, but only if your dog is happy for you to hold it there.
4. **Keep your dog calm** and resting.
5. **Stop them licking or chewing** the spot.
6. **Leave fresh water** available, without forcing them to drink.
7. **Watch closely** for any sign of an allergic reaction, and call your vet straight away if one appears. Keep a particularly close eye on a dog stung on the face, mouth or head, in case swelling affects breathing or swallowing.

## What shouldn't I do after a sting?

Don't give human medicines yourself, don't rely on home remedies if there's any warning sign, and don't squeeze the sting.

- **Don't give human medicines** on your own initiative, including antihistamines, steroids or painkillers. The PDSA says to contact your vet before giving your dog any medication; the vet will decide whether a medicine is suitable, which one, and the dose. See our guide to [human painkillers and pets](/animals/nsaid-painkillers-dogs-cats).
- Don't rely on home remedies. The PDSA says vinegar for wasp stings or bicarbonate of soda for bee stings do no harm, but may not help as much as people think, and they are never a substitute for a vet if any warning sign is present.
- Don't squeeze the sting with your fingers.
- Don't put your hand in the mouth of a dog whose face is swelling or who is frightened: you risk being bitten. Go to the clinic.

## When should I call the vet anyway?

Call for advice even without warning signs if the sting is on the face, in the mouth or near the eyes, if swelling keeps growing, if your dog is small, young, old or unwell, or if the spot becomes infected.

Even without the warning signs above, phone your clinic for advice if:

- the sting is on the face, in the mouth or near the eyes
- the swelling keeps getting bigger or isn't going down
- your dog is very small, a puppy, or old or unwell
- over the following days the spot becomes hot, hard, sore or oozes pus. The MSD Veterinary Manual notes that a honeybee sting occasionally stays embedded and an **abscess** can form

## What happens if a dog is stung many times?

A lot of venom can cause vomiting, bloody diarrhoea, weakness, fever, seizures and damage to the blood, kidneys and liver, so always see a vet.

Multiple stings are a different situation. The MSD Veterinary Manual says the venom can cause, as well as pain and swelling, **vomiting and diarrhoea, sometimes with blood**, collapse, **fever**, **seizures**, damage to blood cells, and **kidney and liver failure**. A dog stung many times should always see a vet, **even if they seem fine at first**.

## What will the vet want to know?

When it happened, what insect it was, where and how many times your dog was stung, which signs you saw and whether you gave anything.

- when it happened and what insect it was (bee, wasp, hornet), if you know
- where your dog was stung and how many times
- which signs you noticed, and when they started
- whether your dog has reacted to stings before
- whether you gave anything

Our [visit brief](/visit-brief) helps you get this down quickly. If your dog has had a severe reaction before, ask your vet what to do if it happens again and whether any precautions suit your dog.

## How can I prevent stings?

Discourage your dog from chasing insects, and keep them away from nests and hives.

- Discourage your dog from snapping at flying insects or going near nests and hives.
- If you find a wasps' nest in the garden, keep your dog away and have it removed by a professional.
