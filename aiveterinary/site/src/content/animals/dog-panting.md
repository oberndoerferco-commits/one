## First: go to a vet now if you see these signs

The PDSA calls struggling to breathe "a life-threatening emergency". Go **straight to the nearest vet**, and ring on the way so they can prepare, if your dog:

- is **struggling to breathe**, with fast, short breaths, an open mouth, the head and neck stretched out, or the tummy working hard with each breath
- has **white, grey, blue or very pale gums** or tongue
- has **collapsed**, is very weak or can't stand
- is getting **rapidly worse**
- makes **new noises** when breathing, such as wheezing, snorting or whistling
- pants heavily and **won't settle after heat or exercise**, or has bright red gums, foaming at the mouth, vomiting or wobbliness (possible heatstroke)
- pants and paces, **retches without bringing anything up**, drools a lot or has a **swollen tummy** (possible bloat, GDV)

If your dog may have heatstroke, start cooling now: see [heatstroke](/animals/heatstroke). Keep your dog calm and cool on the way. Cornell suggests using the car's air conditioning, and laying a dog that can't stand on its chest with the head and neck extended.

## Is it normal for my dog to pant a lot?

Yes, after exercise, on a warm day or when excited or stressed; but a relaxed, comfortable dog normally doesn't pant.

Dogs don't sweat like we do, and panting is how they cool down. The PDSA says panting is usually a response to something, so it is most likely after exercise or on warm days. Flat-faced breeds and overweight dogs may pant more easily. Cornell adds that dogs may pant heavily during stress, excitement or exertion.

What matters is whether there is a reason, and whether it stops once the reason has gone. The PDSA advises seeing a vet if your dog pants with no obvious cause, or pants a lot of the time.

## Why is my dog panting at rest or at night?

It can be stress or pain, but it can also be a sign of illness such as heart disease, a breathing problem, Cushing's disease or fever.

The PDSA lists these less common causes of panting:

- **pain or injury**
- **heart disease**
- **BOAS**, the breathing problem of flat-faced breeds
- **Cushing's disease**, a hormone disorder
- **fever**
- **stress or anxiety**: ears pinned back, tail tucked, hiding or avoiding eye contact

The PDSA notes that these illnesses often bring other signs too, such as coughing, drinking and peeing more, or low energy. Cornell adds lung and airway problems (for example a collapsing windpipe, laryngeal paralysis, bronchitis or pneumonia), fluid around the lungs or heart, and conditions that only mimic a breathing problem, such as anaemia and anxiety.

For panting in older dogs or dogs with a heart condition, the breathing rate during sleep is a useful thing to track: our German article [Hund atmet schnell im Schlaf](/de/animals/hund-atmet-schnell-im-schlaf) explains how to count it.

## How fast should my dog breathe at rest?

Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center gives a normal rate of 12 to 30 breaths per minute, slower and deeper at rest.

Count one breath each time the chest rises and falls, for a full minute, while your dog is calm or asleep, away from heat and not straight after a walk. A rate that is clearly faster than your dog's usual, or that keeps rising, is worth a call to your vet. Your vet can tell you what is normal for your dog.

## Panting or struggling to breathe: what's the difference?

Panting is fast, shallow, open-mouthed breathing to cool down; respiratory distress is when your dog can't get enough oxygen.

Cornell describes the signs of distress as rapid breathing, often with an open mouth, a bluish tinge to the gums, visible effort from the tummy, the head and neck stretched out, louder breathing noises, and weakness or collapse. The PDSA adds fast, short breaths, a tongue curling upwards and lying stretched out. Any of these means going to a vet now, not waiting.

## What should I do right now?

Stop any activity, get your dog somewhere calm and cool, offer water, and call your vet if the panting doesn't settle or has no obvious cause.

1. **Stop exercise** and move your dog to a cool, quiet place (PDSA).
2. **Offer fresh water**, but don't force it.
3. **Keep your dog calm.** The PDSA says stress can make breathing worse.
4. **Check the gums** and how your dog is breathing. If you see any of the emergency signs above, go now.
5. **Watch whether it settles.** If the panting eases as your dog cools down and calms, it was probably normal. If it doesn't, or keeps coming back at rest, call your vet today.
6. **Don't give any medicines**, human painkillers included, unless your vet tells you to. See [painkillers for dogs and cats](/animals/nsaid-painkillers-dogs-cats).

## What will the vet do?

Examine your dog, listen to the heart and lungs, and then look for the cause of the panting.

Which tests follow depends on what your vet finds, and a dog that is struggling to breathe will be stabilised first. A short video of the panting at home, and any breathing counts you have written down, help your vet see what you see.

## When should I take my dog to the vet?

Straight away for any emergency sign above, and within a day if your dog keeps panting at rest, at night or for no obvious reason.

Also book an appointment if the panting comes with:

- coughing (see [dog cough and gagging](/animals/dog-cough-gagging))
- drinking or peeing more than usual (see [dog drinking a lot](/animals/dog-drinking-a-lot))
- low energy or tiring quickly on walks
- signs of pain, such as limping, restlessness or not wanting to be touched

The PDSA advises that even when breathing problems improve once a pet has calmed down, it is still best to have them checked, especially if breathing is faster or noisier than usual. If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know about my dog's panting?

When it happens, how long it lasts, what makes it better or worse, and any other changes you've noticed.

- when the panting started and whether it is new for your dog
- when it happens: at rest, at night, after walks, in heat, during storms or visits
- whether it settles once your dog is cool and calm
- breathing counts at rest or asleep, if you have them, and a short video
- the colour of the gums, and any noises, coughing or collapse
- whether your dog is drinking, peeing or eating more or less than usual
- any signs of pain, vomiting or a swollen tummy
- your dog's age, breed, weight and any heart or other conditions
- any medicines your dog takes, and anything it could have eaten
