## First, the signs that mean go now

A poor appetite is often the first thing owners notice when a reptile is ill: the MSD Veterinary Manual lists it among the signs of many different diseases. Go to a vet who treats reptiles now, and call ahead, if your reptile is not eating and:

- is **weak or unresponsive**, or cannot walk or lift its body off the ground
- is **breathing with its mouth open**, gasping, wheezing or whistling, or has a **discharge or bubbles** at the nose, mouth or eyes
- is **twitching, trembling or having fits**
- is a **female that may be carrying eggs** and is becoming weak, lethargic or unresponsive
- has **stopped passing droppings** or seems constipated, especially if it is also losing weight

Why these signs matter:

- A female that cannot lay her eggs (egg-binding, or dystocia) is in most cases an emergency, says the Tierspital Zürich, the University of Zurich's animal hospital. It happens in snakes, lizards and tortoises, and the signs include weakness, listlessness, long straining, a prolapse, no appetite and difficulty breathing.
- Open-mouth breathing, discharge from the nose and difficulty breathing are frequent signs of breathing problems, according to the MSD Veterinary Manual. The RSPCA describes a whistling sound when breathing and gasping for breath in royal pythons with a respiratory infection.
- Muscle spasms can be a sign of metabolic bone disease (MSD Veterinary Manual), and the RSPCA advises seeking urgent vet advice for signs of it. MSD also lists convulsions among the signs of septicaemia, a blood infection that is a common cause of death in reptiles.
- The RSPCA says abnormal droppings or constipation together with weight loss can be serious in leopard geckos, and that impaction, a blockage of the gut, is potentially fatal.

## Is it normal for my reptile to stop eating?

Sometimes: snakes often refuse food before a shed, many reptiles eat less in the breeding season or in their winter slowdown, and females carrying eggs may stop eating, but a reptile that is losing weight or seems unwell needs a vet.

- **Before a shed.** "Before shedding, snakes lose their appetite, and their skin color becomes mildly translucent and dull," says the MSD Veterinary Manual, and the Tierspital Zürich notes that the eyes turn cloudy a few days before. The RSPCA says a royal python may refuse food when it is time to shed.
- **Females with eggs.** The British Chelonia Group (BCG), a UK tortoise and turtle charity, says refusing food is natural in female tortoises carrying eggs. But the Tierspital Zürich lists a lack of appetite, together with weakness and listlessness, among the signs of egg-binding, so a female that becomes weak or lethargic needs a vet at once.
- **The winter slowdown (brumation) and hibernation.** The RSPCA says bearded dragons, leopard geckos and corn snakes may eat less during brumation but should not lose weight, and that a bearded dragon should not stop eating entirely. For tortoises, the BCG says refusing food is natural in the month before hibernation and in cold weather. See [tortoise hibernation](/animals/tortoise-hibernation).
- **Royal pythons.** The RSPCA notes that royal pythons "have a reputation for going off their food", sometimes seasonally, and still says to consult your reptile vet if your snake suddenly stops feeding.

Weigh your reptile regularly, so you notice weight loss early. The RSPCA recommends this for snakes, to make sure they are not becoming underweight.

## Could my reptile be too cold to eat?

Yes: reptiles depend on the warmth and light of their enclosure, and the MSD Veterinary Manual says they cannot process fluids and nutrients properly if the conditions are not right for the species.

- **Temperatures.** Check the basking spot and the cool end with a thermometer and compare them with a care sheet for your species, or ask your vet. The MSD Veterinary Manual says a sick reptile should be housed at a temperature near the upper limit preferred by its species.
- **Lighting.** The Tierspital Zürich warns that UV lamps only last about 6 months and that window glass filters out UV light. A missing or worn-out UVB lamp is one cause of [metabolic bone disease](/animals/reptile-metabolic-bone-disease), which itself causes a poor appetite.
- **Humidity.** Measure it with a hygrometer: the RSPCA recommends this for snakes, and the right level depends on the species.

## What illnesses make a reptile stop eating?

Many: infections, parasites, gut blockages, gout, metabolic bone disease, vitamin A deficiency and egg-binding are among them, so a vet needs to find the cause.

- **Infections and gout.** The MSD Veterinary Manual lists loss of appetite among the signs of inclusion body disease in boas and pythons and of herpesvirus infection in tortoises, and says gout can be so painful that some reptiles refuse to move, eat or drink.
- **Metabolic bone disease,** a weakening of the bones caused by a diet with the wrong balance of calcium and phosphorus, a lack of vitamin D3 or a lack of UVB light, causes a poor appetite and weakness, according to the MSD Veterinary Manual.
- **Vitamin A deficiency,** which the Tierspital Zürich says is far more common in terrapins than in plant-eating tortoises, can cause a lack of appetite, swollen and inflamed eyelids, ear abscesses and breathing problems. The MSD Veterinary Manual notes that turtles with pneumonia often have an underlying vitamin A deficiency.
- **Parasites.** The Tierspital Zürich explains that gut parasites rob reptiles of nutrients, so they lose weight, or young animals fail to gain it, sometimes with diarrhoea. The MSD Veterinary Manual adds that snakes with roundworms may have no appetite. The RSPCA lists changes in appetite among the signs that may point to internal parasites in terrapins.
- **Impaction** from swallowed loose substrate: the RSPCA calls impaction potentially fatal in leopard geckos.
- **Egg-binding** in females (Tierspital Zürich).
- **Problems after hibernation** in tortoises: the Tortoise Protection Group (TPG) says all tortoises should start eating within one week of waking, and that veterinary help should be sought if they do not.

## What can I do while I arrange the vet visit?

Check and correct the set-up, weigh your reptile, offer water and its usual food, and do not force-feed it or give it any medicines or vitamins.

- **Fix the set-up first.** Temperatures, lighting and humidity right for the species (MSD Veterinary Manual).
- **Weigh it** and write down when it last ate and passed droppings.
- **Look for dehydration.** The MSD Veterinary Manual lists loose skin or sunken eyes as signs.
- **Offer water and its usual food, but don't force-feed.** The MSD Veterinary Manual says assisted feeding is best directed by your vet, because feeding a severely dehydrated, malnourished reptile can cause further problems.
- **Don't give vitamins, calcium or medicines** without your vet's advice. The Tierspital Zürich warns that too many vitamins can themselves be harmful.
- **Wash your hands** after handling your reptile or anything in its enclosure: the CDC notes that even healthy reptiles can carry Salmonella.

## When should I take my reptile to the vet for not eating?

Today if it is not eating and is also losing weight, quieter than usual or showing any other change, or if you cannot find a normal reason, and straight away for the signs at the top of this page.

See a vet today if your reptile:

- is not eating and seems quieter, thinner or different in any way
- has lost weight, or its tail base looks thinner (the RSPCA describes a thick tail base as a sign of a healthy bearded dragon and a well-fed leopard gecko)
- is a snake that suddenly stops feeding (RSPCA)
- is a tortoise that is not feeding or appears ill, which the BCG says should be taken to a vet without delay
- is a tortoise that has not started eating within a week of waking from hibernation (TPG)
- has swollen eyelids or a discharge from its eyes or nose (Tierspital Zürich, MSD Veterinary Manual)
- is a bearded dragon, leopard gecko or corn snake that loses weight during brumation (RSPCA)

Not every vet treats reptiles. The MSD Veterinary Manual advises asking about a vet's experience with reptiles, and an annual health check helps make sure your reptile is well nourished and free from diseases and parasites.

## What will the vet want to know about my reptile?

The species, age and sex, when it last ate, its weight history, the temperatures, lighting and humidity, its diet and supplements, and any recent shed, breeding season or change.

- The species, age and sex, and how long you have had it
- When it last ate, what it ate, and how often it normally eats
- Its weight history, if you have one
- The temperatures at the basking spot, at the cool end and at night, and how you measure them
- The type and age of the UVB lamp, and your humidity readings
- Its diet and any supplements
- When it last shed, and whether the skin came off normally
- When you last saw droppings and urates, and what they looked like
- Any recent change: a move, a new enclosure, new animals, more handling, the breeding season, brumation or hibernation
- For a female, whether she could be carrying eggs
- Whether other reptiles in your household are ill
- Any treatment you have already given

If your reptile has stopped eating and is weak, breathing with its mouth open or twitching, stop reading and call a vet who treats reptiles now.
