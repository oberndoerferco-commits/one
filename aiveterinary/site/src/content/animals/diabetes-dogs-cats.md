## First, the signs that mean go now

Diabetes itself is a "this week" problem. It becomes an emergency when the blood turns acidic (ketoacidosis). Go to a clinic today, and call ahead, if a dog or cat that is drinking and urinating a lot also:

- stops eating, vomits, or is very quiet and weak
- has sweet or nail-varnish smelling breath
- is breathing fast or deeply at rest
- collapses

And if your pet is already on insulin: weakness, wobbling, trembling, odd behaviour, seizures or collapse can mean low blood sugar. Rub honey or glucose syrup on the gums and call your vet immediately.

## What is diabetes in dogs and cats?

In diabetes mellitus the body cannot move sugar from the blood into the cells, either because the pancreas no longer makes enough insulin or because the cells have stopped responding to it. Sugar builds up in the blood, spills into the urine and drags water with it. That is why the animal pees more, drinks more, and loses weight while eating hungrily: the cells are starving in the middle of plenty.

In dogs the usual picture is an insulin deficiency that does not reverse, mostly in middle-aged and older dogs. In cats it looks more like type 2 diabetes in people: overweight, middle-aged or older cats, more often neutered males, sometimes after long courses of steroid medicines.

## What are the first signs of diabetes in dogs and cats?

Drinking more, urinating more, eating more and losing weight despite the appetite.

- **Drinking more**, from taps, puddles, anywhere
- **Urinating more**: bigger clumps in the litter tray, accidents indoors, a dog that needs out at night
- **Eating more**, sometimes ravenously
- **Losing weight** despite the appetite
- In dogs: eyes that turn cloudy or white (cataracts), sometimes quite suddenly
- In cats: weak back legs, walking flat on the hocks, no longer jumping up
- Tiredness, a dull coat, recurring urinary infections

The first three can also point to kidney disease or, in cats, an overactive thyroid, which is why a vet needs to look rather than guess.

## How is diabetes diagnosed in dogs and cats?

The diagnosis rests on three things together: the typical signs, a persistently high blood sugar, and sugar in the urine. One high reading is not enough, especially in cats, where the stress of the clinic visit alone can push blood sugar up. Your vet may add a blood test that reflects the average level over the previous weeks (fructosamine).

Expect a fuller blood and urine panel too, looking for the companions of diabetes: urinary infection, kidney and liver values, pancreatitis, and in cats other hormonal diseases that make diabetes harder to control.

## How is diabetes in dogs and cats treated day to day?

With insulin injections, usually twice a day, a consistent diet agreed with your vet and, for some cats, an oral medicine instead of insulin.

**Insulin.** Almost every diabetic dog, and most diabetic cats, need insulin by injection under the skin, usually twice a day about twelve hours apart. The needles are tiny and most animals barely notice; the clinic will show you and let you practise.

A few rules that matter:

- Keep insulin in the fridge, never frozen, and roll the bottle gently rather than shaking it.
- Never change the dose yourself. Adjustments are made by your vet, slowly, usually not more often than once a week.
- If you are not sure an injection went in, do not give a second one. A missed dose is far safer than a double dose.
- Do not inject a pet that has not eaten or is vomiting; call the clinic instead.

**Food.** Dogs do best on two equal meals timed with the injections, the same food in the same amounts every day. For cats, a wet diet that is low in carbohydrate helps control blood sugar and, in overweight cats, slow weight loss can reduce the amount of insulin needed. Agree any diet change with your vet rather than cutting food on your own.

**Other options.** For some otherwise healthy cats a newer class of once-daily oral medicine is an alternative to insulin; it is not suitable for every cat. In an unneutered female dog, spaying is usually recommended because the hormones of the cycle work against insulin.

## How do I monitor my diabetic pet at home?

Keep a simple daily diary: insulin given and when, appetite, thirst (measure the water you put down), urination, energy, vomiting or diarrhoea, and a weekly weight. Many owners also check urine with dipsticks for sugar and ketones, or learn to take a drop of blood from the ear with a pet glucose meter; some clinics use skin sensors that read blood sugar continuously for a couple of weeks. Your vet combines your diary with periodic blood sugar curves or fructosamine tests, more often at the start and whenever something changes.

## How long can a diabetic dog or cat live?

With insulin, food and monitoring, most diabetic dogs and cats live comfortably for years, and the routine soon becomes a habit. In cats, treatment started early and controlled well can even lead to remission: the diabetes goes quiet and insulin can be stopped, though it may return and the diet should stay. Remission is rare in dogs. Cataracts in diabetic dogs can often be operated on. The main risks are ketoacidosis when diabetes goes untreated or another illness tips it over, and low blood sugar from too much insulin; both are largely preventable.

The dose, the diet and the monitoring plan are built for your animal, not for diabetic pets in general, so take this vocabulary and your diary to your own vet.
