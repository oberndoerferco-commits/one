## First, the signs that mean go now

Egg binding means a female bird can't pass an egg. VCA Animal Hospitals warns that small birds can die within a few hours of becoming egg bound, and the Merck Veterinary Manual says egg-bound birds often arrive at the vet as emergencies. Caught early, VCA says, egg binding may be resolved easily.

Go to a vet who treats birds now, and call ahead, if your female bird:

- is **straining**, as if trying to pass droppings or lay an egg
- is **weak**, not perching, or **sitting low on the perch or on the cage floor**
- has a **swollen belly**
- has an **egg showing at the vent**, or **tissue bulging out of the vent**. The MSD Veterinary Manual says this kind of prolapse needs immediate veterinary care
- **bobs her tail** while breathing, breathes hard or keeps her eyes closed
- has **bloody droppings**
- can't stand, or can't grip the perch

These signs are described by VCA, the MSD Veterinary Manual and the Merck Veterinary Manual. Other illnesses can cause them too, which is one more reason for your vet to examine her straight away.

## What is egg binding?

It is when a female bird can't pass an egg, so the egg stays inside her body.

The MSD Veterinary Manual names cockatiels, budgerigars (budgies) and lovebirds as commonly affected, though larger parrots can be too. VCA adds canaries and finches to the list of small birds that are often affected, and stresses that any female bird can become egg bound.

## Can my budgie lay eggs without a male?

Yes. A female bird can lay eggs without ever meeting a male, so a hen kept on her own can become egg bound too.

VCA notes that many owners are surprised that a female bird will still lay eggs even when not exposed to a male. Not every female will lay, but all of them can. Each egg still needs calcium: the MSD Veterinary Manual explains that calcium is necessary to form the hard shell around an egg just before it is laid.

## Why do birds become egg bound?

Diet plays a big part: seed-heavy diets lack calcium and other nutrients, and overweight, inactive hens are most at risk.

- **Seed-based diets.** VCA says seed-based diets are deficient in many vitamins and minerals, especially calcium, selenium and vitamins D and E. Low calcium can stop the oviduct from contracting, and the wrong calcium levels can cause soft or misshapen eggs. The RSPCA also warns that purely seed-based diets can lead to obesity, fatty liver syndrome, and vitamin A and calcium deficiency.
- **Weight and exercise.** The MSD Veterinary Manual says egg binding is most common in overweight, inactive birds.
- **Laying a lot.** The Merck Veterinary Manual describes excessive egg laying as repeat clutches, or more eggs than normal. It is common in budgerigars, lovebirds and especially cockatiels and, if untreated, often leads to more serious reproductive problems, egg binding among them.
- **Other causes** named by the MSD and Merck Veterinary Manuals include vitamin A deficiency, disease or tumours of the oviduct, being a first-time layer, genetic factors, and an unsuitable environment or no nest box. VCA adds old age.

## How long should it take my bird to lay an egg?

VCA says a healthy hen that is in the process of laying should pass a formed egg within 24 to 48 hours, but a hen that is straining or unwell needs a vet now, not later.

Don't use that time as a reason to wait. VCA explains that if a long time has passed since a bird started trying to lay, she may become critically ill, and that small birds can die within a few hours of becoming egg bound. If she is straining, fluffed up or on the cage floor, go now.

## What should I do while I get her to the vet?

Keep her warm, dark and calm in a carrier and go. Don't try to get the egg out or treat her at home.

- Put her in a **secure, well-ventilated carrier**, keep it **warm** and **cover at least three sides** with a towel. The MSD Veterinary Manual suggests placing the carrier on a heating pad or next to a warm water bottle.
- **Don't pull, press or massage** her belly or the egg, and don't put anything into the vent. Getting a stuck egg out is a vet's job, sometimes under sedation.
- **Don't give calcium, oils or other supplements** unless your vet tells you to.
- If she has already laid an egg, **bring it**, even if it is broken or soft.
- Bring the cage paper with her **latest droppings**.

## What will the vet do?

Usually an examination and X-rays, then treatment matched to how ill she is, from warmth, fluids and calcium to removing the egg under sedation or, sometimes, surgery.

The MSD Veterinary Manual explains that vets often take X-rays to see the size and position of the egg. They may give calcium and fluids, lubricate the vent and put the bird in a warm cage with a humidifier to help the egg pass. If that fails, she may need to be sedated so the egg can be removed through the vent. VCA adds that critically ill birds are first treated for shock, that an egg without a shell may need an ultrasound to be seen, and that some cases need surgery. Which treatment is right is a decision for your vet.

## Why does my cockatiel keep laying eggs?

Some hens, cockatiels above all, lay clutch after clutch. Left untreated, this often leads to more serious reproductive problems, so talk to your vet.

The Merck Veterinary Manual calls excessive egg laying common in small birds, especially cockatiels. VCA explains that chronic layers are commonly treated with hormonal drug therapy to stop egg laying for a while. That is a vet decision, and so are the changes at home that usually go with it.

VCA describes signs of hormonal behaviour in a hen: tearing up paper in the cage, constantly looking for a place to hide, or lifting her tail to rub her vent on objects. The changes it lists include fewer hours of light each day, no stroking or petting for a month, and less high-energy food, like fruit and nuts, for several weeks. The MSD Veterinary Manual notes that stroking a bird's back can stimulate sexual behaviour, especially in cockatoos. Ask your vet which of these suits your bird before you start.

## When should I take my bird to the vet about egg laying?

Now if she is straining or unwell; within a few days if she lays a lot, lays soft or misshapen eggs, or lives mostly on seeds.

Book a visit soon if your hen:

- lays clutch after clutch, or more eggs than usual
- lays eggs with soft or misshapen shells
- eats mostly seeds, or is overweight
- shows hormonal behaviour, such as tearing up paper, looking for places to hide or rubbing her vent on objects
- is a cockatiel, budgie or lovebird that has started laying and you are not sure her diet is right

## What will the vet want to know about my bird?

Her species and age, when she last laid and started straining, how many eggs she has laid, her diet, and what her droppings look like.

- species and age
- when she last laid an egg, and when the straining or other signs started
- how many eggs in this clutch, and roughly how many this year
- whether any eggs had soft, thin or no shells, or were misshapen
- her diet: seeds, pellets, vegetables, treats, and any calcium source
- whether there is a male, a nest box, or a hiding place she keeps going to
- how many hours of light and darkness she gets
- what her droppings look like, and whether there has been any blood
- any treatment you have already given
