## First, the signs that mean go now

Egg binding means a female bird can't pass an egg. The Merck Veterinary Manual says egg-bound birds often arrive at the vet as emergencies, and a client handout from LafeberVet, an educational website for veterinarians, says a bird with signs of egg binding should be seen by an avian vet right away.

Go to a vet who treats birds now, and call ahead, if your female bird:

- is **straining**, as if trying to pass droppings or lay an egg
- is **weak**, not perching, or **sitting low on the perch or on the cage floor**
- has a **swollen belly**
- has an **egg showing at the vent**, or **tissue bulging out of the vent**. The MSD Veterinary Manual says this kind of prolapse needs immediate veterinary care
- **bobs her tail** while breathing, breathes hard or keeps her eyes closed
- has **bloody droppings**
- can't use her legs, or her wings droop

These signs are described by the MSD Veterinary Manual, the Merck Veterinary Manual and LafeberVet. Other illnesses can cause them too, which is one more reason for your vet to examine her straight away.

## What is egg binding?

It is when a female bird can't pass an egg, so the egg stays inside her body.

The MSD Veterinary Manual names cockatiels, budgerigars (budgies) and lovebirds as commonly affected, though larger parrots can be too. LafeberVet adds finches and canaries to the small species in which reproductive problems are common.

## Can my budgie lay eggs without a male?

Yes. A female bird can lay eggs without ever meeting a male, so a hen kept on her own can become egg bound too.

LafeberVet's handout confirms that a female can produce eggs without a male, though the eggs will not be fertile. Each egg still needs calcium: the MSD Veterinary Manual explains that calcium is necessary to form the hard shell around an egg just before it is laid.

## Why do birds become egg bound?

Diet plays a big part: seed-heavy diets lack calcium and other nutrients, and overweight, inactive hens are most at risk.

- **Seed-based diets.** The RSPCA warns that purely seed-based diets can lead to obesity, fatty liver syndrome, and vitamin A and calcium deficiency. The Merck Veterinary Manual names calcium deficiency, which leads to misshapen or soft-shelled eggs, as a factor in egg binding.
- **Weight and exercise.** The MSD Veterinary Manual says egg binding is most common in overweight, inactive birds.
- **Laying a lot.** The Merck Veterinary Manual describes excessive egg laying as repeat clutches, or more eggs than normal. It is common in budgerigars, lovebirds and especially cockatiels and, if untreated, often leads to more serious reproductive problems, egg binding among them.
- **Other causes** named by the MSD and Merck Veterinary Manuals include vitamin A deficiency, disease or tumours of the oviduct, being a first-time layer, genetic factors, and an unsuitable environment or no nest box. LafeberVet adds old age.

## How long should it take my bird to lay an egg?

Don't count the hours: a hen that is straining or unwell needs a vet now, not later.

LafeberVet's handout says an avian vet should see an egg-bound bird right away if she seems depressed, eats poorly, stands with her legs wide apart, strains, wags her tail, can't use her legs or droops her wings. The Merck Veterinary Manual adds that egg-bound birds often arrive as emergencies. If she is straining, fluffed up or on the cage floor, go now.

## What should I do while I get her to the vet?

Keep her warm, dark and calm in a carrier and go. Don't try to get the egg out or treat her at home.

- Put her in a **secure, well-ventilated carrier**, keep it **warm** and **cover at least three sides** with a towel. The MSD Veterinary Manual suggests placing the carrier on a heating pad or next to a warm water bottle.
- **Don't pull, press or massage** her belly or the egg, and don't put anything into the vent. Getting a stuck egg out is a vet's job, sometimes under sedation.
- **Don't give calcium, oils or other supplements** unless your vet tells you to.
- If she has already laid an egg, **bring it**, even if it is broken or soft.
- Bring the cage paper with her **latest droppings**.

## What will the vet do?

Usually an examination and X-rays, then treatment matched to how ill she is, from warmth, fluids and calcium to removing the egg under sedation or, sometimes, surgery.

The MSD Veterinary Manual explains that vets often take X-rays to see the size and position of the egg. They may give calcium and fluids, lubricate the vent and put the bird in a warm cage with a humidifier to help the egg pass. If that fails, she may need to be sedated so the egg can be removed through the vent. The MSD Veterinary Manual adds that some birds need surgery under full anaesthesia to remove the egg through the abdomen. Which treatment is right is a decision for your vet.

## Why does my cockatiel keep laying eggs?

Some hens, cockatiels above all, lay clutch after clutch. Left untreated, this often leads to more serious reproductive problems, so talk to your vet.

The Merck Veterinary Manual calls excessive egg laying common in small birds, especially cockatiels. LafeberVet's handout says medical treatment may include hormone injections or implants. That is a vet decision, and so are the changes at home that usually go with it.

Signs of hormonal behaviour named by the Merck Veterinary Manual include broodiness, a wide-based stance and rubbing the vent on objects. The changes at home described by LafeberVet and the Merck Veterinary Manual include fewer hours of light each day, removing nest boxes, nesting material and toys she treats like a mate, and not stroking her, especially on the back. The MSD Veterinary Manual notes that stroking a bird's back can stimulate sexual behaviour, especially in cockatoos. Ask your vet which of these suits your bird before you start.

## When should I take my bird to the vet about egg laying?

Now if she is straining or unwell; within a few days if she lays a lot, lays soft or misshapen eggs, or lives mostly on seeds.

Book a visit soon if your hen:

- lays clutch after clutch, or more eggs than usual
- lays eggs with soft or misshapen shells
- eats mostly seeds, or is overweight
- shows hormonal behaviour, such as broodiness or rubbing her vent on objects
- is a cockatiel, budgie or lovebird that has started laying and you are not sure her diet is right

## What will the vet want to know about my bird?

Her species and age, when she last laid and started straining, how many eggs she has laid, her diet, and what her droppings look like.

- species and age
- when she last laid an egg, and when the straining or other signs started
- how many eggs in this clutch, and roughly how many this year
- whether any eggs had soft, thin or no shells, or were misshapen
- her diet: seeds, pellets, vegetables, treats, and any calcium source
- whether there is a male, a nest box or nesting material
- how many hours of light and darkness she gets
- what her droppings look like, and whether there has been any blood
- any treatment you have already given
