## First, rule out a blocked bladder

When a cat goes in and out of the litter tray, squats and strains with nothing to show for it, many owners assume constipation. **But the same behaviour can mean your cat cannot pass urine.** The two look very alike, and International Cat Care notes that straining to pass urine "can be an emergency".

A blocked urethra is a **true emergency**. The Cornell Feline Health Center says the time from complete blockage to death may be **less than 24 to 48 hours**. **Male cats, including neutered males**, are at much greater risk because their urethra is longer and narrower.

Go to a vet **now**, at night too, and call ahead, if your cat:

- keeps going to the tray, squats and strains, but passes **no urine or only a few drops**
- leaves **little or no urine** in the tray
- **cries out** while straining
- licks the genital area a lot
- has a tense, painful belly, vomits, won't eat, or is dull or hiding

If you can't tell whether your cat is trying to pass urine or faeces, **treat it as an emergency**, especially in a male cat. Don't massage the belly or give a laxative while you wait. An unnecessary visit is far better than a missed blockage. See our [emergency page](/emergency) and [cat straining in the litter box](/animals/cat-straining-litter-box).

Also go straight away if your constipated cat is vomiting repeatedly, is very dull, or has a hard, painful belly.

## How can I tell constipation from a urinary blockage?

Look in the litter tray for normal-sized urine clumps, and watch whether any faeces come out when your cat strains.

- **Check the tray.** Are there wet clumps of normal size? Have they got smaller or fewer since yesterday? Clumping litter makes this easier to see.
- **Watch a visit to the tray.** Does any faeces come out, and what does it look like?
- In a home with several cats this is hard to judge. If in doubt, phone your vet.

## When should I take my constipated cat to the vet?

Today if your cat hasn't passed faeces for more than 48 hours or is straining with hard, dry stools, and sooner if there are other signs.

The Ontario Veterinary College's pet nutrition team says most healthy adult cats pass faeces **once or twice a day**, and advises calling your vet if your cat **hasn't pooped in more than 48 hours**, has hard, dry stools or is straining.

Book a visit today if your cat also:

- is vomiting, off food, quiet, or has a sore belly
- has lost weight or is drinking more than usual
- has been constipated before
- has recently had an accident, such as a fall, or has trouble walking
- is older or has kidney disease

## How do I know if my cat is constipated?

Small, hard, dry faeces, long visits to the tray and straining, sometimes with a little liquid that looks like diarrhoea.

International Cat Care lists:

- straining or difficulty passing faeces, and spending longer in the tray
- **small, hard, dry faeces**, inside or outside the tray
- crying out in pain
- in severe cases, loss of appetite or vomiting

A little **liquid faeces** can leak around hard, dry stool from constant straining, so it can look like diarrhoea when it isn't.

If constipation isn't treated, or keeps coming back, faeces build up and harden in the colon (**obstipation**). In time the colon can stretch and lose its ability to push, a condition called **megacolon**, which may need surgery. That's why vets treat constipation promptly rather than letting it drag on.

## Why is my cat constipated?

Common causes are not drinking enough, kidney disease, swallowed hair or litter, being older and overweight, avoiding the tray, pain, a narrowed pelvis, some medicines, or no clear cause.

- **Dehydration.** The colon draws more water out of the faeces. Cats with chronic kidney disease are often dehydrated.
- **Hair, bones or litter** mixed in with the faeces.
- **Being older and overweight**, with weaker colon contractions.
- **Avoiding the tray** because it's dirty, another cat is guarding it, or because of stress.
- **Pain**, for example arthritis that makes squatting hard.
- **A narrowed pelvis** after a badly healed fracture, or a mass.
- **Nerve or metabolic problems**, including abnormal blood potassium or calcium levels.
- **Side effects of some medicines.**
- In many cats no single cause is found (idiopathic megacolon).

## What should I not do at home?

Don't give enemas, laxatives, oil or human painkillers, and don't wait days if your cat is unwell.

- **Never use a human enema.** The MSD Veterinary Manual says **phosphate-containing enemas must be avoided in cats**, because they can cause severe, potentially life-threatening salt imbalances. If an enema is needed, your vet decides and gives it.
- **Don't give human laxatives** or put **oil** in your cat's mouth. Laxatives can make dehydration worse, and oil given by mouth can be inhaled and cause pneumonia.
- **Don't give human painkillers.** Paracetamol is toxic to cats even in small amounts, and ibuprofen and aspirin are dangerous too.
- Don't try to remove faeces with your fingers or any object.
- Don't wait days "to see if it passes" if your cat is vomiting, off food or dull.

**Things that help if your cat is otherwise bright:** offer wet food, put out several water bowls in different places, keep the tray clean and easy to reach, and note when your cat last passed normal faeces.

## What will the vet do?

Check the bladder isn't blocked, find out how much faeces has built up and why, and treat with fluids, diet, cat-safe laxatives, enemas or emptying the bowel under anaesthetic.

First your vet makes sure the bladder isn't blocked. Then, by feeling the belly and often with x-rays and blood tests, they assess how much faeces has built up and look for causes such as kidney disease. Treatment depends on severity: rehydration first, then diet changes, laxatives suitable for cats, enemas, or, in stubborn cases, removing the faeces under anaesthetic. For repeated constipation your vet may suggest ongoing treatment. Our [visit brief](/visit-brief) helps you note when your cat last passed faeces and urine.

## How can I prevent constipation?

Help your cat drink more, feed wet food, add fibre gradually with your vet's advice, keep the tray clean and keep your cat a healthy weight.

- **More water:** fresh water every day, several bowls away from the tray.
- **Wet food** adds water to every meal.
- **Fibre:** your vet may suggest a higher-fibre food or supplement. Add it gradually, as too much can cause loose stools.
- **Several clean trays** in quiet places, with low sides for cats with stiff joints.
- **Healthy weight**, and treatment for problems like arthritis.

And the most important rule: a cat, especially a male, straining in the tray with nothing coming out needs a vet now. Don't assume it's "just" constipation.
