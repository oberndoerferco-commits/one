## First, the signs that mean go now

A guinea pig's gut depends on a steady supply of hay: the RSPCA says guinea pigs need hay and/or grass for their digestive systems to work properly. The PDSA warns that a guinea pig that stops eating is at risk of a life-threatening problem called gut stasis. Guinea pigs also hide illness well. The MSD Veterinary Manual says guinea pigs hide signs of illness until they are very sick, so by the time you notice, things may already be serious.

Go to a vet immediately, and call ahead, if your guinea pig:

- has stopped eating, or you are not sure when it last ate
- is passing fewer droppings than usual, or none at all
- has stopped drinking, or has severe diarrhoea
- is drooling and not eating
- has a swollen, bloated belly, or seems to be in pain
- is breathing with difficulty
- is weak, very quiet, or hiding more than usual

Do not wait for a set number of hours. The RSPCA advises consulting your vet immediately if your guinea pig's eating or drinking changes, or its droppings get fewer or stop, because it could be seriously ill.

Not every practice sees many guinea pigs. The MSD Veterinary Manual advises a check-up at least once a year with a veterinarian experienced with guinea pigs: find one now, before you need it. Our [emergency page](/emergency) lists the signs for other animals too.

## Why is it so serious when a guinea pig stops eating?

Without a steady flow of food and fibre, the gut can slow down or stop, which causes gas, bloating and pain and makes the guinea pig eat even less.

The MSD Veterinary Manual explains that hay is essential for wearing down the teeth and keeping the gut bacteria healthy. Without enough fibre, or with too many pellets and sugars, the normal gut bacteria change and food slows down or stops moving through the gut. This leads to gas, bloating, pain, loss of appetite and potentially rapid decline and death.

The PDSA adds that stomach upsets in guinea pigs can quickly become very serious, especially if your guinea pig has stopped eating or has severe diarrhoea, because it can become very dehydrated and lethargic. Because guinea pigs can decline quickly, the MSD Veterinary Manual says early treatment is often critical.

## What should I do while I get my guinea pig to the vet?

Phone ahead, keep your guinea pig calm, keep its usual hay, greens and water within reach, note when it last ate and passed droppings, and give no medicines of your own.

- Phone the practice and say you are bringing a guinea pig that has stopped eating.
- Keep your guinea pig calm and quiet in a secure carrier, with some of its usual hay.
- Offer its usual hay, fresh greens and water. Do not force-feed or syringe-feed unless your vet has shown you how and asked you to.
- Note when it last ate normally and when you last saw normal droppings.
- Do not give any human medicine, or medicine left over from another pet. The MSD Veterinary Manual warns that many common antibiotics are dangerous for guinea pigs.

Whether your guinea pig needs fluids, syringe feeding or tests is a vet decision. The MSD Veterinary Manual describes vets giving fluids by injection and starting syringe feeding when a guinea pig is not eating and is dehydrated.

## Why has my guinea pig stopped eating?

Dental problems, too little hay, a sudden change of diet and too little vitamin C are common causes; respiratory infections, ovarian cysts and bloat are less common but serious.

| Common | Less common but serious |
|---|---|
| Overgrown or misaligned teeth | Pneumonia or another respiratory infection |
| Too little hay, or too many pellets and sugary foods | A stomach upset caused by infection or parasites |
| A sudden change of diet | Ovarian cysts in females |
| Too little vitamin C (scurvy) | Bloat and twisting of the stomach |

Where this comes from: the MSD Veterinary Manual says dental problems are common in guinea pigs, and lists [vitamin C deficiency](/animals/guinea-pig-vitamin-c), [pneumonia](/animals/guinea-pig-sneezing) and ovarian cysts among the conditions that take away a guinea pig's appetite. The PDSA warns that changing the diet too quickly can cause a tummy upset and stop a guinea pig eating, which is very serious in guinea pigs, and names infections and parasites among the common causes of stomach upsets. The Merck Veterinary Manual describes gastric dilation and volvulus, where the stomach swells and twists, as generally fatal in guinea pigs, and notes that owners often miss the first signs.

If a guinea pig's appetite drops, the MSD Veterinary Manual says the vet will look for the cause, such as dental disease, an unbalanced diet or another illness, using x-rays and blood tests. This list is to help you follow that conversation, not to diagnose.

## Could my guinea pig's teeth be the problem?

Often, yes: guinea pig teeth grow all their lives, and when they are not worn down they can become too long and painful to eat with.

The PDSA explains that guinea pigs' teeth keep growing throughout their lives. If they are not worn down, they can grow too long and cut the gums, cheeks and tongue. The MSD Veterinary Manual lists poor genetics, too little chewing because of too little hay, and vitamin C deficiency among the causes of dental problems.

According to the PDSA, signs of dental problems include drooling, eating less or not eating, teeth that look too long or out of place, and swellings on the jaw or face.

Never try to trim your guinea pig's teeth yourself. The RSPCA says only vets should correct overgrown or misaligned teeth, and suggests checking your guinea pig's front teeth every week.

## When should I take my guinea pig to the vet for eating less?

For guinea pigs, the honest answer is: as soon as you notice. Even if your guinea pig is still eating a little, see a vet today if it:

- is eating less than usual, or seems to have trouble chewing or swallowing
- is drooling, or has a wet chin
- has teeth that look too long or out of place, or a swelling on its jaw or face
- is passing smaller or fewer droppings, or soft droppings that stick to its back end
- has droppings stuck around its bottom
- is losing weight
- is drinking or peeing more than usual

The RSPCA recommends checking every day that your guinea pigs are eating and passing plenty of dry droppings.

## What will the vet want to know about my guinea pig?

When it last ate and passed normal droppings, what it eats, any recent changes, its weight, past dental problems and any medicine given at home.

- When your guinea pig last ate normally, and what it ate
- When you last saw normal droppings, and what they looked like
- Whether it is drinking, and whether it has diarrhoea
- Diet: hay, which vegetables, which pellets, and when you opened the bag
- Any recent change: new food, a new enclosure or home, a new or lost companion
- Its usual weight, if you know it
- Previous dental problems or illness and, for a female, her age
- Any medicine or treatment given at home

If your guinea pig has stopped eating or passing droppings, stop reading and call your vet, or the nearest vet that sees guinea pigs, now.
