## Why does so much happen in a puppy's or kitten's first year?

Because most of what protects a dog or cat for life, from vaccines and wormers to microchip, teeth and food, happens before the first birthday.

Most of what protects a dog or cat for life happens before the first birthday: vaccines, wormers, microchip, teeth, food and the neutering decision. The timing matters because a young animal's own immunity only gradually takes over from the protection in its mother's milk. The table is a typical European schedule; your vet will adjust it to your animal and region.

## What happens when in a puppy's or kitten's first year?

The table below shows a typical European schedule, which your vet will adjust to your animal and region.

| Age | What usually happens | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Birth to 8 weeks (breeder or shelter) | First worming from 2 weeks (puppies) or 3 weeks (kittens), repeated every two weeks. Often a first vaccine dose at 6 to 8 weeks. | Roundworms pass to the young before birth and through milk. |
| Week 8 to 9 (arrival) | First vet visit within days. Full check, weight, parasite check, breeder's record. First or second core vaccine dose. Worming, flea and tick plan. Insurance. | A baseline exam catches murmurs, hernias, bite problems and parasites early. |
| Week 12 | Second (or third) core dose. Leptospirosis for dogs. Rabies possible from 12 weeks if you will travel. Worming. Microchip if not done. | Core doses repeat every 3 to 4 weeks because maternal antibodies can block earlier ones. |
| Week 16 | Final core dose, at 16 weeks or older. Second leptospirosis dose for dogs. Worming. | The dose most likely to work, as maternal antibodies have faded. |
| Months 4 to 6 | Monthly worming while risk is high. Adult teeth start coming in from about 4 months; check for retained baby teeth. Start brushing. Neutering conversation. | ESCCAP, WSAVA dental guidance and MSD Veterinary Manual. |
| Around 6 months (26 weeks) | Extra core dose at or shortly after 26 weeks. For puppies, an antibody test from 20 weeks that shows protection can replace it. | WSAVA 2024 moved the old 12-to-16-month booster to 26 weeks. |
| Months 7 to 12 | Worming at least four times a year from now on, flea cover year-round, tick cover through tick season. Adult food when your vet says growth is done. | ESCCAP: one to three wormings a year are not enough. |
| 12 to 15 months | First annual health check. In Germany, many vets, such as LMU Munich's small animal clinic, also give a core dose one year after the puppy series. | Move to adult intervals. |

## What happens at a puppy's or kitten's first vet visit?

The vet weighs your animal and checks heart, lungs, mouth, eyes, ears, belly and skin for problems such as hernias, fleas and ear mites.

Bring the vaccination record, worming dates, the current food and a fresh stool sample if you can. The vet will weigh your animal, listen to heart and lungs, look in mouth, eyes and ears, feel the belly, check the umbilicus for a hernia and the testicles in males, and look for fleas and ear mites. Ask about out-of-hours cover now, not the night you need it.

## Which vaccinations do puppies and kittens need?

The core vaccines are distemper, adenovirus and parvovirus for dogs and panleukopenia, herpesvirus and calicivirus for cats, plus rabies where required.

The 2024 WSAVA guidelines name the core vaccines as distemper, adenovirus and parvovirus for dogs, and panleukopenia, herpesvirus and calicivirus for cats. Rabies is core wherever it is endemic, and is needed wherever the law requires it, including for travel within the EU. Leptospirosis should be considered core for dogs where it is common, as in much of Europe; feline leukaemia is core for cats under a year old where the virus is common, and for adult cats with outdoor access.

WSAVA's pattern: first dose from 6 weeks (usually 6 to 8 weeks), repeats every 3 to 4 weeks until a final dose at 16 weeks or older, one more at or shortly after 26 weeks. In Germany, LMU Munich's small animal clinic, drawing on StIKo Vet and WSAVA, vaccinates puppies from (6 to) 8 weeks every 3 to 4 weeks until 16 weeks, then again one year later. Rabies is required for travel within the EU: the vaccine can be given from 12 weeks of age and only counts 21 days after the primary vaccination.

## How often should puppies and kittens be wormed and treated for fleas and ticks?

Worming starts in the first weeks and repeats on the ESCCAP schedule, flea control is year-round, and tick protection covers at least the tick season.

ESCCAP recommends roundworm treatment from 14 days in puppies and 3 weeks in kittens, every two weeks until two weeks after weaning, then monthly to six months where risk stays high. Adults are treated at least four times a year, monthly for hunters, raw feeders and travellers. Flea control is year-round, because heated homes keep fleas breeding in winter; tick protection covers at least the tick season, and all year where ticks stay active.

## Does my puppy or kitten need a microchip?

A chip only helps if it is registered to you, and whether it is compulsory depends on the country, region and species.

A chip only helps if it is registered to you. In Italy, dogs must be chipped and entered in the anagrafe within 60 days of birth, or within days of taking on an older dog (the window is regional: ten days in Lazio). For cats the rules vary by region: registration is voluntary in Lazio, and in Tuscany a cat must be registered once it has a microchip. Germany has no general duty to chip or register, but the Bundestierärztekammer strongly recommends both; registration with FINDEFIX, for example, is free. In Austria, puppies must be chipped by 12 weeks and entered in the Heimtierdatenbank before they are first passed on; other dogs within a month.

## When should I neuter my puppy or kitten?

There is no single right age. Breed, adult size, sex, behaviour and whether you can manage an entire animal safely all change the answer; AAHA's 2019 canine guidance suggests ages by expected adult size and asks vets to tailor the timing to each dog. Raise it at the four-month visit so you have time to think.

## What else should I think about in the first year?

Early socialisation classes, watching the change to adult teeth, brushing early, changing food gradually and, if you want it, insurance in the first month.

WSAVA strongly supports early socialisation, which can begin before the vaccine series is finished: done properly, puppy classes have been shown to be safe with regard to parvovirus. Until the series is complete, avoid places that may be contaminated and allow contact only with healthy puppies and fully vaccinated adult dogs. Adult teeth start coming in at about four months and are all in by about seven months in dogs; a baby tooth still in place beside its adult tooth may need removing. Start brushing early: WSAVA reports periodontal disease in 80% of dogs and 70% of cats by age two, and brushing is the best prevention. Change food over several days, never overnight. If you want insurance, consider taking it out early and check the policy's waiting periods and exclusions for conditions diagnosed before cover starts.
