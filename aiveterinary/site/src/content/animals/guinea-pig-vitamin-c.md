## First, the signs that mean go now

A guinea pig that is short of vitamin C can become seriously ill. The MSD Veterinary Manual warns that without enough vitamin C, guinea pigs can lose their appetite, develop dental problems, have swollen joints, bleed under the skin or in organs, become weak, and possibly die.

Go to a vet immediately, and call ahead, if your guinea pig:

- has stopped eating, or seems unable to pick up its food (see [guinea pig not eating](/animals/guinea-pig-not-eating))
- is limping, or seems weak in its legs
- has swollen joints, or seems to hurt when you touch it
- has unexplained bleeding, for example under the skin
- is weak or very quiet
- has diarrhoea and is eating less

Do not try to treat suspected scurvy at home with a supplement. The MSD Veterinary Manual says treatment includes daily vitamin C by mouth or by injection as directed by a veterinarian, and that the vet will give fluids and feed with a syringe until digestion is normal.

## Why do guinea pigs need vitamin C in their food?

Because, unlike most mammals, guinea pigs cannot make vitamin C themselves, so it has to come from what they eat.

Unusually for mammals, guinea pigs cannot produce vitamin C, the PDSA explains, so they need it from their food. The Merck Veterinary Manual gives the reason: guinea pigs have a faulty (mutated) gene for an enzyme, L-gulonolactone oxidase, that is needed to make vitamin C. Guinea pigs of all ages need vitamin C in their diet, it adds.

According to the MSD Veterinary Manual, vitamin C is essential for healthy skin, joints, blood vessels and other tissues. The MSD Veterinary Manual names vitamin C deficiency (scurvy) as one of the most common nutritional problems in guinea pigs.

## What are the symptoms of vitamin C deficiency (scurvy) in guinea pigs?

A poorer appetite, a rough coat, weight loss, limping, swollen and painful joints, diarrhoea, bleeding and wounds that heal slowly; it can also lead to dental problems.

The PDSA lists tiredness, weight loss and swollen joints as symptoms of scurvy. The MSD Veterinary Manual and the Merck Veterinary Manual add:

- loss of appetite, or difficulty picking up food
- a rough coat
- weight loss
- lameness, or weakness in the legs
- swollen joints and general pain
- diarrhoea
- bleeding under the skin or in organs, because the blood vessels are weakened
- wounds that heal slowly
- dental problems: the Merck Veterinary Manual explains that the teeth can become misaligned because there is not enough collagen to anchor them properly

Some guinea pigs are at greater risk. The Merck Veterinary Manual says growing, pregnant, nursing and sick guinea pigs can show signs of deficiency more quickly than healthy ones. It also lists vitamin C deficiency among the factors that make pneumonia more likely, and notes that eye inflammation (conjunctivitis) can be a sign of it. The PDSA puts it simply: low vitamin C levels can affect a guinea pig's ability to fight infection.

## How do I make sure my guinea pig gets enough vitamin C?

Feed fresh vegetables every day, including some rich in vitamin C, alongside plenty of hay and a small amount of fresh guinea pig pellets.

The PDSA's daily guide, for each guinea pig:

- **hay and/or grass**, which should make up 85–90% of the diet
- **fresh vegetables and greens**: the PDSA recommends one cup of fresh vegetables and greens per guinea pig per day, including some high in vitamin C, with at least five to six different types each day
- **guinea pig pellets**: one tablespoon per guinea pig per day, and the pellets should also contain vitamin C

Fresh water must be available at all times, the MSD Veterinary Manual adds.

Foods high in vitamin C, according to the MSD Veterinary Manual, include bell peppers, oranges, strawberries, broccoli, parsley, red cabbage, turnip greens and kale. The PDSA singles out red bell peppers, and the RSPCA calls fresh vegetables and herbs excellent sources of vitamin C.

Balance matters too:

- The MSD Veterinary Manual advises giving high-calcium greens such as parsley, kale and spinach only now and then, and fruit only in small amounts as a treat.
- The PDSA warns that large amounts of some vegetables, such as cabbage or broccoli, can cause gas.
- Change the diet gradually: the PDSA recommends doing it slowly over two to four weeks.

The MSD Veterinary Manual also suggests talking with your vet about the best vitamin C sources and amounts for your guinea pig.

## Are pellets enough to give my guinea pig vitamin C?

Not on their own: the vitamin C in pellets breaks down over time, faster with heat, light, air and moisture, so pellets must be fresh and stored well, and fresh vegetables are still needed.

- The vitamin C in pellets breaks down over time, faster with heat or sunlight; the PDSA advises storing pellets somewhere dry, cool and dark and using them within three months of purchase.
- The RSPCA adds that vitamin C is destroyed more quickly with exposure to the air, so give a fresh portion of pellets each day rather than topping up the bowl, and make sure they are eaten by the best before date.
- The MSD Veterinary Manual warns that foods with added nutrients can lose much of their vitamin C within a few months.
- Never feed rabbit pellets instead: the MSD Veterinary Manual says they don't have enough vitamin C for guinea pigs. The Merck Veterinary Manual notes that the history of a guinea pig with scurvy can include being fed rabbit pellets, old pellets, or pellets without a stable form of vitamin C.

## Should I add vitamin C drops to my guinea pig's water?

No: the PDSA and the MSD Veterinary Manual both advise against adding vitamin C to the drinking water.

The PDSA explains that sunlight weakens vitamin C in water, and that the taste of the drops can put some guinea pigs off drinking. The MSD Veterinary Manual simply says: don't add supplements to the water.

If you are worried your guinea pig is not getting enough vitamin C, the PDSA suggests a daily vitamin C supplement given in or on the food; ask your vet which one suits your guinea pig. The MSD Veterinary Manual usually advises against multivitamins, because some added vitamins can be harmful to guinea pigs.

A supplement in the food is prevention, not treatment. A guinea pig that already shows signs of scurvy needs a vet.

## When should I take my guinea pig to the vet about vitamin C?

Today, if you notice any sign of scurvy; for a diet check alone, at the next routine visit.

See a vet today if your guinea pig:

- is eating less, or seems to struggle with its food
- has a rough or scruffy coat
- is losing weight
- is limping, or moving less than usual
- has swollen joints
- has wounds that are slow to heal
- has diarrhoea

If your guinea pig is young, pregnant, nursing or ill, ask your vet about its vitamin C, because the Merck Veterinary Manual says these guinea pigs can show signs of deficiency more quickly. Otherwise, the MSD Veterinary Manual recommends a check-up at least once a year with a veterinarian experienced with guinea pigs. Take a list of what your guinea pig eats.

## What will the vet want to know about my guinea pig's diet?

What it eats on a normal day, which pellets you use and how you store them, any supplement, when the signs started, and whether it is young, pregnant or ill.

- What your guinea pig eats on a normal day: hay, which vegetables and roughly how much, and pellets
- Which pellets you use (guinea pig or rabbit pellets), when you opened the bag and where you store it
- Whether you give a vitamin C supplement, and how (in the water or on the food)
- When you first noticed the signs: appetite, weight, coat, walking
- Whether it is young, pregnant, nursing or has another illness
- Any medicine or treatment given at home

If your guinea pig has stopped eating or cannot walk properly, stop reading and call your vet now.
