## First: go now if your cat is straining and passing little or no urine

If your cat keeps going to the litter tray, straining, and **passes little or no urine**, **go to a vet now** and call ahead. International Cat Care says a cat that "is trying and failing to pass urine, or only passing very small amounts of urine" is an emergency and needs to be seen immediately.

This is most urgent in **male cats**. Cornell explains that their urethra (the tube from the bladder) is longer and narrower, so it blocks more easily, and that a cat with a complete blockage can die in "less than twenty-four to forty-eight hours".

Cornell says a blocked cat passes little or no urine and becomes more and more distressed. Our article on [a cat straining in the litter box](/animals/cat-straining-litter-box) explains how to tell a blocked bladder from constipation.

## What is FLUTD in cats?

FLUTD (feline lower urinary tract disease) is an umbrella term for problems of the bladder and urethra. It describes the signs, not the cause.

International Cat Care says FLUTD "is not a diagnosis and has several causes that will need investigating by your veterinary clinic". Cornell describes lower urinary tract disease as a range of disorders, from mild to serious, that affect the bladder and urethra. Cornell says surveys suggest it is the number one reason cats are taken to the vet, and International Cat Care says it affects about 1 to 3% of cats each year.

So a vet saying your cat has "FLUTD" or "lower urinary tract signs" is the start: the next step is finding out why.

## What are the signs of FLUTD?

Straining, going often but passing only a little, crying while weeing, blood in the urine, weeing outside the tray, and licking the genitals.

International Cat Care and Cornell list:

- straining, or spending a long time in the litter tray
- going more often, passing small amounts each time
- crying out while weeing
- blood in the urine, which International Cat Care says may only show up on a vet's urine test
- weeing outside the litter tray (see [cat peeing outside the litter box](/animals/cat-peeing-outside-litter-box))
- licking or overgrooming the belly or genitals, sometimes with hair loss
- changes in behaviour, such as irritability or playing less (International Cat Care)

A cat that passes **nothing**, or only drops, may be blocked: go now.

## What causes FLUTD?

Most often stress cystitis (feline idiopathic cystitis), then bladder stones, urethral plugs, and less often infection, birth defects or cancer.

International Cat Care gives these causes:

- **Feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC):** the most common cause. International Cat Care says around 60 to 70% of cases have no underlying disease that can be found, and that stress plays a major role. Cornell and the MSD Veterinary Manual also name FIC as the most common diagnosis. See our article on [stress cystitis in cats](/animals/cat-cystitis-stress).
- **Bladder stones:** around 10 to 15% of FLUTD cases. Struvite and calcium oxalate are the most common types (International Cat Care, Cornell).
- **Urethral plugs:** a soft build-up of protein, cells, crystals and debris, mainly a cause of blockage in male cats.
- **Bacterial infection:** relatively uncommon in cats, and seen more in older cats or cats with conditions such as kidney disease or diabetes. International Cat Care says most cases of FLUTD do not need antibiotics.
- **Birth defects** of the urinary tract, and, uncommonly, a **tumour** of the bladder or urethra, particularly in older cats.

A cat can have more than one of these at once (Cornell).

## Which cats get FLUTD?

Any cat can, but it is more common in middle-aged, neutered, overweight and indoor cats that exercise little.

International Cat Care says FLUTD can affect cats of any age, breed or sex, but is more common in middle-aged, neutered and overweight cats, cats that take little exercise, cats with little or no access outdoors, and cats fed a dry diet. Cornell adds low water intake, being male, living with other cats, and changes in routine such as moving house. Male cats are the ones most at risk of a blockage.

## How will the vet find out what is causing it?

Usually with an examination and a urine test first, then if needed blood tests, X-rays or an ultrasound scan.

- **Urine test:** from a sample in the litter tray or taken straight from the bladder with a fine needle, which International Cat Care says most cats barely notice. Cornell says the examination and urine test come first.
- **X-rays and ultrasound:** these show many stones, and ultrasound shows a thickened bladder wall (International Cat Care).
- **Blood tests:** to check general health, for example the kidneys.
- **Urine culture:** to look for infection (Cornell, MSD).
- **Biopsy:** a small tissue sample, especially if a tumour is suspected (International Cat Care).

The MSD Veterinary Manual explains that FIC is diagnosed by ruling out the other causes, which is why your vet may suggest several tests.

## How is FLUTD treated?

It depends on the cause: a blockage is cleared as an emergency, stones may be dissolved with a diet or removed, and stress cystitis is managed at home with your vet.

- **Blockage:** Cornell describes passing a thin tube (catheter) under sedation or anaesthetic, with fluids and other care in hospital for a few days or longer.
- **Stones:** Cornell says struvite stones can often be dissolved with a special diet, with surgery if that fails; calcium oxalate stones cannot be dissolved by diet and may need to be flushed out or removed surgically.
- **FIC:** treatment aims to prevent flare-ups, with more water, less stress and changes to the home (Cornell, MSD).

International Cat Care says every cat with FLUTD needs to drink more and wee more often. Don't change your cat's diet, or add urinary products, without your vet: Cornell warns that adding urine acidifiers without veterinary advice can do harm, and that diet changes can trigger FIC flare-ups in some cats.

## Can FLUTD come back, and how can I help prevent it?

It can. Most cats have only occasional episodes, but some have repeated flare-ups, blockages or stones. More water, healthy weight and less stress help.

Cornell says most cats rarely have another episode or have only occasional ones, but some have frequent recurrences, and cats that have had stones are at higher risk. To help, International Cat Care and Cornell suggest:

- wet food rather than dry, if your vet agrees, and fresh water in several places
- keeping your cat at a healthy weight, with play to encourage exercise
- one more litter tray than the number of cats, kept clean, in quiet, safe places
- keeping changes to routine, diet and feeding times small
- climbing places and chase toys to reduce stress

International Cat Care says that in less severe cases, most cats can live a fairly normal life with these changes and veterinary care.

## When should I take my cat to the vet?

Now if your cat is straining and passing little or no urine; within a day for any other urinary signs.

- **Go now:** straining with little or no urine, or crying and distress in the tray, especially in a male cat.
- **Today:** blood in the urine, going to the tray more often, weeing outside the tray, or licking the genitals, even if your cat is still passing urine. Cornell says stones and plugs are causes of a blockage.
- **Follow-up:** keep any check-ups and urine tests your vet books after an episode.

If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know about my cat's urinary problem?

When you last saw your cat pass urine, what the signs are and how long they have gone on, past episodes, diet and any changes at home.

- when your cat last passed urine, and roughly how much
- how often it goes to the tray, and whether it strains or cries
- any blood in the urine, or wee outside the tray
- how long the signs have been going on, and whether it has had them before
- whether it is eating, drinking and has vomited
- what it eats (wet, dry or both) and how much it drinks
- any recent changes at home: moving, new pets, building work, visitors
- any medicines it takes, and any past bladder stones or blockages
