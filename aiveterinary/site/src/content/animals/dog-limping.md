## First, the signs that mean go now

Go to a veterinary clinic immediately, and call ahead, if your dog:

- cannot put any weight on the leg at all, or is holding it up constantly
- has suddenly become severely lame: the MSD Veterinary Manual lists sudden, severe lameness among the signs that need a vet immediately
- has a leg that looks misshapen, is held at an odd angle, or looks shorter than the other
- has been hit by a car, even if the limp seems mild: the PDSA warns of internal injuries you cannot see from the outside
- has a serious wound, for example one that is bleeding heavily or shows bone
- suddenly cannot use its back legs, or has become wobbly
- is crying out in pain when moved, or when the leg or back is touched

Not all limps start in the leg: a front-leg limp can come from the neck or spine. Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center advises calling your vet at the first sign of back trouble, because the longer a damaged spinal cord waits, the less likely it is to recover.

## Can I watch my dog's limp at home?

Only while you get in touch with your vet, who should see any limp, even a minor-looking one, and within 24 hours if it has not gone. Meanwhile, keep your dog still, check the paw and do not give human painkillers.

Call your vet about any limp, even one that seems mild: the PDSA points out that some dogs are very good at hiding pain. The MSD Veterinary Manual advises seeing a vet within 24 hours for a limp that lasts more than 24 hours, and Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center adds that pain that is severe, sudden or getting worse is not something to wait on.

What you can do at home:

- Check the paw first. Look between the toes and pads for cuts, grass seeds, thorns, grit, torn nails or anything stuck. The PDSA counts a grass seed or other object in the foot, and claw injuries, among the common causes of a limp.
- Limit your dog's movement while you call the vet: the RSPCA advises keeping a dog that may have a fracture indoors, for example in a crate, and the PDSA says not to let a dog walk on a leg that may be broken.
- Do not give human painkillers. Ibuprofen and paracetamol can be dangerous for dogs. If your dog needs pain relief, that is a vet decision and a prescription.
- Film the limp if you can. Some dogs walk normally once they reach the clinic, and Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center calls videos from home extremely helpful.

## When should I take my limping dog to the vet?

Call your vet about any limp: without delay if the pain is severe, sudden or getting worse, within 24 hours if the limp has lasted more than 24 hours, and book in if it keeps coming back or comes with swelling, stiffness or licking.

Contact your vet today if:

- the pain seems severe, came on suddenly, or is getting worse
- the limp has lasted more than 24 hours, even if it seems mild

Book an appointment with your vet if:

- the limp keeps coming back
- a leg or joint looks swollen
- your dog is stiff after rest, slow to get up, or reluctant to jump or climb stairs
- your dog is licking one spot on the leg repeatedly
- your dog is older and the limp is new

## What will the vet want to know about my dog's limp?

Which leg, when it started and what happened, how it changes with rest and exercise, any swelling or wounds, your dog's exercise, past injuries, medicines and travel.

- Which leg, and whether it is always the same one
- When it started, and whether anything happened (a jump, a slip, a game of fetch)
- Whether it is worse after rest or after exercise
- Whether it is getting better, worse, or staying the same
- Any swelling, wounds, licking or heat you have noticed
- Your dog's usual exercise, and any recent increase
- Any previous injuries, surgery or joint problems
- Any medication or supplements

## Why is my dog limping?

Paw injuries, sprains, torn nails, muscle injuries, arthritis and growing pains in young dogs are likely causes; cruciate rupture, fractures, dysplasia, joint infection, bone tumours and disc disease are less common but important.

| Likely | Less common but important |
|---|---|
| Cut, thorn or grass seed in the paw | Cruciate ligament rupture in the knee |
| Sprain or strain from exercise | Fracture or dislocation |
| Torn or broken nail | Hip or elbow dysplasia in young dogs |
| Muscle injury | Joint infection (septic arthritis) |
| Arthritis in older dogs | Bone tumour |
| Growing pains (panosteitis) in young dogs | Disc disease in the neck or back |

Your vet decides which of these is likely for *your* dog, based on the examination and, where needed, X-rays or other tests. This list is to help you understand the conversation, not to diagnose.

If your dog's limp lasts more than 24 hours, book in with your own vet rather than waiting for it to resolve on its own.
