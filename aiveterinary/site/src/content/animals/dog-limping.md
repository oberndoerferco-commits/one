## First, the signs that mean go now

Go to a veterinary clinic immediately, and call ahead, if your dog:

- cannot put any weight on the leg at all, or is holding it up constantly
- has a leg that looks bent, twisted, or is dangling at an odd angle
- has been hit by a car, fallen from a height, or been in a fight, even if the limp seems mild (internal injuries can show later)
- has a wound that is bleeding heavily, or where bone is visible
- has a hot, swollen limb and is also quiet, off food or feverish
- is dragging the back legs, or has suddenly become wobbly on more than one leg
- is crying out in pain when moved, or when the leg or back is touched
- is a large-breed puppy that is suddenly very lame and reluctant to move

A dog that limps on a front leg and also has a swollen or painful neck or back should be seen urgently too; not all limps start in the leg.

## Can I watch my dog's limp at home?

Yes, for a couple of days, if your dog is still bearing some weight, bright, eating and comfortable at rest: check the paw, feel the leg and rest your dog properly.

A dog that is limping but still bearing some weight, bright, eating and comfortable at rest can usually be watched for a couple of days.

What you can do at home:

- Check the paw first. Look between the toes and pads for cuts, grass seeds, thorns, grit, torn nails or anything stuck. Many limps are a paw problem.
- Feel gently up the leg from the toes to the shoulder or hip, comparing with the other side. Note any swelling, heat, or a spot where your dog flinches. Stop if your dog growls or seems distressed.
- Rest your dog properly: short lead walks only to toilet, no running, no jumping on and off furniture, no stairs if you can manage it. Many vets recommend several days of strict rest.
- Do not give human painkillers. Ibuprofen and paracetamol can be dangerous for dogs. If your dog needs pain relief, that is a vet decision and a prescription.
- Film the limp if you can. Limps often disappear in the consulting room, and a short video helps your vet see which leg is affected and how badly.

## When should I take my limping dog to the vet?

This week or sooner if the limp has not improved after two or three days of rest, keeps coming back, or comes with swelling, heat, stiffness or licking.

Book an appointment this week, or sooner, if:

- the limp has not improved after two or three days of rest
- the limp comes back as soon as your dog is active again
- your dog is limping on and off over several weeks
- there is swelling, heat, or a lump on the leg
- your dog is stiff after rest, slow to get up, or reluctant to jump or climb stairs
- your dog is licking one spot on the leg repeatedly
- your dog is a puppy or young large-breed dog with any lameness that lasts more than a day or two
- your dog is older and the limp is new

## What will the vet want to know about my dog's limp?

Which leg, when it started and what happened, how it changes with rest and exercise, any swelling or wounds, your dog's exercise, past injuries, medicines and travel.

- Which leg, and whether it is always the same one
- When it started, and whether anything happened (a jump, a slip, a game of fetch)
- Whether it is worse after rest or after exercise
- Whether it is getting better, worse, or staying the same
- Any swelling, wounds, licking or heat you have noticed
- Your dog's usual exercise, and any recent increase
- Any previous injuries, surgery or joint problems
- Any medication or supplements
- Any tick bites, or travel abroad

## Why is my dog limping?

Paw injuries, sprains, torn nails, bruising, arthritis and pad burns are likely causes; cruciate rupture, fractures, dysplasia, bone disease, disc disease and tick-borne disease are less common but important.

| Likely | Less common but important |
|---|---|
| Cut, thorn or grass seed in the paw | Cruciate ligament rupture in the knee |
| Sprain or strain from exercise | Fracture or dislocation |
| Torn or broken nail | Hip or elbow dysplasia in young dogs |
| Bruising from a knock | Bone infection or bone cancer |
| Arthritis in older dogs | Disc disease in the neck or back |
| Pad burn from hot pavements | Tick-borne disease |

Your vet decides which of these is likely for *your* dog, based on the examination and, where needed, X-rays or other tests. This list is to help you understand the conversation, not to diagnose.

If your dog's limp is not clearly improving within a few days of rest, book in with your own vet rather than waiting for it to resolve on its own.
