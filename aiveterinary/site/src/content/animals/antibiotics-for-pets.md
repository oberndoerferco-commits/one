## When does my pet actually need antibiotics?

Only when a bacterial infection is likely, because antibiotics kill bacteria and do nothing against viruses.

Antibiotics kill bacteria or stop them multiplying. They do nothing against viruses, which cause many infections, and not every bacterial infection needs one either. That is why your vet will not always prescribe one, and why the European companion animal veterinary associations ask owners not to request antibiotics "just in case". Many wound and skin infections settle with local wound care and antibacterial washes alone.

When a bacterial infection is likely, your vet may want a sample first: a swab, a urine sample, a skin scrape. Growing the bacteria in the laboratory shows which antibiotic will actually work, which is better for your animal and for everyone else, because every unnecessary or half-hearted course teaches bacteria to resist.

## Why do I have to finish the whole course of antibiotics?

Because too little, too rarely, or stopping early leaves the toughest bacteria alive, so your pet can relapse with an infection that is harder to treat.

The prescription states a dose, a number of times a day and a number of days. All three matter. Too little, too rarely, or stopped early leaves the toughest bacteria alive; your pet seems better, then relapses, and the second infection is harder to treat. Resistant bacteria also pass between pets and people in the same household.

So:

- Give every dose, at the times written, until the pack or the prescribed days are finished, even if your pet looks completely well after two days.
- Do not change the dose yourself and do not stop early without talking to your vet.
- If your pet is not improving within the time your vet mentioned, or is getting worse, call. Do not simply carry on.
- Keep any follow-up appointment that was booked.

## How do I give my pet an antibiotic tablet?

Hide it in a small piece of food if it can be given with food, or place it far back on the tongue, and always check that it actually went down.

- Ask whether the tablet can be given with food. Many can, and hiding it in a small piece of something irresistible, given before the main meal, works for most dogs. Some antibiotics work better on an empty stomach or must not be crushed; the label or your vet will tell you.
- Check that the tablet actually went down. Dogs and cats are expert at swallowing the treat and spitting out the pill a minute later.
- For cats, and for dogs that chew around the pill, place it directly: tilt the head gently upward, open the mouth, put the tablet far back on the tongue, close the mouth and stroke the throat until you see a swallow. Then give a little water, for example from a syringe into the side of the mouth, so the tablet does not stick in the throat, and a treat as a reward.
- A soft treat moulded around the tablet helps, and so can a pill giver if your vet shows you how to use it, because used wrongly it can injure the mouth. Hands behind the head, calm voice, short session.

## How do I give my pet a liquid antibiotic?

Draw up the dose in the syringe provided, slide the tip into the corner of the mouth behind the canine tooth, and press slowly so your pet can swallow between squirts. Aim towards the cheek, not straight into the throat, so the liquid does not go into the lungs. Shake the bottle if the label says so, and store it as instructed: some liquids belong in the fridge, and once made up they keep only for the time stated on the label.

## What should I do if I miss a dose of my pet's antibiotic?

Do not double the next dose; call your vet or the clinic and ask what to do with this particular medicine.

It happens. Do not double the next dose to catch up, and do not guess. Call your vet or the clinic and ask what to do with this particular medicine, because the right answer depends on the drug and how late you are.

## Which antibiotic side effects should I report to the vet?

Call the clinic about vomiting, diarrhoea or refusing food, signs of an allergic reaction such as facial swelling, rash or difficulty breathing, or anything else that worries you.

Most courses pass without trouble. Call the clinic if you see:

- vomiting, diarrhoea or refusing food
- swelling of the face, skin rash, fever, difficulty breathing (stop the antibiotic and call or go now)
- any sign that worries you, even if it is not on this list

Apart from an allergic reaction, do not stop the antibiotic on your own; your vet may switch it, adjust how it is given, or add something to settle the stomach. Side effects, and treatments that did not work, are worth reporting because that information feeds the official monitoring of veterinary medicines.

## Can I reuse leftover antibiotics for my pet?

No: ask your clinic how to dispose of them, and never keep them for next time, give them to another pet or take them yourself.

If you finish the course as prescribed there should be nothing left. If there is, because the vet stopped it early or changed drugs, ask your clinic how to dispose of it. Never keep it for next time, never give it to another pet, and never take it yourself. The antibiotic that was right for last year's skin infection may be wrong, and harmful, for this year's ear or bladder, and a few days of the wrong drug makes the next infection harder for everyone to treat.

## What hygiene precautions should I take while my pet has an infection?

Wash your hands after handling a pet with an infection, and wear gloves and clean your hands before and after dealing with wounds or cleaning ears. If young children or anyone with a weakened immune system lives with you, ask your vet which extra precautions apply.

Which antibiotic, how long, and with or without food are decisions for the vet who examined your animal, so when in doubt about a dose, a delay or a side effect, ring your own vet rather than searching for a rule.
