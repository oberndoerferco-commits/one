## When does my pet actually need antibiotics?

Only when a bacterial infection is likely, because antibiotics kill bacteria and do nothing against viruses.

Antibiotics kill bacteria or stop them multiplying. They do nothing against viruses, and nothing against most of the things that make pets itch, cough, vomit or have diarrhoea for a few days. That is why your vet will not always prescribe one, and why the European companion animal veterinary associations ask owners not to request antibiotics "just in case". Many skin and wound problems settle with cleaning and local treatment alone; many gut upsets settle with time and a bland diet.

When a bacterial infection is likely, your vet may want a sample first: a swab, a urine sample, a skin scrape. Growing the bacteria in the laboratory shows which antibiotic will actually work, which is better for your animal and for everyone else, because every unnecessary or half-hearted course teaches bacteria to resist.

## Why do I have to finish the whole course of antibiotics?

Because too little, too rarely, or stopping early leaves the toughest bacteria alive, so your pet can relapse with an infection that is harder to treat.

The prescription states a dose, a number of times a day and a number of days. All three matter. Too little, too rarely, or stopped early leaves the toughest bacteria alive; your pet seems better, then relapses, and the second infection is harder to treat. Resistant bacteria also pass between pets and people in the same household.

So:

- Give every dose, at the times written, until the pack or the prescribed days are finished, even if your pet looks completely well after two days.
- Do not change the dose yourself and do not stop early without talking to your vet.
- If your pet is not improving within the time your vet mentioned, or is getting worse, call. Do not simply carry on.
- Keep the follow-up appointment if one was booked; some infections are checked with a second sample before treatment ends.

## How do I give my pet an antibiotic tablet?

Hide it in a small piece of food if it can be given with food, or place it far back on the tongue, and always check that it actually went down.

- Ask whether the tablet can be given with food. Many can, and hiding it in a small piece of something irresistible, given before the main meal, works for most dogs. Some antibiotics work better on an empty stomach or must not be crushed; the label or your vet will tell you.
- Check that the tablet actually went down. Dogs and cats are expert at swallowing the treat and spitting out the pill a minute later.
- For cats, and for dogs that chew around the pill, place it directly: tilt the head gently upward, open the mouth, put the tablet far back on the tongue, close the mouth and stroke the throat until you see a swallow. Follow with a little water or a treat, because a tablet stuck in the throat can hurt and may not be absorbed.
- Pill-giving syringes and soft pill pockets help. Hands behind the head, calm voice, short session.

## How do I give my pet a liquid antibiotic?

Draw up the dose in the syringe provided, slide the tip into the corner of the mouth behind the canine tooth, and press slowly so your pet can swallow between squirts. Aim towards the cheek, not the throat, to avoid choking. Shake the bottle if the label says so, and store it as instructed, since some liquids live in the fridge and expire within days of being made up.

## What should I do if I miss a dose of my pet's antibiotic?

Do not double the next dose; call your vet or the clinic and ask what to do with this particular medicine.

It happens. Do not double the next dose to catch up, and do not guess. Call your vet or the clinic and ask what to do with this particular medicine, because the right answer depends on the drug and how late you are.

## Which antibiotic side effects should I report to the vet?

Call the clinic about vomiting, diarrhoea or refusing food lasting more than a day, facial swelling or difficulty breathing, unusual tiredness or yellow gums, or anything else that worries you.

Most courses pass without trouble. Call the clinic if you see:

- vomiting, diarrhoea or refusing food that lasts more than a day
- swelling of the face, hives, sudden itching, difficulty breathing (stop and go now)
- unusual tiredness, wobbliness, or yellow gums or eyes
- any sign that worries you, even if it is not on this list

Do not stop the antibiotic on your own; your vet may switch it, adjust how it is given, or add something to settle the stomach. Side effects, and treatments that did not work, are worth reporting because that information feeds the official monitoring of veterinary medicines.

## Can I reuse leftover antibiotics for my pet?

No: take them back to the clinic or a pharmacy, and never keep them for next time, give them to another pet or take them yourself.

If you finish the course as prescribed there should be nothing left. If there is, because the vet stopped it early or changed drugs, take it back to the clinic or a pharmacy. Never keep it for next time, never give it to another pet, and never take it yourself. The antibiotic that was right for last year's skin infection may be wrong, and harmful, for this year's ear or bladder, and a few days of the wrong drug makes the next infection harder for everyone to treat.

## What hygiene precautions should I take while my pet has an infection?

Wash your hands after handling a pet with an infection and after touching wounds, ears or bedding, and use gloves for dressings. Keep an infected animal off the bed and away from young children and anyone with a weakened immune system until your vet says the infection is under control.

Which antibiotic, how long, and with or without food are decisions for the vet who examined your animal, so when in doubt about a dose, a delay or a side effect, ring your own vet rather than searching for a rule.
