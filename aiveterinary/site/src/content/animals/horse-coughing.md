## First, the signs that mean go now

A cough that doesn't go away needs a vet visit, and some signs mean you need the vet now. Call your vet straight away if your horse is coughing and:

- breathes fast or with effort at rest, with flared nostrils, noisy breathing, its head stretched out, or its belly muscles working hard to breathe out
- has food material coming out of its nose, drools, or stretches and arches its neck, especially if it started while eating
- has swollen glands under the jaw or at the throat, thick nasal discharge or difficulty swallowing
- has a high temperature and is dull, weak or off its food, especially after a long journey
- shows pain when breathing, or doesn't want to move or lie down

These signs come from the MSD Veterinary Manual and the University of Cambridge's equine hospital. Food coming out of the nose is the classic sign of **choke**, a blockage of the gullet; the MSD Veterinary Manual names inhaling food, which causes pneumonia, as its main complication. Swollen glands with fever can mean **strangles**, and the Cambridge factsheet says to contact your vet immediately and isolate the horse if you suspect it. Fever with painful, shallow breathing after a long journey can mean **pleuropneumonia** (shipping fever), and the MSD Veterinary Manual stresses that treatment delayed by more than 48 hours can lead to a poor outcome.

If your horse may be choking, the MSD Veterinary Manual says to move it away from food and water, or remove them from the stable, until your vet says otherwise.

## Why is my horse coughing?

Dust and allergy in the airways (equine asthma) and infections such as flu or strangles are among the causes. Choke and pleuropneumonia can also cause coughing, and both are urgent.

The MSD Veterinary Manual lists coughing, dry or with mucus or blood, among the signs of lung and airway disease, alongside nasal discharge, fast breathing at rest and laboured breathing.

| Possible cause | Typical clues |
|---|---|
| Equine asthma (recurrent airway obstruction, heaves) | cough and nasal discharge, worse when stabled, on straw or fed hay (MSD Veterinary Manual) |
| Inflammatory airway disease | chronic cough and poorer performance in athletic horses (MSD Veterinary Manual) |
| Equine influenza | high fever, clear nasal discharge, dry harsh cough, spreads fast between horses (MSD Veterinary Manual) |
| Strangles | fever, thick nasal discharge, swollen glands under the jaw (Cambridge Equine Hospital) |
| Choke | food coming out of the nose, drooling, neck stretched out (MSD Veterinary Manual) |
| Pleuropneumonia (shipping fever) | fever, painful shallow breathing, often after a long journey (MSD Veterinary Manual) |

This table is to help you follow the conversation with your vet, not to diagnose your horse.

## What is equine asthma?

An allergic airway disease, usually triggered by dust from hay and bedding. It is managed mainly by changing the horse's environment.

- **What it is.** The MSD Veterinary Manual describes equine asthma, sometimes called heaves, as a common respiratory disease of horses. It is thought to be an allergic reaction to long-term exposure to allergens, such as mould and dust in hay in poorly ventilated stables, and it inflames and narrows the lower airways. The manual's page on recurrent airway obstruction (heaves) compares it to asthma in people.
- **Triggers.** According to the MSD Veterinary Manual, the most common culprits are organic dusts in hay, and episodes tend to occur when susceptible horses are stabled, bedded on straw or fed hay.
- **Signs.** The MSD Veterinary Manual lists chronic cough, nasal discharge, breathing difficulty, flared nostrils, rapid breathing, wheezing when breathing out, belly muscles working hard to push air out, and poor performance during exercise. Mildly affected horses may show few signs at rest.
- **Inflammatory airway disease.** The MSD Veterinary Manual says it occurs in up to 50% of athletic horses, with a chronic cough, nasal discharge and poor tolerance of strenuous exercise, and often follows a viral infection.

## Is my horse's cough contagious?

It can be. Flu and strangles spread quickly between horses, so keep a coughing horse apart until your vet knows the cause.

- **Equine influenza.** The MSD Veterinary Manual calls it highly contagious: it spreads rapidly, by breathing in respiratory secretions. Signs appear about 1 to 3 days after exposure: a high fever (up to 41 °C), a clear nasal discharge, swollen lymph nodes and a dry, harsh cough, often with dullness, loss of appetite and weakness. Horses aged 1 to 5 years are the most susceptible. In uncomplicated cases the signs usually last less than 3 days, but the cough can persist for several weeks.
- **Strangles.** The MSD Veterinary Manual describes it as a highly contagious bacterial disease (*Streptococcus equi equi*) with a low death rate in otherwise healthy horses. The Cambridge factsheet lists a high temperature, often 39 to 40 °C, as usually the first sign, then dullness, nasal discharge that turns to thick pus, swollen glands under the jaw that may form abscesses and burst, cough and difficulty swallowing.
- **Silent carriers.** Some horses keep carrying strangles bacteria after they recover, without signs. The Cambridge factsheet says up to 10% of recovered horses may become carriers.

## How do I protect my other horses?

Separate the coughing horse, check the temperature of the others, and keep people and equipment apart until your vet advises otherwise.

- The MSD Veterinary Manual says a horse should be isolated as soon as strangles is suspected. The Cambridge factsheet advises isolating confirmed cases and in-contact horses, stopping all movement on and off the yard, and checking the rectal temperature of in-contact horses twice a day.
- The Cambridge factsheet recommends designated people and equipment for infected horses, gloves, and warning vets, farriers, dentists and feed merchants before they visit.
- For new arrivals, the MSD Veterinary Manual says isolating horses new to a property for 2 weeks reduces exposure to influenza. The Cambridge factsheet says a new horse that has had no fever or other signs of illness for 3 to 4 weeks should be safe to mix, although a silent carrier can still bring strangles in.
- The MSD Veterinary Manual notes that paddocks and stables used by horses with strangles should be regarded as contaminated for about 2 months.
- Ask your vet about vaccination against influenza and strangles. The Cambridge factsheet points out that the strangles vaccine reduces signs but is not a substitute for good stable management.

## What can I do at home for a coughing horse?

Let the vet find the cause first. Then cut the dust, give the rest your vet recommends, and don't give cough remedies or leftover medicines unless the vet agrees.

- **Less dust.** For equine asthma, the MSD Veterinary Manual calls environmental management to reduce allergen exposure the single most important treatment: keep the horse at pasture with fresh grass where possible, don't store hay overhead, avoid straw bedding and be aware that round-bale hay is particularly allergenic. Soaking hay before feeding may control signs in mildly affected horses.
- **Rest after flu.** The MSD Veterinary Manual advises resting a horse 1 week for every day of fever, with a minimum of 3 weeks. Your vet will tell you when your horse can go back to work.
- **No medicines without the vet.** Which medicines a vet may use for your horse depends on its passport: in the EU, horses are deemed to be intended for the food chain unless the passport irreversibly excludes them (Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/963, Article 38), the vet must check this before treating (Article 39), and the status decides which rules on medicines apply (Regulation (EU) 2019/6, Article 112(4)).

## When should I call the vet about a cough?

Today if the cough comes with fever, discharge, swollen glands or other ill horses; book a visit for any cough that lasts more than a day or two.

Call the vet **today** if:

- your horse has a temperature above its normal range (see [horse vital signs](/animals/horse-vital-signs)), nasal discharge or swollen glands
- several horses on the yard are coughing, or a new horse has arrived recently
- the cough started after a long journey or a show
- there is blood or pus in the nasal discharge

**Book a visit** if:

- the cough lasts more than a day or two: the MSD Veterinary Manual lists coughing among the signs that call for a veterinary examination if they last that long
- your horse coughs when ridden or worked, tires more easily or performs less well, which the MSD Veterinary Manual links with equine asthma and inflammatory airway disease
- the cough gets worse in the stable, with hay or with straw bedding

## What will the vet want to know about my horse's cough?

When and how it coughs, its temperature, any discharge, how it eats and swallows, its stable and feeding, contact with other horses, vaccinations and any medicine.

- when the cough started, how often it happens, and whether it is dry or brings up mucus
- when it coughs: at rest, while eating, at the start of exercise, in the stable or at grass
- your horse's temperature, measured twice a day if you can do so safely
- any nasal discharge, and its colour: clear, thick and yellow, or with blood
- whether it is eating normally, has trouble swallowing or has swollen glands
- what hay it eats and how it is stored and fed, the bedding, and how much time it spends in the stable
- whether other horses on the yard are coughing or ill, and any recent new arrivals, shows or long journeys
- its age and its vaccination record
- any medicine it has had, and its passport with its food-chain status
