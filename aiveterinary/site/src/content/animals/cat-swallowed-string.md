## First: call your vet now, and go straight in if you see these signs

If your cat has swallowed string, thread, wool, a hair tie, an elastic band, ribbon or tinsel, or you think it might have, **call your vet now**, even if your cat seems fine. The PDSA's advice is: "Never wait to see what happens." Signs may not appear straight away.

Go straight to the nearest clinic, and ring on the way, if your cat:

- has **string, thread or tinsel hanging from its mouth**, or from its bottom: **don't pull it**
- is **vomiting** or retching
- is **struggling to breathe** (Cats Protection: seek veterinary help immediately for any breathing problem)
- is **drooling**, smacking its lips or swallowing again and again
- has **stopped eating** or drinking
- is **lethargic**, hiding, growling or doesn't want to be touched
- is straining or **not pooing**
- may have swallowed a **sewing needle** with the thread

## Why is string so dangerous for cats?

One end can get caught, often under the tongue or in the stomach, while the gut keeps pulling on the rest, so the intestine bunches up and can be cut through.

The MSD Veterinary Manual explains that long, thin objects such as string, yarn or fabric can get caught around the base of the tongue. When the rest reaches the intestine, the gut's normal movements make the string saw into the gut wall. That can make a hole, which leads to infection inside the tummy. International Cat Care says string-like objects can make the intestines bunch up and usually need surgery to remove.

The Cornell Feline Health Center gives tinsel as an example: a piece wrapped around the base of the tongue "acts like a piano wire" and can perforate the intestines, which it calls "very life-threatening".

According to the PDSA, string, thread, hair bands, elastic bands and tinsel are the items that most often cause a gut blockage in cats. International Cat Care adds wool, ribbon, dental floss, blind cords and the string around meat.

## Should I pull the string out of my cat's mouth or bottom?

No: never pull a string, thread or piece of tinsel you can see. Leave it where it is and get your cat to the vet.

The PDSA says it plainly: if string is hanging from your cat's mouth, "never pull it". The professional version of the MSD manual says a string caught under the tongue must never be pulled; the vet cuts it free at the point where it is caught.

None of the sources we used deals separately with a string hanging from the bottom. But because the other end may still be caught under the tongue or in the stomach, the same applies: don't pull it, and go to the vet.

## Should I try to make my cat vomit?

No: don't try to make your cat sick at home unless your vet specifically tells you to.

That is the PDSA's advice. The ASPCA says there is nothing that can be given at home to make a cat vomit safely.

Even at the clinic, making a cat vomit is not always possible. The PDSA says vets only do it if the object hasn't yet moved on from the stomach, and not if it is sharp or could catch in the gut. String can catch in the gut, as explained above.

## My cat seems fine. Can I wait and see if the string comes out?

No: don't wait. Swallowed string often doesn't pass, and cats hide illness well.

International Cat Care advises not to assume that a swallowed object will pass through, because they often don't. It also points out that cats often hide illness: an affected cat may just sleep more or seem less interested in you. The PDSA warns that symptoms might not appear straight away, and that a cat could become very ill or even die without treatment.

## What will the vet do?

Usually look under the tongue, take X-rays or do an ultrasound scan, and remove the string, most often by surgery.

- **Mouth check.** The professional version of the MSD manual tells vets to look under the tongue, where string is often caught.
- **Tests.** X-rays and an ultrasound scan of the tummy (International Cat Care, PDSA), and often blood tests (MSD Veterinary Manual).
- **Removal.** The MSD Veterinary Manual says some objects can be removed from the stomach with an endoscope, a camera tube passed through the mouth, but surgery is needed if that isn't possible or the object is in the intestine. The professional version adds that endoscopy should not be used when a string-like object is suspected. International Cat Care says surgery is commonly needed. The PDSA says part of the intestine may need removing.
- **Supportive care.** A fluid drip, pain relief and antibiotics (PDSA).

**Recovery.** The PDSA says your cat will stay in hospital until it is recovering and eating. At home, give all the medicines you are prescribed, offer several small meals a day, give your cat a warm, quiet place to rest, and go back for the check-ups your vet asks for.

**Outlook.** The PDSA says cats treated promptly, without complications, are likely to recover well, although some don't recover even with prompt treatment. The MSD Veterinary Manual also says many cats recover well when the problem is diagnosed and treated quickly.

## How can I stop my cat swallowing string?

Put thread, wool, string, hair ties and Christmas decorations away, and check toys for loose parts.

- Keep thread, wool, string, hair bobbles and Christmas decorations out of reach (PDSA).
- Don't let your cat play with anything small enough to swallow, and check toys for parts that are coming loose (PDSA).
- Avoid long ribbon ties on presents, especially under the tree (Cornell Feline Health Center).
- Give your cat safe toys, regular play and things to do. International Cat Care says indoor cats without enough stimulation may be more likely to eat things that aren't food, and that young, curious cats are more likely to.

## When should I take my cat to the vet?

Straight away if you know or suspect your cat has swallowed string, thread, a hair tie or tinsel, even if it seems well.

Also see your vet if you didn't see it happen but your cat has signs that could mean a gut blockage. International Cat Care and the PDSA list:

- vomiting, retching or nausea
- refusing food
- lethargy or sleeping more
- tummy pain
- diarrhoea, or pooing less
- weight loss

For vomiting that happens now and then in an otherwise well cat, see our article on [cat vomiting](/animals/cat-vomiting). If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know about what my cat swallowed?

What it was, how long, when, whether a needle was attached, and how your cat has been since.

- what your cat swallowed (string, thread, wool, a hair tie, ribbon, tinsel) and roughly how long a piece is missing
- whether a sewing needle, a toy or anything else was attached
- whether you can see any of it at the mouth or bottom (don't pull it)
- when it happened, or when you last saw the item
- whether your cat has vomited, eaten, drunk and pooed since
- your cat's age, weight and any health problems
- whether your cat has swallowed things before
- any medicines your cat takes
