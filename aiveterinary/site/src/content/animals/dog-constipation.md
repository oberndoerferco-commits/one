## First: go to a vet now if your dog is straining and has any of these signs

Constipation can be a sign of a **gut blockage**, which is an emergency. Call your vet or an emergency clinic and go straight in if your dog is straining or not passing poo and:

- is **vomiting**
- has a **painful or swollen tummy**
- is **lethargic**, weak or off its food
- may have **swallowed a bone, toy, sock or other object**; see [my dog swallowed an object](/animals/dog-swallowed-object)
- is straining but you **can't tell whether it is trying to poo or to pee**, and no urine is coming out
- is crying out or whimpering in pain when it tries to go
- has blood coming from its bottom, or something pink sticking out of it

The PDSA lists a painful tummy, low energy, vomiting and a reduced appetite among the signs of constipation, and says that constipation "can get worse the longer you leave it so it's always better to see a vet as soon as possible". If your dog is straining but otherwise well, **call your vet today**.

## How do I know if my dog is constipated?

Your dog strains, or tries to go again and again, but passes little or nothing, or only hard, dry poo.

The PDSA says most dogs poo one to three times a day, puppies somewhat more often, and that every dog is different, so a change from your dog's usual pattern matters. Signs of constipation listed by the PDSA:

- straining for longer than usual, or trying to poo several times in a row
- only small amounts coming out, or a little liquid poo
- whining or whimpering while pooing
- blood on the poo or spots of blood from the bottom
- mucus on the poo, or thin, ribbon-like poo
- licking or over-grooming the back end

The Merck Veterinary Manual adds that the poo is often foul-smelling, and that some dogs are quite ill, with lethargy, loss of appetite, vomiting and tummy pain. Severe constipation where the dog cannot pass any poo at all is called **obstipation**.

## Is it constipation, diarrhoea, or a problem peeing?

Straining can look the same in all three, so check what actually comes out.

- **Diarrhoea.** The PDSA notes that dogs with diarrhoea can strain too, but they pass soft or liquid poo, often a lot. With constipation, very little comes out. A small amount of liquid around a hard mass can also be constipation.
- **Peeing.** The PDSA says it can be hard to tell whether a dog is struggling to pee or to poo, especially in females, and that the tail is often held up for pooing and down for peeing. If your dog is straining and producing no urine, call your vet now.

## Why is my dog constipated?

Most often something hard or hard to digest, such as bones or hair, but pain, illness or other problems can be to blame.

- **Bones and other material.** The Merck Veterinary Manual says the most common cause is a dog being unable to pass "poorly digestible, often firm matter" such as hair or bones. The PDSA also names swallowed sharp objects and other blockages. For cooked bones, see [my dog ate chicken bones](/animals/dog-ate-chicken-bones).
- **Not drinking enough.** A lack of water makes poo hard and dry (Merck).
- **Pain.** Sore hips or back legs, a broken pelvis or problems around the bottom can make squatting painful, so the dog holds on (PDSA). Merck adds a reluctance to poo because of stress.
- **Something pressing on the gut.** An enlarged prostate in male dogs, a badly healed pelvic fracture, swollen lymph nodes or a tumour (PDSA, Merck).
- **Matted fur** around the bottom (PDSA).
- **Other conditions.** An underactive thyroid, nerve or spinal problems, and some medicines (PDSA, Merck).
- **Obesity** (PDSA).

## Can I give my dog a laxative, oil or an enema at home?

Not without speaking to your vet first.

The PDSA says laxatives for dogs should only be used under advice from your vet, because the underlying cause may need investigating. The Merck Veterinary Manual warns that laxatives made for people "can be very dangerous for animals", and that laxatives should be avoided in dehydrated animals because they can make dehydration worse. A laxative won't help, and could do harm, if the cause is a bone or object blocking the gut. Enemas are something your vet does.

What you can do safely: make sure your dog has fresh water, let it out often to try to go, and watch what (if anything) comes out.

## What will the vet do?

Examine your dog, find out why it is constipated, empty the bowel if needed, and treat the cause.

The PDSA says the vet will examine your dog, which may include feeling inside the bottom (sometimes under sedation), and may suggest blood tests, poo tests, X-rays or an ultrasound scan. The Merck Veterinary Manual says X-rays can show whether the poo contains bones or other foreign material.

For mild cases, the PDSA describes a laxative, changes to diet and water intake, and possibly a small enema. In severe cases, the dog may need an anaesthetic so the vet can remove the poo, which Merck says can take several attempts over several days. Merck says that in most cases the problem is easily corrected; surgery is needed only rarely, when long-term constipation does not respond to diet and medicines.

## How can I help prevent constipation?

Plenty of water, regular exercise and toilet breaks, a healthy weight, and no bones.

- **Water.** Make sure your dog drinks plenty (PDSA); the Merck Veterinary Manual recommends easy access to water.
- **Exercise and opportunities to go.** Regular exercise helps pooing (PDSA), and Merck recommends frequent chances to poo.
- **Diet.** Merck says mild constipation can often be helped by a high-fibre diet; ask your vet which food suits your dog before changing it.
- **No bones.** Keep your dog away from bones, rubbish and other hard material (Merck).
- **Healthy weight** (PDSA).
- **Grooming.** Keep the fur around the bottom clean and free of mats (PDSA).

## When should I take my dog to the vet for constipation?

Straight away if your dog has any of the go-now signs above, and the same day if it is straining or hasn't pooed normally.

The PDSA says: "Always contact your vet if your dog is struggling to poo," and "You know your dog best, always contact your vet if you're concerned." Also see your vet if constipation keeps coming back, even if each episode clears up. If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know about my dog's constipation?

When your dog last pooed normally, what has come out since, what it may have eaten, and whether it is eating, drinking and peeing.

- when your dog last passed a normal poo, and what its usual pattern is
- what has come out since (hard, dry, small, liquid, ribbon-like, blood, mucus), ideally with a photo
- whether your dog could have eaten bones, toys, fabric, hair or rubbish
- whether it is vomiting, eating and drinking normally
- whether it is peeing normally, and how much
- any signs of pain, stiffness or difficulty squatting
- for male dogs, whether he is neutered
- any medicines your dog takes, and any laxatives or remedies you have given
- your dog's age, weight and any health problems, and any earlier episodes of constipation
