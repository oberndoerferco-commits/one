## First: call your vet or an emergency vet now, even if your dog seems fine

If your dog has eaten anything that contains **xylitol**, such as sugar-free chewing gum, mints or sweets, **call your vet or an emergency vet now** and get ready to go in. Tox Info Suisse, the Swiss poison centre, says a dog that has swallowed xylitol should be seen by a vet as soon as possible, and that a single xylitol-sweetened chewing gum can cause serious symptoms. Don't wait for signs: low blood sugar can start within minutes.

Go **straight in**, and ring on the way, if your dog:

- is **vomiting**
- is **weak, wobbly**, staggering or very quiet
- is **trembling**, has a **seizure** or **collapses**
- is hard to wake or unresponsive

Have this ready when you call: **what** your dog ate (bring the packaging), **how much** may be missing, **when**, and your dog's **weight**. The amount of xylitol varies hugely between products, so the label matters.

## Why is xylitol so dangerous for dogs?

In dogs, xylitol triggers a large release of insulin that makes blood sugar drop sharply, and larger amounts can also damage the liver.

The MSD Veterinary Manual explains that in most mammals xylitol has little effect on insulin, but in dogs it causes a rapid insulin release that can lead to severe low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia). Larger amounts can cause liver injury or liver failure, and the MSD manual notes that some dogs develop liver injury without ever having low blood sugar. Tox Info Suisse warns that, untreated, xylitol poisoning can lead to loss of consciousness, liver damage and death.

## How quickly do symptoms of xylitol poisoning start?

Low blood sugar can start within half an hour, sometimes sooner, and liver problems can appear a day or two later.

The sources differ a little on timing, so take the earliest:

- **Pet Poison Helpline** says low blood sugar can occur within 10 to 15 minutes.
- The **MSD Veterinary Manual** says within 30 minutes, but that with products that release it slowly, such as some gums, signs may be delayed by many hours.
- **Tox Info Suisse** says the first signs, vomiting and weakness, usually appear after 30 to 60 minutes.
- **Liver signs** may not show until 1 to 2 days after eating it (Merck Veterinary Manual).

So a dog that seems normal an hour later is not necessarily safe. That is why vets usually keep exposed dogs in for monitoring.

## What are the symptoms of xylitol poisoning in dogs?

Vomiting, weakness, wobbliness, lethargy, trembling and seizures from low blood sugar, and later lethargy, vomiting and yellow skin or eyes if the liver is affected.

- **Low blood sugar:** vomiting, weakness, incoordination, lethargy, seizures and coma (MSD Veterinary Manual). The small-animal clinic of the University of Giessen also lists trembling.
- **Liver damage:** low energy, vomiting, and yellowing of the skin or the whites of the eyes (jaundice). The MSD manual adds bleeding problems.

## Where is xylitol found?

In sugar-free gum, mints and sweets above all, but also in some peanut butters, baked goods, toothpaste, chewable vitamins and liquid medicines.

- **Food:** sugar-free gum, sweets and mints, some peanut and nut butters, baked goods, puddings, ice cream, jams, syrups, energy bars and protein powders (MSD, Merck, Pet Poison Helpline, ASPCA).
- **Non-food:** toothpaste and mouthwash, chewable or gummy vitamins and supplements, some liquid medicines, nasal sprays, cosmetics and skin care products (MSD, Pet Poison Helpline, ASPCA).

Tox Info Suisse says sweets and chewing gum can be up to 90% xylitol. The ASPCA describes it as a sugar alcohol that was originally found in birch bark. In Germany it is often sold as "Birkenzucker" (birch sugar) and can appear on labels as E 967, according to the German consumer advice centres (Verbraucherzentrale). Check the ingredients of anything sugar-free before your dog can reach it.

## Should I make my dog vomit?

No: don't try to make your dog sick at home. Your vet can do it if it's still safe.

The Merck Veterinary Manual advises against inducing vomiting at home because low blood sugar can develop quickly. The MSD manual says vomiting should ideally be induced only under veterinary supervision, and only in dogs that are not yet showing signs. Don't give anything to make your dog sick, and don't give food or remedies unless your vet or a poison line tells you to.

## What should I do right now?

Take away the rest of the product, find the packaging, and call your vet.

1. **Remove** any remaining gum, sweets or product, and check bags and pockets your dog could have got into. The ASPCA notes dogs often search bags for gum.
2. **Find the packaging** and look for xylitol in the ingredients. Work out how much could be missing.
3. **Call your vet or emergency vet** and tell them what, how much, when and your dog's weight. Go in if they tell you to.
4. **Don't make your dog sick** and don't give anything by mouth unless your vet tells you to.
5. **Watch your dog** closely on the way: vomiting, weakness or trembling mean it is getting worse.

## What will the vet do?

Check blood sugar straight away, make your dog vomit if it is early and safe, and usually keep your dog in to monitor blood sugar and the liver.

- **Decontamination.** If your dog is still well and it was recent, the MSD Veterinary Manual says the vet may make it vomit. Activated charcoal is not used, because it does not bind xylitol well.
- **Sugar through a drip.** Low blood sugar is treated with sugar given into a vein. The ASPCA says this may be needed in hospital for 12 to 24 hours, sometimes longer.
- **Monitoring.** The MSD manual says exposed dogs are kept in hospital with blood sugar checked frequently for at least 12 hours, and with larger amounts, liver blood tests repeated daily for at least three days.
- **Liver support** and treatment of bleeding problems if the liver is affected.

**Outlook.** The MSD manual says uncomplicated low blood sugar has a good outlook with prompt treatment and that mild liver changes usually settle within a few days. Severe liver damage or liver failure carries a guarded outlook, even with intensive care. Early treatment matters.

## Is xylitol dangerous for cats?

According to the MSD Veterinary Manual and Tox Info Suisse, dogs are the domestic species in which xylitol poisoning is reported, and it is not considered toxic to cats.

Tox Info Suisse names rabbits, cows and goats among other animals it can affect. If your cat has eaten something you're unsure about, call your vet for advice. See [poisoning first aid](/animals/poisoning-first-aid).

## When should I take my dog to the vet?

As soon as you know or suspect your dog has eaten something with xylitol in it, whether or not it shows signs.

Also go straight in if your dog has signs of low blood sugar after getting into bags, bins or the bathroom: vomiting, weakness, wobbliness, trembling or a seizure. In the following days, call your vet if your dog becomes lethargic, keeps vomiting, or the whites of its eyes or gums look yellow. For other foods dogs mustn't eat, see [toxic foods for dogs](/animals/dog-toxic-foods) and [dog ate chocolate](/animals/dog-ate-chocolate). If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know about the xylitol?

What it was, how much, when, your dog's weight, and any signs since.

- the product's name and the ingredient list (bring the packaging)
- how many pieces or how much could be missing
- when it happened, or when you last saw the product intact
- your dog's weight, age and any health problems
- whether your dog has vomited, and whether any of the product came up
- any weakness, wobbliness, trembling or seizures, and when they started
- whether your dog has eaten since
- any medicines your dog takes
