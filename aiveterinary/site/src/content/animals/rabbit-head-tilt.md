## First: call your vet now, and go straight in if you see these signs

If your rabbit's head has started to tilt to one side, **contact a vet who treats rabbits today**. The PDSA says to do so **immediately** if your rabbit has also lost its balance, seems distressed, is falling over or has **stopped eating**.

Go straight to a clinic, and ring on the way, if your rabbit:

- is **falling over, rolling** or spinning, or can't stand
- has **stopped eating** or passing droppings (in rabbits this is an emergency on its own; see [my rabbit is not eating](/animals/rabbit-not-eating))
- is having a **fit** (seizure): the RWAF advises phoning the vet immediately and padding the carrier with rolled towels
- has had a fall or a knock to the head
- has weak or dragging back legs

While you get ready: put your rabbit in a carrier padded with towels, keep it quiet, and handle it as little and as slowly as you can. The PDSA warns that moving a rabbit with head tilt makes its symptoms much worse.

## What causes head tilt in rabbits?

Most often an inner ear infection or the parasite *E. cuniculi*; less often mites, injury or other brain or ear problems.

The PDSA explains that head position is controlled by the balance (vestibular) system inside the ear and brain, and names two common causes:

- **Inner ear infection.** A deep, serious infection inside the ear. The Merck Veterinary Manual says pus or fluid in the middle or inner ear makes the rabbit twist its head, and that the bacterium *Pasteurella multocida* was found in 97% of cases in the study it cites. The same bacterium causes [snuffles](/animals/rabbit-sneezing-snuffles).
- ***Encephalitozoon cuniculi* (E. cuniculi).** A tiny parasite that can inflame the brain and damage the kidneys. The RWAF quotes research saying ear infection and *E. cuniculi* are the most common causes of head tilt in pet rabbits in the UK.

Less common causes listed by the PDSA include ear mites, a head injury, neck pain, a reaction to a medicine, brain infection or abscess, a tumour in the brain or ear, and poisoning.

Merck notes that ear infection and *E. cuniculi* can be hard to tell apart, because both are common and can occur together. Your vet may need tests or imaging to find out.

## What other signs might I see?

Loss of balance, circling, flickering eyes and, with *E. cuniculi*, weak back legs or drinking and peeing more.

The PDSA lists signs that often come with a head tilt: losing balance or falling over, walking in circles or bumping into things, flickering eye movements, uneven pupils, a sunken eye or a visible third eyelid, head shaking and itchy ears, tremors, a drooping face on one side, and in severe cases being unable to stand or rolling uncontrollably.

For *E. cuniculi* specifically, the PDSA and the RWAF also list weak, wobbly or dragging back legs, urine leaking or a dirty bottom, drinking and peeing more, cataracts and other eye problems, seizures and behaviour changes. A rabbit with a dirty, wet bottom is at high risk of [flystrike](/animals/rabbit-flystrike), so check its rear end every day.

## What is E. cuniculi, and could my other rabbits have it?

A common parasite spread in urine; many rabbits carry it without ever falling ill.

- **How common.** The Merck Veterinary Manual reports infection in 50 to 75% of pet rabbits in various countries. The PDSA says it is "fairly common" for a rabbit to have *E. cuniculi* without signs of disease.
- **How it spreads.** Spores are passed in urine and picked up from contaminated food, water, bedding or grass. The PDSA says they survive on most surfaces for about four weeks.
- **Why it flares up.** The RWAF says infection can stay quiet for months and flare up at any point, "especially under stress or with another disease".
- **People.** The PDSA says humans can catch *E. cuniculi*; people with a weakened immune system should ask their doctor.

The RWAF does not recommend separating bonded rabbits, because isolation harms their welfare; ask your vet whether your other rabbits should be tested or treated.

## How can I help my rabbit at home?

Keep it safe, calm and fed, and follow your vet's instructions. Don't give any medicine your vet hasn't prescribed.

The PDSA's advice:

- **A safe space.** "Keep them in a flat area with lots of padding, no sharp edges and no deep water."
- **Calm.** Stress makes symptoms worse, so keep your rabbit somewhere quiet and familiar.
- **Gentle handling.** Keep handling to a minimum. When you must lift your rabbit, do it very slowly and hold it securely.

Make sure your rabbit can reach its hay, greens and water. Watch closely that it keeps eating and passing droppings, and call your vet straight away if it stops. The PDSA notes that rabbits with E. cuniculi may need help with feeding and grooming.

## What will the vet do?

Examine your rabbit, look for the cause, give pain relief and support, and treat the cause where possible.

According to the RWAF and Merck, tests for *E. cuniculi* include blood tests for antibodies, which show exposure but not always current infection, and urine tests that can give false negatives. The PDSA says vets often start treatment based on the signs because testing is difficult. For ear infection, Merck describes long courses of antibiotics, imaging such as a CT scan to separate ear disease from brain disease, and sometimes ear surgery when medicines are not enough. Rabbits that are very unwell may need to stay in hospital for fluids and supportive care (PDSA).

## Will the head tilt go away?

Sometimes it improves, but often some tilt remains, and many rabbits adapt to it.

The PDSA says some head tilts, usually those caused by ear mites, get better with treatment, but most are permanent and either stay the same or get worse over time, and that most rabbits adapt with time. The RWAF says treatment for *E. cuniculi* is "not always 100% effective", and that the damage already done to the brain or kidneys remains. The PDSA adds that treating *E. cuniculi* early improves the chance of success. Talk to your vet about outlook and quality of life; if a rabbit is not coping or is getting worse, the PDSA says it may be kinder to put it to sleep.

## When should I take my rabbit to the vet for head tilt?

Always. Go the same day for any new head tilt, and straight away if your rabbit is falling, rolling, fitting or not eating.

Also call your vet if your rabbit's tilt was stable but has got worse, or if it develops new signs such as weak back legs, a wet bottom or drinking more. If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know about my rabbit's head tilt?

When it started, whether it is getting worse, what else you have noticed, and whether your rabbit is eating.

- when you first noticed the tilt, and whether it came on suddenly or gradually
- which side the head tilts to, and whether it is getting worse
- whether your rabbit is falling, rolling, circling or can't stand
- whether its eyes flicker, and any changes to the eyes
- whether it is eating, drinking and passing normal droppings, and how much it is drinking and peeing
- any head shaking, ear scratching or discharge from the ears, nose or eyes
- any recent fall or injury
- your rabbit's age, and any previous snuffles, ear, dental or kidney problems
- other rabbits in the home, any new rabbits, and whether any have had similar signs
- any medicines or treatments your rabbit takes or has had recently
