## First: see a vet today, and go straight in if you see these signs

If your cat is holding an eye closed, squinting, or has a red, watery or sticky eye, **book a vet appointment today**. The PDSA says to contact your vet as soon as you notice a problem with your cat's eyes, and immediately if your cat is in pain, because leaving it untreated can lead to much more serious problems or even loss of vision.

Ring your vet straight away and ask to be seen urgently if your cat:

- seems in **a lot of pain**: off its food, very quiet or hiding away (the PDSA's signs of extreme pain)
- has an **eye injury**, for example after a fight
- has an eye that looks **bulging, bigger than normal or suddenly cloudy**
- has **blood** in the eye
- has a change in the **size or shape of a pupil**, or seems to have **lost its sight**
- has something **stuck in the eye**, such as a grass seed (don't try to pull it out)

Have this ready when you call: **which eye**, **since when**, what the discharge looks like, and whether your cat is sneezing or unwell in other ways.

## Why is my cat keeping one eye closed?

Most often because the eye hurts: a cat with a painful eye tends to hold it closed, blink more and hide away.

The PDSA describes exactly that pattern and lists the usual causes:

- **injuries**, which are common in cats, especially cats that fight
- **something stuck in the eye**, such as a grass seed
- an **eye ulcer**, a wound on the clear surface of the eye (the cornea)
- **conjunctivitis**, an inflamed, often infected eye
- **irritants** such as smoke, dust, sand, wind and chemicals
- **allergic reactions**
- **glaucoma**, raised pressure inside the eye, which the PDSA calls "extremely painful": the eye becomes red, cloudy and bigger than normal
- **uveitis**, inflammation inside the eye

Several of these look alike from the outside. Only an examination tells them apart, which is why the PDSA advises an appointment as soon as possible if your cat is blinking more than usual.

## Why is my cat's eye watering or red?

The most common reason is conjunctivitis, often from cat flu, but injuries, ulcers, allergies and blocked tear ducts can do the same.

- **Conjunctivitis.** The Cornell Feline Health Center calls it the most common feline eye disorder. Its most frequent infectious causes are herpesvirus, calicivirus and two bacteria, chlamydophila and mycoplasma. Signs are squinting, frequent blinking, red and swollen tissue around the eye, and a discharge that may be clear and watery or thick and dark. It is most common in young cats and in multi-cat homes.
- **Cat flu.** International Cat Care says over 90% of cat flu is caused by feline calicivirus or herpesvirus, and both can cause eye discharge and conjunctivitis. If your cat is also sneezing, see our article on [cat sneezing](/animals/cat-sneezing).
- **Eye ulcers.** The MSD Veterinary Manual says herpesvirus is a frequent cause of corneal ulcers in cats, and that these ulcers can be slow to heal and come back. International Cat Care lists red eyes, sensitivity to light, squinting, pawing at the eye, a visible third eyelid and a cloudy eye as signs.
- **Allergies and irritants**, such as pollen, dust, mould or chemicals (International Cat Care).
- **Tear overflow.** International Cat Care says the most common reason tears overflow is a blocked tear duct, often linked to inflammation in the nose and sinuses. Flat-faced breeds such as Persians and Exotic Shorthairs often have runny eyes because of their face shape.

International Cat Care adds that a green or yellow discharge usually suggests bacteria are involved.

## Can I wait and see if my cat's eye gets better?

No: the PDSA's advice is never to wait to see whether an eye problem gets better on its own.

The PDSA warns that an untreated infection can lead to a painful ulcer that can affect sight, and in some cases to the loss of the eye. The Cornell Feline Health Center says mild conjunctivitis often clears up without medication, but that a cat with eye discomfort and discharge should still see a vet to rule out more serious eye disorders. International Cat Care adds that untreated eye infections can cause permanent damage and even blindness.

So even if the eye looks only mildly sore, an appointment today or tomorrow is the safe choice. Go sooner if your cat is in pain.

## What can I do at home before the appointment?

Keep your cat calm and indoors, gently wipe away discharge if your cat allows it, and don't put anything in the eye that your vet hasn't prescribed.

1. **Wiping away discharge.** International Cat Care describes using cotton wool dampened with clean water, wiping from the corner of the eye outwards and using a separate piece for each eye. Take care not to touch the eyeball, which is painful.
2. **Don't remove anything yourself.** If you can see something in the eye, the PDSA says not to try to remove it: you could damage the surface of the eye and cause an ulcer.
3. **Don't use drops or ointment you have at home**, from a human or another pet, unless your vet tells you to. Eye drops should be used only when prescribed.
4. **Keep smoke, sprays and harsh cleaning products away** from your cat (PDSA).
5. **Wash your hands** after touching your cat's eyes. The PDSA says some of the bacteria behind conjunctivitis can spread between cats, other pets and people, so keep your cat away from other pets and don't put your face close to theirs.

## What will the vet do?

Examine the eye, often with a special dye to look for ulcers, and treat the cause.

- **Dye test.** The MSD Veterinary Manual says the vet may put a special dye on the eye to find small ulcers.
- **Treatment.** Depending on the cause, the PDSA lists eye drops or ointment, lubricating drops, and pain relief. Antibiotic drops are used only when bacteria are suspected, and the PDSA stresses finishing the whole course so the infection doesn't come back. If herpesvirus is suspected, Cornell says antiviral treatment may be added.
- **Further tests.** If the eye doesn't improve or the problem keeps coming back, the PDSA says your vet may take swabs, measure tear production or refer your cat to an eye specialist.

Cornell says most cats with conjunctivitis eventually become immune and don't relapse, but cats that carry herpesvirus can have flare-ups throughout life, rather like cold sores in people.

## How can I prevent eye infections in my cat?

Keep vaccinations up to date, avoid smoke and harsh sprays, and check your cat's eyes regularly.

The PDSA says regular vaccination is the best way to prevent cat flu, one of the most common causes of conjunctivitis. International Cat Care suggests checking the eyes in good light: healthy eyes are clear and bright, with no discharge or inflammation. Flat-faced cats may need their eyes wiped regularly. Not every cause can be prevented. For vaccination, see [vaccinations for dogs and cats](/animals/vaccinations-dogs-cats).

## When should I take my cat to the vet?

Today for any red, watery, sticky or half-closed eye, and straight away if your cat is in pain or the eye looks injured, cloudy or bulging.

The PDSA's list of eye signs that need a vet includes:

- weeping or discharge
- redness or swelling
- cloudiness
- pain, blinking, or keeping one or both eyes closed
- a visible third eyelid
- a lump in or around the eye
- blood in the eye or a bulging eye
- a change in the size or shape of the pupil
- loss of vision

Also call back if your cat has had eye drops and the eye isn't getting better, or if you are struggling to give them. International Cat Care says your vet team can help. If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know about my cat's eye?

Which eye, since when, what you have seen, and whether your cat is unwell in other ways.

- which eye, or both, and when you first noticed it
- whether your cat holds it closed, blinks, paws at it or rubs its face
- what any discharge looks like: clear and watery, or thick, green or yellow
- whether the eye looks red, cloudy, bigger or different from the other
- whether your cat has been in a fight, or could have something in the eye
- whether your cat is sneezing, has a runny nose, or is eating less
- whether this has happened before, and what helped
- your cat's vaccination history, and whether other cats at home have similar signs
- any medicines or drops your cat has had
