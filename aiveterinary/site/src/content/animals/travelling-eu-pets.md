## The three things every EU trip needs

For dogs, cats and ferrets moving between EU countries with their owner (non-commercial travel), the rules are set at EU level:

1. **A microchip** (ISO 11784/11785 standard), implanted **before** the rabies vaccination. If the chip was placed after the vaccination, the vaccination does not count.
2. **A valid rabies vaccination.** The first vaccination must be at least 21 days old before travel, and the animal must be at least 12 weeks old when vaccinated. Boosters must be given before the previous one expires to stay valid without a new waiting period.
3. **An EU pet passport**, issued by an authorised vet, recording the chip number, the rabies vaccination and the owner's details.

## Country extras

Some countries add requirements. Check the destination's official rules before every trip, because they change. Well-known examples:

- **Tapeworm treatment for dogs** entering Finland, Ireland, Malta and Norway: given by a vet between 24 and 120 hours before arrival and recorded in the passport.
- **Breed rules**: some countries restrict or ban certain dog breeds or require muzzles and leads in public.
- **Young animals**: puppies and kittens under 12 weeks, or between 12 and 16 weeks without a valid rabies vaccination, can only enter some countries under specific conditions, and some do not allow it at all.

## Practical advice from vets

- Book the vet appointment for the passport or booster **well before** the trip. The 21-day rule after a first rabies vaccination is the most common reason a holiday is cancelled.
- Check the passport with your vet: chip number correct, vaccination dates legible, the vet's stamp and signature present. Border staff do check.
- Carry any medication your animal takes with the prescription or a note from your vet.
- Make sure the phone number registered to the microchip is one you will answer while abroad. A found animal is only returned quickly if the registry has a working number.

## What a microchip does and does not do

A microchip is an identification number. It does not hold your animal's medical history, and scanning it does not tell a vet abroad what vaccinations or treatments your animal has had. The passport and any records you carry do that. This is one of the problems AI Veterinary is interested in solving over time, always with the owner's control over who sees what.

## Sources to check

Rules are published by the European Commission and by each country's agriculture or food-safety authority. Your vet can point you to the current official page for your destination.
