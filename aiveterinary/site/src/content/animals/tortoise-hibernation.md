## First, the signs that mean go now

Hibernation and the weeks after it are a risky time for a tortoise. The British Chelonia Group (BCG), a UK charity for tortoises, turtles and terrapins, says that any tortoise that is not feeding or appears ill should be taken to a vet without delay. Go to a vet who treats reptiles now, and call ahead, if your tortoise:

- has eyes that are **not clear** or look **swollen** after hibernation, or it seems **blind or disoriented**
- has a **discharge** from the nose or eyes, or is **breathing with its mouth open**
- has a **yellowish-white, cheesy coating** inside its mouth (mouth rot)
- is **weak**, cannot move its legs properly, or will not go to the warmth of its basking lamp
- was exposed to **frost or freezing temperatures** while hibernating
- **passed urine or lost a lot of weight** during hibernation, which the BCG says means something is wrong

The BCG says blindness or disorientation after hibernation can be caused by frost damage, and that mouth rot (stomatitis) is often seen after hibernation. The Tortoise Protection Group (TPG), a UK group dedicated to protecting tortoises, warns that a tortoise that stays reluctant to find heat could have a problem. Open-mouth breathing and discharge from the nose are frequent signs of breathing problems in reptiles, according to the MSD Veterinary Manual, and the Tierspital Zürich, the University of Zurich's animal hospital, names nose and eye discharge among the signs of mycoplasma infection, an upper airway disease of tortoises.

A tortoise that has not started eating within a week of waking also needs a vet: the TPG says all tortoises should start eating within one week of waking, and that veterinary help should be sought if they do not.

## Should I hibernate my tortoise at all?

Only a healthy tortoise of a species that hibernates in the wild should hibernate; if it is ill, underweight or you have any doubt, keep it awake and warm indoors instead.

- **Species first.** The TPG says to make sure your tortoise is a species that would hibernate naturally in the wild, and warns that trying to hibernate a non-hibernating species "may well prove fatal, particularly with tropical tortoises". If you are not sure which species you have, ask your vet.
- **Health and weight.** The TPG advises against hibernating a tortoise that is not 100% healthy by the end of August or has not gained enough weight over the summer: "If there are any doubts DO NOT risk hibernation." The BCG says an underweight or ill tortoise should be overwintered instead, on an indoor tortoise table or in an indoor run or vivarium.
- **Very small tortoises.** The TPG recommends that tortoises weighing under 35 grams at the start of the wind-down are over-wintered rather than hibernated.
- **Why healthy tortoises hibernate.** Germany's Federal Chamber of Veterinarians (Bundestierärztekammer) calls the yearly winter rest very important for the health of pet Hermann's and spur-thighed tortoises: their hormones and immune system depend on it, and young tortoises grow far too fast without it.

How risky is it? In a survey of owners of 270 captive tortoises in the UK, 7.78% of the tortoises died during hibernation (also called brumation), and hibernating in a garden and an uncontrolled drop in temperature before hibernation were the main risk factors (Baldrey and colleagues, Veterinary Record, 2022).

## Does my tortoise need a vet check before hibernation?

Yes: the Bundestierärztekammer advises a check-up at the vet beforehand, where the tortoise's health is examined and its droppings are tested for parasites.

The TPG also suggests these checks at home before hibernation:

- **Body:** no lumps, swelling, cuts or grazes
- **Tail:** no swelling or discharge
- **Eyes:** both clear, with no inflammation or discharge
- **Mouth:** clean and a healthy pink inside, with no yellowish-white cheesy coating
- **Nose:** clear, with no discharge
- **Ears:** no abscesses; the membranes covering the ears should be flat or concave

If your tortoise does not pass this check, the TPG says to consult a tortoise vet. Both the TPG and the BCG recommend keeping weight records from year to year, so you can see whether your tortoise has gained enough weight over the summer.

## How do I prepare my tortoise for hibernation?

Stop feeding for several weeks while it stays warm enough to digest, bathe it regularly so it stays hydrated, and only then cool it down gradually.

- **An empty gut.** The TPG says there must be no undigested food in the stomach at the start of hibernation, because it will rot during hibernation. The BCG notes that it takes 4 to 6 weeks for a tortoise's gut to empty.
- **The wind-down.** The TPG generally uses 3 weeks for young juveniles and 4 weeks for sub-adults and adults, occasionally up to 6 weeks, with no food at all, while the tortoise still gets light and heat so it can digest what is already in its stomach. The Bundestierärztekammer describes reducing light and temperature step by step over about three weeks and stopping feeding, while still offering drinking water.
- **Baths.** Both recommend lukewarm baths during the wind-down: the TPG says they encourage drinking, and the Bundestierärztekammer that they help empty the gut. By the end, the TPG says, the tortoise must have an empty stomach and a full bladder.
- **Cooling down.** Lower the temperature gradually, not suddenly: in the UK survey, an uncontrolled drop in temperature before hibernation was one of the main risk factors.

## What temperature should a hibernating tortoise be kept at?

Cold, stable and always above freezing: each organisation gives a slightly different range, all a few degrees above 0°C, so check the temperature with a reliable thermometer.

- The BCG: "a frost free environment, at temperatures of 4 - 8°C".
- The TPG gives the optimum as 4°C to 5°C and recommends a range of 3°C (37°F) to 7°C (45°F). It warns that as temperatures approach freezing, the tortoise could freeze or suffer irreparable eye damage, and that as they approach 10°C (50°F) it may start to become active and use up its vital reserves.
- The Bundestierärztekammer: a constant 4 to 6 degrees Celsius.

The BCG suggests a maximum-minimum thermometer to check for temperature changes. The TPG's advice: always use at least one well-tested thermometer.

## Is it safe to hibernate a tortoise in the fridge?

It can be the most controllable method, as long as the fridge holds a steady temperature without forming ice and you check it every day.

The Bundestierärztekammer calls a separate fridge, used only for the tortoise for hygiene reasons, the best method, because the temperature can be set exactly and checked with an electronic thermometer. After "a number of tragic losses" in one winter, the BCG published these tips:

- The ideal fridge is one where ice never forms, such as a wine cooler. A larder fridge without an ice box is not necessarily safe, because beads of ice can still form on the back wall.
- Set the fridge up at least a month before the tortoise goes in, and check the temperature regularly until you are sure it has settled at around 5°C.
- Put the tortoise in a box with a suitable substrate, or loosely wrapped in a towel to absorb condensation, away from the back wall and not too close to the light.
- Open the door every day for fresh air, and don't use this method if you will be away from home during the hibernation.
- Weigh your tortoise regularly.
- The BCG does not recommend the fridge for older tortoises that have hibernated successfully by traditional methods for many years, or for very young ones, and says it may not suit Horsfield tortoises, which have a strong urge to burrow and need a deep box.

What about the garden or a shed? The Bundestierärztekammer calls a cellar, balcony or garage more problematic, because the temperature cannot be kept constant, and says burying in the garden has risks too: hard frost reaches the hiding place, warm spells drive the tortoise out, and you can hardly check on it. In the UK survey, hibernating in a garden was one of the main risk factors for illness and death.

## How long should a tortoise hibernate, and how do I check on it?

Adults usually for a few months and young tortoises much less, with regular weighing, and you bring it out early if it loses a lot of weight or passes urine.

- **How long.** According to the Bundestierärztekammer, the winter rest usually lasts three to five months, and for tortoises in their first year a shortened rest of six to eight weeks is enough.
- **Weight.** The BCG says an adult tortoise loses about 1% of its pre-hibernation weight, "so a 1000g tortoise is allowed to lose 10g monthly". The Bundestierärztekammer suggests taking the tortoise out every 14 days to weigh and check it, and says it must not lose more than ten per cent of its body weight in total. Agree with your vet beforehand which limit applies to your tortoise.
- **Warning signs.** "Drastic loss of weight or urination means something is wrong and the animal should be brought out of hibernation," says the BCG. If a young tortoise has to be woken because it urinated, the TPG says to bathe it in warm water before it basks, because it will be very short of fluid.
- **When to check most closely.** The BCG advises checking tortoises regularly, especially from the end of January.

## How do I wake my tortoise up?

Warm it up gradually, let it walk to its basking lamp, then give it a lukewarm bath so it can drink, and repeat the baths for the first few days.

The TPG describes these steps:

1. Take the hibernation box into a warm room and leave the lid on. Avoid direct sunlight through a window or the heat of a radiator: a gradual warm-up is less stressful.
2. After about an hour, take the tortoise out and put it in a warm, bright place just away from its basking lamp, so it can walk to the heat when it is ready. This also shows you whether it moves its legs normally.
3. Once it is thoroughly warm, check it over (body, tail, eyes, mouth, nose and ears), then bathe it in lukewarm water for about 20 minutes, topping up the water to keep it tepid, so it can drink.
4. Repeat the baths at least twice a day for the first few days.

The BCG adds: keep the tortoise warm during the day and indoors overnight until the nights get warmer.

Why this matters: the University of Edinburgh's vet school explains that tortoises are especially vulnerable after waking. Their white blood cell count is low, so they fight infection less well, they are likely to be dehydrated, and they must clear the toxins that built up in their kidneys during hibernation. The TPG adds that tortoises of every size are very vulnerable to dehydration when they wake up.

**Hygiene during baths.** The Association of Reptile and Amphibian Veterinarians says that carrying Salmonella appears to be very common in reptiles, and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) notes that even healthy reptiles can carry it. The CDC advises keeping reptiles and their equipment out of the kitchen, never using food preparation areas to clean their equipment, and washing your hands after handling them or anything in their environment. Children under 5, adults over 65 and people with weakened immune systems are more likely to get sick.

## My tortoise won't eat after hibernation: what should I do?

Keep it warm, keep up the baths and offer food, and if it has not started eating within a week of waking, take it to a vet.

- **The timeline.** The TPG says food can be offered as soon as the tortoise has finished its bath; it may eat straight away, or it may take hours. Young tortoises should generally eat within 24 hours of waking, and all tortoises should start eating within one week. If they do not, the TPG says, veterinary help should be sought.
- **Why it happens.** Writing in Vet Times, Mark Rowland describes not eating as a common problem in tortoises after hibernation, linked to poor preparation, hibernating for too long, unsuitable hibernation conditions and a suppressed immune system.
- **Eyes.** A tortoise that seems blind or disoriented after hibernation may have frost damage, which the BCG and Rowland both describe.
- **Swollen eyes.** The Tierspital Zürich lists swelling around the eyes, eye and nose discharge and conjunctivitis among the signs of mycoplasma infection in tortoises, often with a reduced appetite and less activity. It also warns that too many vitamins can themselves be harmful, so do not give vitamins yourself.
- **Don't force-feed.** The MSD Veterinary Manual says assisted feeding of a reptile is best directed by your vet, because feeding a severely dehydrated, malnourished reptile can cause further problems. Rowland describes how vets look for the underlying cause and support the tortoise, and calls fluid therapy "profoundly important" for these patients.

If your reptile is a lizard or a snake that is not eating, see [my reptile is not eating](/animals/reptile-not-eating).

## When should I take my tortoise to the vet?

Before hibernation for a check-up, during it if you had to wake it early, and after it if it has not eaten within a week or shows any of the signs at the top of this page.

Take your tortoise to a vet:

- **in autumn, before hibernation,** for a health check and a parasite test of its droppings, as the Bundestierärztekammer advises
- if it **fails the TPG home check**, or you are not sure whether it should hibernate at all
- if you had to **wake it early** because it urinated or lost a lot of weight
- if it has **not started eating within a week** of waking (TPG)
- **without delay** if it is not feeding or seems ill in any way (BCG), and **straight away** for the signs at the top of this page

Not every vet treats reptiles. The MSD Veterinary Manual advises asking a vet about their experience with reptiles when you choose one, so find one before hibernation season, not on the day you need it.

## Does my tortoise need papers?

Very likely, if it is a European tortoise: Hermann's, spur-thighed and marginated tortoises are listed in Annex A of the EU wildlife trade regulation, Council Regulation (EC) No 338/97.

- The city of Cologne's information for tortoise keepers says that all species of European tortoise are listed in Annex A of Regulation 338/97 and that an EU marketing certificate is needed to market them. In Germany, every tortoise you take on, breed, give away, or that dies, must be reported to the local nature conservation authority, and tortoises are usually identified by photographs of their shell.
- In Great Britain, guidance from the Animal and Plant Health Agency (APHA) says that tortoises listed in Annex A, including Hermann's (*Testudo hermanni*), spur-thighed (*Testudo graeca*) and marginated (*Testudo marginata*) tortoises, require an Article 10 (A10) licence before any commercial transaction can take place.

Ask the wildlife or CITES authority where you live which rules apply to your tortoise.

## What will the vet want to know about my tortoise?

The species, its weights before and during hibernation, how and where it hibernated, when it woke, and whether it has drunk, passed urine and eaten since.

- The species (if you know it), its age and sex, and where it came from
- Its weight before hibernation, any weights recorded during it, and its weight now
- How and where it hibernated (fridge, box, shed, garden), the temperatures you recorded, and for how long
- Whether it passed urine during hibernation
- When it woke, and whether it has drunk, passed urine or droppings, or eaten since
- Its set-up now: basking lamp, UVB lamp and temperatures
- Any changes to its eyes, nose, mouth, breathing or movement
- For a female, whether she could be carrying eggs
- Other tortoises in your household, and any new arrivals

If your tortoise has not eaten since waking or seems unwell, stop reading and call a vet who treats reptiles now.
