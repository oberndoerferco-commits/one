## First, the signs that mean go now

With a horse, "go now" means: phone your vet now, at night too, so the vet can come to you or tell you where to take your horse. Colic is a word for belly (abdominal) pain, usually from a problem in the gut. The University of Liverpool's Leahurst Equine Practice says **any horse showing signs of colic needs immediate veterinary attention**, because early diagnosis and treatment give it the best chance of recovery.

Call your vet straight away if your horse:

- paws at the ground, keeps looking round at its flank, or kicks at its belly
- lies down and gets up again and again, rolls, or lies flat out on its side for long periods
- is restless, walks round the stable or circles, or sweats for no clear reason
- eats less or nothing, or passes fewer droppings, none at all, or droppings that look different
- stretches out as if to pass urine but doesn't, or curls its upper lip
- breathes fast, has a raised heart rate, quieter or absent gut sounds, or gums that have changed colour
- is dull, lies down more than usual or stands with its head low
- has fresh scrapes (abrasions) over the eyes

These signs come from the REACT campaign of the British Horse Society (BHS) and the University of Nottingham, from the Leahurst leaflet and from the MSD Veterinary Manual, which notes that it is uncommon for a horse to show all of them. Redwings, the horse charity, warns that **milder signs don't necessarily mean the problem is less serious**, and the BHS says a horse's behaviour may not reflect how much pain it is in.

Don't measure first. The BHS and University of Nottingham guide on temperature, pulse and breathing says that if your horse is showing signs of discomfort or illness, get veterinary help immediately and **don't waste time measuring its values beforehand**.

## Why is colic an emergency in horses?

Colic ranges from mild to severe, and the Leahurst leaflet calls it one of the most common causes of death in horses. Critical cases are a race against time, and horses cannot vomit.

- The BHS calls equine colic **the most common emergency and cause of death in horses worldwide**, and says critical colic is time sensitive.
- Research at the University of Nottingham found that colic accounts for **one in three** emergency vet call-outs. At least one in ten cases may be critical, and **up to 80%** of those critical cases end in death or euthanasia. More than 90% of the owners surveyed did not feel confident spotting the early signs.
- Horses cannot vomit. The Leahurst leaflet explains that fluid building up in the stomach could be fatal if it made the stomach rupture. This is one reason the vet may pass a tube through the nose into the stomach.
- According to the Leahurst leaflet, **in around 10% of cases surgery is needed** to save the horse's life.

## What should I do while I wait for the vet?

Take feed away, let your horse lie down or roll in a safe place if it wants to, keep yourself out of harm's way, and keep watching so you can tell the vet what has changed.

- **Call your vet straight away**, as the BHS advises. If the vet doesn't know your yard, the BHS suggests sharing a what3words or live location, and making sure they have a number to reach you on.
- **Take away hay and feed.** Penn State Extension advises removing all feed from the area. Penn Vet's New Bolton Center points out that impactions (blockages of food) are a common cause of colic.
- **Let your horse lie down or roll if it wants to.** The BHS says this won't make colic worse. Clear away anything your horse could hurt itself on, including water buckets, hay nets and equipment.
- **You don't have to walk your horse.** Redwings says the old idea that a horse with colic should be walked is now known not to be the case. The BHS says some horses are more comfortable walking gently, but don't force a reluctant horse, and don't walk for more than 30 minutes unless your vet has told you to. Penn Vet warns that walking without end may exhaust your horse.
- **Keep yourself safe.** Penn State Extension warns not to get in the way of a rolling or thrashing horse. The BHS advises wearing a riding hat, gloves and suitable footwear, says a lunge line can be safer, and says not to do anything that would put you at risk.
- **Keep watching**, so you can tell the vet how your horse has changed when they arrive (BHS).
- **Find your horse's passport** (see below).

## Can I give my horse a painkiller or anything else for colic?

No, not unless your vet tells you to. Pain relief is part of the vet's treatment, after the examination.

Penn State Extension advises owners to avoid giving any medications unless the vet has instructed it, because medication can mask the signs of colic that the vet needs to see. The Leahurst leaflet describes how the vet first examines the horse and then gives pain relief, and the BHS says your vet can prescribe medicines including pain relief and muscle relaxants. If you have already given something, tell the vet exactly what and when.

## Why does my horse's passport matter in a colic emergency?

In the EU, a horse counts as an animal for the food chain unless its passport says otherwise, and that status decides which medicines the vet may use.

- Under Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/963, horses are deemed to be intended for slaughter for human consumption unless they have been excluded. The exclusion is signed in the passport and is **irreversible** (Article 38).
- Before treating, the vet must check this status in the passport, and in the database where they have access (Article 39).
- Under Regulation (EU) 2019/6 on veterinary medicinal products, the rules that let a vet use medicines outside their authorisation in animals not kept for food apply to a horse only if its passport declares it **not intended for slaughter for human consumption** (Article 112(4)).

Keep the passport where you can find it quickly, even at night, and ask your vet about your horse's status before you ever need it in a hurry.

## When should I call the vet about colic?

As soon as you notice any sign, even a mild one, at any time of day or night. Don't wait to see whether it passes.

- The Leahurst leaflet says any horse with colic signs needs immediate veterinary attention, and Redwings says **colic should always be treated as an emergency**.
- The BHS says signs range from a horse that just seems **quieter than normal**, or shows only subtle signs, to obvious distress. A pulse and breathing rate above your horse's normal range may mean it is in pain, which could be colic.
- The BHS and University of Nottingham guide advises seeking veterinary advice if your horse's temperature, pulse or breathing has changed with no reason such as recent stress or exercise.
- Penn Vet advises calling your vet if you find your horse sick or painful in the morning.

Redwings recommends checking every horse carefully at least twice a day, so that changes are noticed early. Knowing your horse's normal values helps: see [horse vital signs](/animals/horse-vital-signs).

## What will the vet do?

The vet examines your horse, relieves its pain and decides whether it can be treated at home or needs a clinic.

The MSD Veterinary Manual describes checking the heart and breathing rates, the colour of the gums, and the gut sounds with a stethoscope, and a rectal examination, which it calls a critical part of the exam. Because horses cannot vomit, a tube passed through the nose into the stomach can release gas or fluid that would otherwise make the stomach rupture. Some horses also need an ultrasound scan or a sample of the fluid in the belly.

If your horse may need surgery or more tests, the vet will refer it to an equine hospital. The Leahurst leaflet points out that referral does not necessarily mean surgery: the horse may need an ultrasound scan, close monitoring, fluids into a vein or intensive care.

## How can I lower the risk of colic?

Keep routines steady, change feed slowly, give plenty of forage and water, and stay on top of worms and teeth. Even so, not every colic can be prevented.

The Leahurst leaflet says that in many cases horses colic without any known risk factor, so colic cannot be prevented completely. It and Redwings recommend:

- **fresh water at all times**; in winter, check that troughs and buckets are not frozen (Leahurst)
- **steady feeding, turnout and exercise routines**, especially over holidays, when routines tend to change (Leahurst)
- **gradual feed changes** over 2 to 3 weeks; Leahurst notes a greater risk of colic in the 7 to 14 days after a change in diet
- **forage as the mainstay of the diet**, because horses are trickle feeders (Redwings)
- **care with sudden extra time in the stable**, for example in bad winter weather, with plenty of water and a check that droppings stay normal (Leahurst)
- **worm control based on faecal egg counts** and regular poo-picking (Leahurst, Redwings)
- **dental checks at least every 12 months** (Leahurst)
- **no hard feed immediately before or after exercise**, and care on sandy ground, where horses may swallow sand (Redwings)

Leahurst and the BHS also advise deciding in advance what you would want in an emergency, including referral, surgery, costs, insurance and transport, so that you can decide quickly if it happens.

## What will the vet want to know about my horse's colic?

When the signs started and how they have changed, droppings, the last meal, recent changes in feeding or routine, past colic, worming, teeth and any medicine given.

The MSD Veterinary Manual, Penn Vet and the BHS list the information that helps the vet:

- when the signs started, how quickly they have changed and how much pain your horse seems to be in
- when your horse last passed droppings, and what they looked like
- when and what it last ate, and any recent change in feed, hay, water or grazing
- whether it was resting or working when the signs began
- whether it has had colic before
- when it was last wormed and when its teeth were last checked
- whether it crib-bites
- any medicine or treatment it has had, and how it responded
- its heart rate, breathing rate and temperature, if you have been able to check them safely
- its passport, with its food-chain status
- what you would want if your horse needed referral or surgery, and how you would transport it
