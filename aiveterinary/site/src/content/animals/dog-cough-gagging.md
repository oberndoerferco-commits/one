## First: go to a vet now if you see these signs

Contact your vet **immediately**, and go in, if your dog:

- is **struggling to breathe**, or breathing very loudly or very fast
- has **pale gums**
- has a **coughing fit that won't stop**
- may have **something stuck in the throat**, such as a piece of stick, a bone, a chew or a grass seed. If the cough started suddenly while chewing something, or after running through long grass, and your dog keeps swallowing, gagging or trying to bring something up, don't wait. Don't put your fingers blindly down the throat
- keeps **retching without bringing anything up**, or only froth and saliva, especially with a **swollen, tight tummy**, restlessness or drooling

That last picture is typical of a **twisted stomach** (gastric dilatation-volvulus, GDV). It mostly affects large, deep-chested breeds such as Great Danes, German Shepherds, Setters, Weimaraners, St Bernards, Standard Poodles and Basset Hounds, but can happen in other dogs. It needs immediate treatment and surgery, and every hour counts. Don't feed your dog and don't wait to see if it settles.

## Why does my dog cough like something is stuck in the throat?

Often nothing is stuck: the gag at the end is part of the cough itself, as in kennel cough.

Many owners describe the same thing: a loud, hacking cough, neck stretched out, then a gag as if about to be sick, with nothing or just a bit of froth coming up. The MSD Veterinary Manual describes **kennel cough** (infectious tracheobronchitis) in exactly this way: spasms of harsh, dry coughing, which may be followed by retching and gagging. It's a common cause, but not the only one.

## Is it a cough, a gag or a reverse sneeze?

It can be hard to tell, so film an episode on your phone to show your vet.

Coughs can be confused with gagging, sneezing or reverse sneezing, and a vet can tell them apart. A short video helps a lot. Note how often it happens, what time of day, and watch for:

- does it start with a cough and end with a bit of froth?
- or does your dog retch from the start, with heaving of the tummy, as before vomiting?
- does anything come up, and what?
- does it happen with excitement, on the lead, after drinking, at rest or at night?

## What is kennel cough?

A very contagious airway infection that causes fits of dry coughing, usually lasting 1 to 3 weeks, while most dogs otherwise seem well.

Despite the name, it isn't only caught in kennels:

- **It spreads easily**, through the air, by direct contact between dogs and on shared bowls and leads, especially where many dogs are close together.
- **Timing.** Signs usually appear 3 to 14 days after contact and last 1 to 3 weeks. The cough usually eases over the first five days.
- **Most dogs are otherwise bright**, though some eat a little less.
- **It can become serious.** In puppies it can develop into a fatal bronchopneumonia. **Fever, thick or pus-like nasal discharge, being very flat, going off food, or a wet cough** suggest a more serious infection and need a vet.
- **Keep your dog away from other dogs** while coughing and for two to three weeks afterwards. Ring the practice before you go, and wait outside if asked.
- **Vaccination** doesn't give complete protection, but it greatly lowers the chance of infection and makes the signs milder. Ask your vet whether it suits your dog, especially if they go to kennels, daycare, classes or shows.

## What else can make a dog cough and gag?

Pulling on the lead, a collapsing windpipe, chronic bronchitis, laryngeal paralysis, heart disease, lungworm, irritants and something stuck in the airway.

The PDSA lists many causes, including:

- **Pulling on the lead.** Collar pressure on the throat can cause coughing; a harness often helps.
- **Tracheal collapse**, common in toy breeds and worse with excitement and exercise.
- **Chronic bronchitis**, mainly in middle-aged to older small dogs. Vets use the term when a cough has lasted at least 2 months with no other cause.
- **Laryngeal paralysis**, most often in older large-breed dogs.
- **Heart disease.**
- **Flat-faced breeds** with breathing problems (BOAS).
- **Lungworm.**
- **Irritants**: cigarette smoke, air fresheners, hairspray, dust.
- **Something stuck in the airway**, and, less often, **cancer** of the throat or lungs.

Finding the cause needs an examination, and sometimes X-rays or other tests.

## When should I take my coughing dog to the vet?

The same day if the cough makes your dog unwell, keeps them up at night, brings up blood, causes collapse, or your dog is a puppy; otherwise if it lasts more than a few days.

Book an appointment **today** if:

- the cough makes your dog seem unwell, or keeps them awake at night
- your dog coughs up **blood** or has **collapsed** after a coughing fit
- a **puppy** is coughing
- your dog also has a fever, is flat, off food, or has thick nasal discharge
- the cough sounds wet or brings up mucus
- your dog has known heart disease and is coughing more or for the first time

Book in the next few days if an otherwise bright adult dog has a dry cough that has lasted **more than a few days**, keeps coming back, or an older dog starts coughing for the first time.

## What can I do at home while I wait?

Rest your dog, keep them warm and away from smoke and other dogs, use a harness, and don't give human cough syrups.

- **Rest.** Exercise can make the cough much worse, so avoid running, rough play and long walks.
- **Use a harness** instead of a collar.
- **Keep your dog warm and comfortable**, away from smoke, sprays and air fresheners.
- **Keep away from other dogs** until you know what it is.
- **Don't give human cough or cold medicines.** Many contain paracetamol or anti-inflammatories such as ibuprofen. Paracetamol can damage a dog's liver, and ibuprofen can cause stomach ulcers and perforation. Your vet will decide whether your dog needs anything.

## Can I prevent a twisted stomach?

For large, deep-chested dogs, vets suggest several small meals instead of one big one, limiting exercise around meals, calm mealtimes and no raised bowls.

For dogs at high risk, many surgeons recommend a preventive operation (gastropexy) that fixes the stomach to the body wall, often done at the same time as neutering. It doesn't stop bloating, but it should prevent the life-threatening twist. Ask your vet if it's worth considering for your dog.

## What will the vet want to know?

When the cough started, contact with other dogs, vaccinations, when it's worse, other signs, anything chewed or swallowed, heart problems and medicines.

- when the cough started, and whether it came on suddenly
- recent kennels, daycare, dog parks, shows, or contact with coughing dogs
- vaccinations and when they were given
- whether it's worse at night, with excitement, on the lead or after drinking
- appetite, fever, nasal discharge, tiredness
- anything your dog might have chewed or swallowed
- known heart problems and current medicines
- worming and lungworm prevention
