## Go to a vet now if your rabbit

The PDSA says diarrhoea in rabbits can become a serious, life-threatening problem and that you should **never wait to see if it improves**. Book an appointment **today** if you notice abnormal droppings. Go **now**, out of hours if necessary, and call ahead, if your rabbit:

- has **watery or shapeless droppings** and is no longer passing normal hard pellets
- is **not eating**, or eating much less than usual
- is passing **few or no droppings**
- has a **bloated** or tight belly
- shows signs of pain: sitting **hunched**, grinding its teeth, shivering, not wanting to move
- is dull, hiding, cold to the touch or weak
- is a **young rabbit** with diarrhoea
- has a dirty bottom and you see **fly eggs or maggots** in the fur

A rabbit with watery diarrhoea that stops eating can go downhill very fast. The MSD Veterinary Manual describes a severe gut disease in young rabbits where one "might seem fine one evening and be dead by morning". See our [emergency page](/emergency) for what to do out of hours.

## Is it diarrhoea or caecotrophs?

Often the soft droppings are uneaten caecotrophs: if your rabbit still passes normal pellets and is eating and bright, it is less likely to be true diarrhoea, but it still needs a vet.

Rabbits make **two kinds of droppings**:

- **pellets**: the round, dry, fibrous droppings you normally see
- **caecotrophs**: soft, sticky, dark brown clusters. They are a normal part of digestion, and rabbits usually eat them straight from the bottom, mostly at night, so you rarely see them

The PDSA notes that caecotrophs are **easily mistaken for diarrhoea**. As a rough guide:

| | Uneaten caecotrophs | True diarrhoea |
|---|---|---|
| Normal round pellets | still being passed | usually missing |
| Appetite | eating normally | eating little or nothing |
| How the rabbit seems | bright | dull, often in pain |
| What to do | book a vet visit today | go now |

Either way, call your vet. The PDSA advises booking a vet visit if you find caecotrophs, because a rabbit only stops eating them when something is wrong.

## Why is my rabbit not eating its caecotrophs?

Common reasons are a diet too rich in nuggets, sugar or starch, being overweight, and arthritis or back problems.

The Rabbit Welfare Association & Fund lists:

- **too much sugar or starch** in the diet, which makes caecotrophs soft and misshapen so they stick to the fur
- **too many nuggets**, so the rabbit produces more caecotrophs than it eats
- **being overweight**, so the rabbit can't reach round to its bottom, which it describes as a major cause
- **arthritis or back problems**, which make it hard to eat caecotrophs straight from the bottom

Dental disease can also make grooming painful. Your vet will look for the cause.

## What causes diarrhoea in rabbits?

Possible causes include a low-fibre diet or sudden diet change, dental disease, gut infections and parasites, liver disease, antibiotics and tumours.

The PDSA lists:

- a **low-fibre diet** or a **sudden change of diet**
- **dental disease**
- **gut infections**, bacterial or viral
- **gut parasites** such as coccidiosis, which the MSD Veterinary Manual says puts young rabbits most at risk
- liver disease
- **antibiotics** prescribed for another problem
- tumours

The MSD Veterinary Manual calls diarrhoea at any age a serious sign of illness, and intestinal disease a major cause of death in young rabbits. Diet changes, stress, antibiotics and dirty cages can upset the normal bacteria in the gut.

## How is diarrhoea linked to gut stasis?

They often share the same roots, so a rabbit with abnormal droppings that is eating less needs to be seen straight away.

Low fibre, dental disease, stress and pain can all lead to **gut stasis**, where the gut slows down. The MSD Veterinary Manual explains that harmful bacteria then build up along with gas, and "the rabbit feels bloated and painful, which makes it even less likely to eat." This can become life-threatening, so a rabbit that is eating less and passing fewer droppings needs a vet. See also [my rabbit is not eating](/animals/rabbit-not-eating).

## What is flystrike?

A painful, potentially fatal condition where flies lay eggs on a dirty bottom and the maggots burrow into the skin.

A bottom soiled with droppings or caecotrophs attracts flies. The PDSA explains that the maggots that hatch burrow under the skin and "can cause extensive damage in as little as one day."

If you see even one maggot, **call your vet now** for an emergency appointment. The PDSA advises **not** trying to remove the maggots yourself and **not** dunking your rabbit in water, as this causes pain and can lead to shock. The Rabbit Welfare Association & Fund puts it plainly: don't wait to wash it, just go to the vet.

## What should I not do at home?

Don't treat the diarrhoea yourself, don't give human medicines or leftover antibiotics, and don't soak your rabbit.

- **Don't try to treat it at home.** The PDSA is clear that a vet visit is essential.
- **Don't give human medicines** or medicines meant for another pet, including leftover antibiotics. The MSD Veterinary Manual warns that certain oral antibiotics, including penicillin, can trigger a serious, potentially fatal gut disease in rabbits.
- **Don't soak your rabbit** to clean its bottom. Ask your vet how to manage it.

While you arrange the visit, keep your rabbit calm with hay and fresh water available and, if you can, bring a **sample of droppings**, as the PDSA suggests. Our [visit brief](/visit-brief) helps you note when it started, what your rabbit eats and any recent changes.

## How can I prevent diarrhoea in my rabbit?

Unlimited good hay, gradual diet changes, few nuggets, a healthy weight and a daily bottom check.

- **Unlimited hay** every day, which the MSD Veterinary Manual puts at the heart of prevention, along with a healthy weight.
- **Gradual diet changes**, never sudden ones.
- **Few nuggets** and very few sugary treats; avoid muesli-style mixes.
- **Regular dental checks** with your vet.
- **Check every day** that your rabbit is passing normal pellets and its bottom is clean and dry, and more often in warm weather. The PDSA also advises removing soiled litter and wet bedding at least once a day.

In rabbits, gut problems move fast. If something about the droppings looks wrong, call your vet.
