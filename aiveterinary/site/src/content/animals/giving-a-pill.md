## Why "exactly as prescribed" matters

Your vet has chosen the medicine, the amount and the number of days for your animal's weight and condition. Missed doses, halved doses because "it seemed better", or stopping early are common reasons treatment fails, and with antibiotics they can make the next infection harder to treat.

If you cannot get the medicine in, do not guess at an alternative. Call the practice. Vets can often change the form (tablet to liquid, or a flavoured version), the timing, or in some cases give a long-acting injection instead. Changing anything about the dose, timing or medicine is a vet decision.

## Before you start

- Read the label. Note whether it should be given with food or on an empty stomach, and how many times a day.
- Ask whether the tablet can be crushed or hidden in food. Some tablets must be swallowed whole, and some must not be given with certain foods.
- Set reminders on your phone. Twice daily means roughly 12 hours apart, not breakfast and lunch.
- Have everything ready before you pick up the animal: tablet out of the packet, a treat to follow, a towel if you need it.
- Stay calm. Short, confident and done is better than long and apologetic.

## Hiding a tablet in food

This is the easiest route when the label allows it.

- Use a small amount of something soft and strong-smelling: a piece of cheese, a little wet food, a soft treat, or a purpose-made pill pocket.
- Give one or two "blank" treats first, then the one with the tablet, then another blank one straight after.
- Watch that the tablet is actually swallowed. Cats can eat around a tablet and spit it out later.
- Never hide tablets in a whole bowl of food. If the animal leaves half the meal, you do not know how much medicine went in.

## Giving a tablet by hand to a dog

1. Hold the top of the muzzle gently with one hand, fingers behind the canine teeth, and tilt the head slightly upward. The lower jaw tends to drop open.
2. With the other hand, pull the lower jaw down and place the tablet as far back on the tongue as you can.
3. Close the mouth, hold it closed, and gently stroke the throat or blow softly on the nose until you see a swallow.
4. Check the mouth, then give a treat and praise.

## Giving a tablet by hand to a cat

Cats need a slightly different approach.

1. Place the cat on a non-slip surface at table height. Wrapping it snugly in a towel with only the head out keeps claws contained and most cats calmer.
2. Hold the head from above, thumb and finger either side of the cheekbones, and tilt it back gently. The mouth usually opens.
3. Lower the jaw with a finger and drop the tablet at the back of the tongue, or use a pill-giver (a tool your vet or nurse can show you).
4. Close the mouth, keep the head level, and stroke the throat until the cat swallows. Licking the lips is a good sign.
5. Follow with a little water from a syringe or a lick of something tasty, so the tablet does not sit in the throat.

## Liquid medication

- Measure with the syringe supplied, to the exact line. Do not use kitchen spoons.
- Put the syringe tip into the side of the mouth, between the cheek and the teeth, not straight down the throat.
- Give slowly, in small squirts, allowing the animal to swallow. Squirting fast causes spluttering and a lost dose.
- If the animal spits most of it out, do not simply give another full dose. Call the practice for advice; this is especially important with medicines where too much is harmful.

## Things not to do

- Do not give human medicines, even "just the once". Paracetamol can kill a cat, and ibuprofen is dangerous for dogs.
- Do not crush tablets unless your vet has said it is fine.
- Do not stop a course early because your animal seems better.
- Do not use medication prescribed for another animal, even in the same house.

## If something goes wrong

If your animal vomits soon after a dose, seems unwell after starting a medicine, or you have given a dose twice by mistake, call your vet before doing anything else. They will tell you whether to repeat, skip or come in.

If you are struggling with any part of this, ask your own vet or veterinary nurse to show you; most practices are happy to demonstrate.
