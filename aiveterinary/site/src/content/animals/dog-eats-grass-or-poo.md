## First: when eating grass or poo means see a vet

Eating grass or poo is rarely an emergency on its own. **Call your vet** if your dog:

- seems **unwell**, or eats grass **excessively**, or wants grass **instead of its normal food** (PDSA)
- has **suddenly** started eating poo and it is out of character, or doesn't seem right in itself (PDSA)
- is also losing **weight**, has **diarrhoea** or **vomiting**, or its coat has changed (PDSA)
- vomits repeatedly, can't keep water down, or seems in pain: see [dog vomiting](/animals/dog-vomiting) for when that is urgent

If your dog belongs to a herding breed such as a Collie, Australian Shepherd or Shetland Sheepdog and has eaten **horse droppings**, call your vet today: ESCCAP Germany warns that dung from horses recently given certain wormers can be dangerous for dogs with the MDR1 gene defect, which is more common in these breeds and in German Shepherds.

## Why does my dog eat grass?

Usually because it likes it. Eating grass is normal dog behaviour.

The PDSA says it is a misconception that dogs eat grass because they feel ill and are about to vomit: "this is normal behaviour for dogs," and some dogs simply like the taste or texture. It adds that eating grass "is usually no cause for concern and isn't linked to health issues or dietary deficiencies".

## My dog eats grass and then vomits. Is that a problem?

Once in a while, in a dog that is otherwise well, usually not. If it happens often, or your dog seems unwell, see your vet.

Some dogs bring grass back up. The PDSA's view is that grass-eating is not, in general, a sign that a dog feels sick. The Merck Veterinary Manual notes, though, that problems in the stomach or gut can make dogs eat things that aren't food, and says medical causes "should be excluded first" when eating habits change. So frequent vomiting, eating large amounts of grass, or grass-eating together with diarrhoea, weight loss or a poor appetite are reasons to book a check-up.

## Why does my dog eat poo?

Mostly out of habit, hunger, boredom or stress. A medical cause is possible but uncommon.

The PDSA lists:

- **habit**, often starting in puppyhood
- **hunger**, for example if a dog is underfed, has become more active, or has an illness or medicine that increases appetite
- **boredom, stress or anxiety**
- **attention**: a dog may learn that it gets a reaction, even a telling-off
- **medical or dietary causes**, which the PDSA calls rare

A mother dog eating her puppies' poo is normal, and puppies often grow out of eating poo (PDSA). In a survey of more than 1,500 owners, Hart and colleagues found that 16% of dogs regularly ate dog poo (seen at least six times). Almost all went for fresh poo, no more than two days old. The habit was not linked to diet or age, and these dogs were more often described as greedy eaters. The Merck Veterinary Manual notes that dogs kept confined too much with too little to do may learn to eat poo, and that coprophagia "occasionally has a medical cause". ESCCAP Germany names inflammation of the pancreas as one example.

## Is it dangerous if my dog eats poo?

Usually not, but poo can carry worms and other germs.

The PDSA says eating poo "doesn't often cause health problems for dogs" but is unpleasant. ESCCAP Germany points out that a dog can pick up parasites and germs this way, such as roundworms, *Giardia* or *Salmonella*, and can then pass them on. Its advice is that even when there is no medical cause, a parasite infection should be ruled out or treated, with a poo test or a worming treatment. Ask your vet which suits your dog; see also [fleas, ticks and worms](/animals/fleas-ticks-worms). If your dog eats poo from one particular dog, the PDSA suggests having that dog checked too.

## How can I stop my dog eating poo?

Clear it up, supervise, reward your dog for coming away, and don't punish.

The PDSA recommends:

- **Removing temptation**: pick up poo straight away, walk away from livestock droppings, and use a lead where there is a lot of poo.
- **Distracting and rewarding**: call your dog away in a cheerful voice and reward it with a treat, game or praise.
- **Supervising**, even on quick garden toilet trips.
- **Putting the litter tray out of reach** if you have a cat.
- **Being patient**: breaking the habit takes time and consistency.

Don't shout or punish: the PDSA says this doesn't work, confuses the dog and may make it eat the poo faster. Ask your vet to check for medical causes first, then, if needed, a qualified behaviourist can help.

The Merck Veterinary Manual suggests making meals more engaging, for example by using part of the daily food for training or putting it in food toys, and making sure your dog has enough exercise and things to do.

**Muzzles and food additives.** The sources disagree on muzzles: ESCCAP Germany says a muzzle stops a dog eating poo while you work on the habit, but the PDSA says a muzzle doesn't remove the urge. If you use one, use it alongside training, not instead of it. On additives, the PDSA says there is no evidence that adding pineapple or courgette to food works, and ESCCAP Germany advises against home remedies sold online, because their effect hasn't been studied and harm can't be ruled out. Hart and colleagues found the reported success of commercial anti-poo-eating products was close to zero.

## When should I take my dog to the vet?

When the habit is new or sudden, when your dog is also unwell, or for a worm check if it regularly eats poo.

Book a routine appointment if your dog eats a lot of grass or poo and you want medical causes ruled out, and call sooner if it has any of the signs in the first section. If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know?

What your dog eats and how often, when it started, and whether your dog has any other signs.

- what your dog eats (grass, its own poo, other dogs', cats' or other animals' droppings, soil) and how often
- when it started, and whether anything changed around then (food, routine, a move, a new pet)
- whether your dog vomits or has diarrhoea afterwards, and how often
- your dog's appetite, weight, and any recent weight loss or coat changes
- what and how much you feed, and how quickly your dog eats
- how much exercise and time alone your dog has
- when your dog was last wormed or had a poo test
- your dog's breed, age and any medicines it takes
