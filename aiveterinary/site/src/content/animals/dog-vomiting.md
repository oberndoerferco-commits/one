## First, the signs that mean go now

Go to a veterinary clinic immediately, and call ahead, if your dog:

- is trying to vomit but nothing comes up, especially with a swollen, hard belly (this can be bloat, which is life-threatening within hours)
- vomits blood, or material that looks like coffee grounds
- is very weak, collapses, or has pale or blue-tinged gums
- may have eaten something poisonous: chocolate, grapes or raisins, xylitol (in sugar-free gum and some peanut butters), rat poison, medications, antifreeze
- may have swallowed an object: a toy, sock, corn cob, bone
- is a puppy, very old, or already has a condition such as diabetes or kidney disease
- vomits repeatedly and cannot keep water down

## If none of those apply

A single episode of vomiting in an otherwise bright dog is common. Dogs eat things they shouldn't, eat too fast, or get an upset stomach from a change in food.

What you can do at home for the first 12 hours:

- Take food away for a few hours (not longer than 12 hours, and not at all for puppies or small breeds, which can get low blood sugar).
- Leave water available in small amounts. Large gulps can trigger more vomiting.
- When you reintroduce food, give a small portion of something bland, then return to normal food over two or three days.
- Note what the vomit looked like, how many times, and whether your dog ate anything unusual. Take a photo if you can. Your vet will ask.

## When to see a vet today

Book an appointment today if:

- vomiting happens more than two or three times in a day
- it continues for more than 24 hours
- there is also diarrhoea, especially with blood
- your dog is drinking much more or much less than usual
- your dog is off food for more than a day
- your dog seems in pain: hunched back, reluctance to move, whining when the belly is touched

## What the vet will want to know

- When it started and how often
- What the vomit looked like (food, foam, yellow bile, blood)
- Whether your dog has eaten anything unusual, including scraps and rubbish
- Any medications or supplements
- Appetite, drinking, and whether stools are normal
- Whether other animals in the house are affected

Bringing this information, even as a few notes on your phone, makes the visit faster and the picture clearer.

## Common causes

| Likely | Less common but important |
|---|---|
| Eating something unsuitable ("dietary indiscretion") | Foreign body blocking the gut |
| Sudden food change | Pancreatitis |
| Eating too fast | Kidney or liver disease |
| Mild stomach upset or infection | Poisoning |
| Motion sickness | Bloat (GDV), especially in deep-chested breeds |

Your vet decides which of these is likely for *your* dog, based on the examination. This list is to help you understand the conversation, not to diagnose.
