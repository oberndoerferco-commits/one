## First, the signs that mean go now

Rabbits are different from dogs and cats. Their gut must keep moving all the time. When a rabbit stops eating, the gut slows and can stop altogether. Vets call this gut stasis (gastrointestinal stasis), and it can kill a rabbit within a day or two.

Go to a vet immediately, and call ahead, if your rabbit:

- has not eaten anything for 12 hours, or you are not sure when it last ate
- has passed no droppings, or far fewer and smaller droppings than usual, for 8 to 12 hours
- is sitting hunched up, pressing its belly to the floor, or grinding its teeth loudly (both are signs of pain)
- has a bloated, tight or swollen belly
- is very still, floppy, cold to the touch, or unresponsive
- is breathing fast or with its mouth open
- has a head tilt, is rolling, or cannot stand
- has fly eggs or maggots around its bottom (flystrike)
- has diarrhoea or liquid faeces, especially a young rabbit

Not every clinic is used to treating rabbits. If your usual vet does not see rabbits, ask them now, before you need it, who they refer to.

## While you are arranging the visit

Do not wait and see. A rabbit that refuses food is already an emergency case. While you get to the vet:

- Keep your rabbit warm and quiet, in a carrier with a towel, away from dogs and noise.
- Offer fresh hay, fresh leafy greens and water. Do not force-feed unless your vet has shown you how and told you to; feeding a rabbit with a blocked gut can do harm.
- Bring the rabbit's companion if it has one. They cope better together, and the companion can often go home with it.
- Bring a sample of recent droppings if you can find any.
- Do not give any human medication, and do not give leftover rabbit medication from a previous illness without speaking to your vet.

Whether your rabbit needs fluids, pain relief, gut-stimulating medication or further investigation is a vet decision, and usually a quick one.

## When to see a vet

For rabbits, the honest answer is: as soon as you notice. Even if your rabbit is eating a little, see a vet today if it:

- is eating less than usual, or only picking at favourites and leaving hay
- is dribbling, has a wet chin, or seems to chew awkwardly (dental problems are the most common underlying cause)
- has droppings that are small, hard, misshapen, or strung together with hair
- is passing sticky, soft faeces that stick to its fur
- is drinking much more or much less
- is quieter than normal, or hiding

## What the vet will want to know

- When your rabbit last ate normally, and what it last ate
- When you last saw normal droppings
- Diet: how much hay, which greens and pellets, any recent change
- Whether your rabbit lives indoors or outdoors, alone or with a companion
- Any recent stress: a new animal, building work, a house move, fireworks, extreme heat
- Any previous dental problems, surgery or illness
- Vaccination status (myxomatosis and rabbit haemorrhagic disease)
- Any medication or treatment given at home

## Common causes

| Likely | Less common but important |
|---|---|
| Pain from overgrown or diseased teeth | Blockage of the gut |
| Not enough hay in the diet | Bloat |
| Stress or a sudden change | Infection, including rabbit haemorrhagic disease |
| Pain from another illness or injury | Liver, kidney or bladder disease |
| Hot weather | Cancer, especially in unneutered females |

Gut stasis is often the *result* of something else, most commonly pain. Your vet will want to find the cause as well as get the gut moving again. This list is to help you understand the conversation, not to diagnose.

If your rabbit has not eaten for 12 hours, stop reading and call your own vet, or the nearest vet that treats rabbits, now.
