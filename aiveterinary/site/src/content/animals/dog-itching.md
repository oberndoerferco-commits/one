## First: contact a vet now if you see these signs

Itching is usually not an emergency, but there are exceptions. Contact your vet **immediately** if your dog:

- has a **swollen face**, especially around the eyes, ears, mouth or paws, or a sudden rash. This can be an allergic reaction, for example to a sting or a medicine. The PDSA says never wait to see if it improves
- has **difficulty breathing**, vomiting, diarrhoea, a racing heartbeat or **collapses**: these are signs of a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)
- has scratched or licked itself into **open wounds** that are bleeding, smelly or spreading fast
- is very flat, off food or in pain as well as itchy

If the itching has been going on for days or weeks but your dog is otherwise well, book an appointment in the next few days. The PDSA is clear that itching shouldn't be ignored.

## When is my dog's scratching not normal?

When it happens more than 4 to 5 times a day, or comes with bald patches, red skin, wounds, saliva-stained fur, darkened thick skin or pain.

According to the PDSA, scratching becomes a problem when it happens **more than 4 to 5 times a day**, or when you see:

- **patches of hair loss**
- **red, inflamed skin**
- **self-inflicted wounds**
- **fur stained** by licking, for example on the paws
- **darkened, thickened skin** in long-standing cases
- signs of pain

Some dogs scratch and lick in private, for example at night. Licking the paws, rubbing the face on the floor or nibbling the tail all count as itching.

## Why is my dog itching so much?

The most common causes are fleas and other parasites, skin allergies, and bacterial or yeast skin infections, often more than one at once.

The PDSA lists **fleas, skin allergies, mites and lice, bacterial or yeast (Malassezia) infections, reactions to medicines or cleaning products, hot spots, wounds and ringworm**, and, rarely, immune-mediated skin disease.

### Fleas

Fleas are the first thing to rule out. In dogs allergic to flea bites, the itchy, sore areas are typically over the **lower back, the base of the tail and the backs and insides of the thighs**.

Not seeing fleas doesn't mean they aren't there: very allergic animals can groom so much that they have few or none left. A simple check: run a **fine-toothed flea comb** through the coat and tip what you collect onto a **damp white paper towel**. If the specks dissolve into **reddish-brown** smears, that's flea dirt (digested blood).

If there are fleas, treatment covers **every pet in the household** and **the home**, not just the itchy dog. Getting rid of an infestation completely often takes **up to 3 months**, so keep going. Ask your vet which product to use.

### Atopic dermatitis (environmental allergy)

This is an allergy to things in the environment, such as pollen, moulds and dust. It usually starts **between 6 months and 3 years of age** and mostly affects the **feet, face, ears, front legs and belly**. It can be seasonal or year-round. In a small number of dogs, **repeated ear infections** are the only sign. Golden Retrievers, Labradors and Boxers are among the breeds more prone to it, but any dog can be affected.

Atopic dermatitis is lifelong. It can't be cured, but it can usually be managed with long-term care and regular check-ups.

### Food allergy

Food allergy is less common than environmental allergy. The reliable way to diagnose it is an **elimination diet** set by your vet, for **up to 3 months**, during which your dog eats **only** that food: no treats, table scraps, flavoured medicines or toothpaste that your vet hasn't approved. Even one slip can spoil the result.

### Sarcoptic mange

This is caused by a mite. It typically causes **intense itching that comes on suddenly**, with sores that usually appear first on the **belly, chest, ears, elbows and legs**. It is **highly contagious** between dogs and can spread to **people**. If anyone at home is itchy, tell your vet and your doctor. The mites can be hard to find, especially in dogs that are bathed often, so your vet may start trial treatment if signs strongly suggest mange.

### Skin infections

Bacteria and yeast take advantage of skin that is already damaged by scratching. In allergic dogs, skin and ear infections are common and make the itching worse, so your vet often treats the infection first and then the underlying cause.

## What shouldn't I do if my dog is itchy?

Don't use human creams or medicines without your vet, don't use dog flea products on cats, and don't keep switching shampoos and foods.

- **Don't use human creams, ointments or tablets** (steroids, antihistamines, painkillers) unless your vet tells you to. Whether to use anything, and what, depends on the cause.
- **Don't use a dog flea or tick product on a cat**, and ask your vet what's safe if you have both. Some dog products are highly toxic to cats: see [toxic foods and plants for cats](/animals/cat-toxic-foods-plants).
- **Don't keep changing shampoos and foods** "to see what works". It makes the cause harder to find.

## What will the vet do for my itchy dog?

Examine the skin and ears, look for fleas and mites, possibly take skin scrapings, then suggest flea treatment, a diet trial or treatment for infection and itch.

If mange is suspected, your vet will gently scrape a little material from the skin surface to look at under the microscope. Depending on what they find, they may suggest effective flea control for all pets, an elimination diet, or treatment for infections and itching. Finding the cause can take more than one visit, so keep your follow-up appointments.

## What will the vet want to know?

When and where your dog itches, which flea treatment you use and how often, what your dog eats, and whether other pets or people are itchy.

- when the itching started, and whether it's worse in certain seasons
- where your dog scratches or licks most (photos help)
- which flea and tick products you use, how often, and for which pets
- everything your dog eats, including treats and chews
- whether other pets or people at home are itchy
