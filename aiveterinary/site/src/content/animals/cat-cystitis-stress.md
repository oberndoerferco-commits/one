## Go to a vet now if your cat

Cystitis can lead to a **blocked urethra**, mostly in male cats. Go to a vet straight away, and call ahead, if your cat:

- keeps going to the tray and straining but passes **no urine or only a few drops**
- cries out when trying to pass urine
- has a tense, painful belly and won't let you touch it
- is lethargic, vomiting or not eating

The PDSA calls being unable to pee "a life-threatening emergency". The Cornell Feline Health Center says the time from complete blockage to death may be **less than 24 to 48 hours**. Male cats, neutered or not, are at greater risk because their urethra is longer and narrower. Don't wait until morning, and don't mistake the straining for constipation: if in doubt, assume it's the bladder. See our [emergency page](/emergency).

## What is stress cystitis in cats?

It is a painful inflammation of the bladder with no infection or stones, in which stress plays an important part.

The medical name is **feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC)**. "Idiopathic" means no infection, stone or other specific cause is found. The bladder becomes inflamed and painful. International Cat Care describes it as a common condition, and stress is believed to play an important role: an episode can be triggered by a stressful event.

It isn't bad behaviour or spite. It is real pain, and a cat that links the tray with pain may start peeing elsewhere. Please don't tell your cat off, as that adds to the stress.

## What can trigger it?

Changes and tensions at home, especially conflict with another cat.

The PDSA and International Cat Care list triggers such as:

- moving house, building work or moving furniture
- new people or pets in the home, or household tension
- **conflict with another cat**, which International Cat Care calls the single most common cause of stress in cats with FIC
- too few trays, bowls or resting places for the number of cats
- a change of diet or litter
- changes in the weather

The PDSA says FIC seems most common in **male, neutered, indoor and overweight cats**, cats that get little exercise and cats living with other cats. International Cat Care says it tends to start in **young adult cats**.

## What are the signs of cystitis in cats?

Passing small amounts of urine often, straining, blood in the urine and peeing outside the tray.

- passing urine more often, in small amounts
- spending a long time in the tray, or going in and out
- **blood in the urine**, sometimes only pink spots on the floor or light litter
- peeing outside the tray, often on cool, smooth surfaces such as the bath or sink, or on beds and rugs
- licking the belly or genital area a lot, or overgrooming
- crying in the tray, restlessness or hiding

International Cat Care says episodes often come on quickly and usually settle over **5 to 10 days**, but they commonly **come back**.

## When should I take my cat to the vet?

Book a visit today if you see blood in the urine, peeing outside the tray or frequent straining, and go now if no urine comes out.

Your vet needs to:

- **rule out a blockage** and other causes, such as stones, crystals or infection, with an examination, a urine test and sometimes x-rays or ultrasound. There is no single test for FIC, so it is diagnosed by ruling the others out
- **treat the pain**, which can make your cat feel much better
- help you build a plan for water, food and the home that reduces repeat episodes

International Cat Care notes that cats with FIC don't have a bacterial infection and **should not be given antibiotics**. Your vet will only prescribe them if tests show an infection.

Go back or call straight away if at any point your cat stops passing urine.

## What can I do at home?

Check that urine is coming out, offer water everywhere and more wet food, provide enough clean trays, reduce stress, and never give human medicines.

- **Check urine is coming out.** Clumping litter shows the size of the clumps. With a male cat, keep a close eye on the tray.
- **Water everywhere:** several bowls in different rooms, away from food and trays. International Cat Care suggests shallow ceramic bowls, fountains or flavoured water.
- **More wet food**, if your cat accepts it, to dilute the urine. Switch gradually and with your vet's advice.
- **Enough clean trays:** one per cat plus one extra, in quiet places, with a litter your cat likes. Clean them daily.
- **Less stress:** a steady routine, daily play if your cat enjoys it, hiding places and high perches, and separate food, water and trays for each cat. Your vet may suggest synthetic pheromone products.
- **No human medicines.** The PDSA says never give your cat anything your vet hasn't prescribed. Paracetamol is toxic to cats even in small amounts, and cats are very sensitive to aspirin and ibuprofen.

## What will the vet want to know?

When it started, how often your cat uses the tray, whether there's blood, your cat's details, diet, the home set-up and recent changes.

- when it started and how many times a day your cat goes to the tray
- whether you've seen blood, and where your cat is peeing outside the tray
- whether urine is actually coming out, and how much
- male or female, neutered, age, weight
- what your cat eats and how much it drinks
- how many trays, where they are, and what litter you use
- how many animals and people live in the home, and how they get along
- what has changed in the last few weeks
- previous episodes and how they were treated

Our [visit brief](/visit-brief) helps you get this ready. Ask your vet whether to bring a urine sample and how to collect it.

## What else could it be?

Besides FIC, vets consider bladder stones, urethral plugs, infection and other diseases.

- **Bladder stones**: the Cornell Feline Health Center says struvite and calcium oxalate are the most common types.
- **Urethral plugs**: soft material made of minerals, cells and mucus-like protein, which can block a male cat's urethra.
- **Urinary tract infection**: it can cause similar signs, but International Cat Care says it is far less common in cats.

Your vet will decide which tests your cat needs.

The PDSA says cats with FIC are likely to stay prone to flare-ups and need ongoing management. Every cat has its own triggers, so build the plan for your cat and your home with your vet.
