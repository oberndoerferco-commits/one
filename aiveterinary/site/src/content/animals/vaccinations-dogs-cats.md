## What is the difference between core and non-core vaccines?

Core vaccines are for every dog or cat, while non-core vaccines depend on where your animal lives and how it lives.

Veterinary bodies in Europe divide vaccines into two groups.

**Core vaccines** are the ones every dog or cat should have, wherever it lives, because the diseases are widespread, serious and often fatal.

**Non-core vaccines** are recommended depending on where you live, whether your animal goes outdoors, travels, boards in kennels or catteries, or meets many other animals. Your vet decides which of these apply to your animal, based on its lifestyle and local disease risk.

## Which vaccines does every dog need?

Distemper, parvovirus and adenovirus, plus rabies in many European countries and leptospirosis where it is common.

- **Canine distemper**: a viral disease affecting the lungs, gut and nervous system. Often fatal.
- **Canine parvovirus**: severe, bloody diarrhoea and vomiting, especially in puppies.
- **Canine adenovirus (infectious hepatitis)**: liver disease.
- **Rabies**: core in many European countries and a legal requirement for travel across borders. Fatal in animals and people.

**Leptospirosis** is treated as core where it is common. The risk is higher in places where farm animals and rats live, such as fields, ponds, stagnant water and rivers. It can be passed to people.

Common non-core vaccines for dogs include kennel cough (Bordetella and parainfluenza), and in some regions leishmaniasis or Lyme disease.

## Which vaccines does every cat need?

Panleukopenia, herpesvirus and calicivirus, plus rabies for travel, and FeLV for young or outdoor cats where FeLV is common.

- **Feline panleukopenia** (feline parvovirus): severe vomiting, diarrhoea and collapse, especially in kittens.
- **Feline herpesvirus and feline calicivirus**: the main causes of "cat flu". Rarely fatal in adults but can cause lasting eye and mouth problems.
- **Rabies**: required for travel, and core in some countries.

**Feline leukaemia virus (FeLV)**: where FeLV is common, WSAVA advises treating the vaccine as core for cats under one year old and for adult cats that go outdoors or live with cats that do. International Cat Care classes it as non-core, given according to each cat's risk, for example a cat that goes outside and may meet cats of unknown status.

## Why do puppies and kittens need several vaccinations?

Because antibodies from their mother's milk can block a vaccine, and they fade at a different age in each animal.

Young animals get antibodies from their mother's milk. These protect them for the first weeks of life, but they also block vaccines from working. The antibodies fade at a different age in each animal, so a single injection could land too early.

That is why the first course is a series of injections, typically starting at around 8 weeks and finishing at 16 weeks or later. WSAVA's 2024 guidelines now advise a further dose at or after about 26 weeks (six months) of age, rather than waiting until 12 to 16 months; PDSA and International Cat Care still describe the first booster at about 12 months, and vets may follow either schedule. Until the course is complete, a puppy or kitten is not reliably protected, and your vet will advise on how much contact with other animals and outdoor areas is sensible in the meantime.

## Why does my pet need booster vaccinations?

Immunity from a vaccine does not last forever, and it lasts for different lengths of time for different diseases. A booster reminds the immune system and keeps protection up.

For some core vaccines, such as distemper, parvovirus and infectious hepatitis in dogs, the interval after the first booster is now every three years. Leptospirosis is given every year. For cat flu and panleukopenia, International Cat Care says boosters may only be needed every one to three years, depending on the vaccine, the disease and the cat's risk. Your vet will set the schedule for your animal; it is not the same for every dog or cat.

If a booster is overdue, ask your vet what to do next; PDSA notes that a dog may stay protected for only about two to three months past the due date. For rabies, EU rules apply: if the booster is given after the previous vaccination has expired, it counts as a new first vaccination, and you must wait at least 21 days before travelling.

## What does the vet record after a vaccination?

Every vaccination is written into your animal's record and, if it has one, its pet passport or vaccination card. For travel in the EU, the pet passport holds the rabies vaccination record.

Keep this document safe, and ask your vet when each vaccine is next due. Kennels, catteries and border officials may ask to see it.

## What are the side effects of vaccines in dogs and cats?

Most animals have no reaction. Some are a little quiet, eat less, sleep more, have a mild fever or are sore at the injection site for a few days. A small swelling at the site is not unusual; ask your vet to check it if it lasts more than two to three weeks or is getting bigger. Call your vet if you are worried, if your animal gets worse, or if signs last more than 24 to 48 hours.

Take your animal to the vet straight away, phoning on the way, if it has swelling anywhere on the body (such as the face), difficulty breathing, a seizure or collapses after a vaccination. If your animal has reacted to a vaccine before, tell your vet before the next one.

## What is titre testing for dogs and cats?

Blood tests that measure antibody levels can show whether a dog or cat still has protection against some diseases. They are an option your vet may discuss, particularly for animals that have reacted to vaccines before. They do not cover every disease and are not accepted in place of a rabies vaccination for travel.

For the right schedule for your own dog or cat, and which non-core vaccines make sense where you live, ask your own vet.
