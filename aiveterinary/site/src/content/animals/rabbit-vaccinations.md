## First: signs that mean call your vet now

Vaccination is planned care, but the diseases it prevents are emergencies. Call your vet **straight away**, and say you suspect an infectious disease, if your rabbit:

- has **blood around the nose, mouth or bottom**
- is suddenly **lethargic**, has a high temperature, or is **eating less** or has stopped eating
- has **red, swollen or puffy eyes**, or swelling of the face, ears, bottom or genitals
- has a companion rabbit that **died suddenly** without an obvious cause

The PDSA says rabbit haemorrhagic disease (RHD) often moves so fast that rabbits die before their owner notices any symptoms, and that there is no cure. A rabbit that stops eating is an emergency for many reasons; see [my rabbit is not eating](/animals/rabbit-not-eating).

## Which vaccinations does my rabbit need?

Vaccines against three diseases: myxomatosis and the two types of rabbit haemorrhagic disease, RHD1 and RHD2 (also called RVHD).

The PDSA and the RSPCA both list these three. All three can kill:

- **Myxomatosis** is a virus that causes puffy swellings around the face, ears, eyes and genitals, then fever and difficulty eating. The RSPCA says signs can take up to 14 days to appear and that it usually kills within 10 to 14 days. The Rabbit Welfare Association (RWAF) says unvaccinated rabbits nearly always die.
- **RHD1** causes very sudden illness and, according to the PDSA, is nearly always fatal within two days.
- **RHD2** usually develops more slowly, over one to two weeks (PDSA), and often the only sign is sudden death. The RSPCA says it was first detected in France in 2010 and has been in the UK since 2013, and that rabbits of all ages can catch it. The MSD Veterinary Manual gives a death rate of 60 to 90 percent for RHD.

## Does an indoor rabbit need vaccinating?

Yes: indoor rabbits can catch these diseases too, because insects, other pets and people's clothes and shoes can carry them into the home.

- The PDSA recommends vaccinating both indoor and outdoor rabbits, because the diseases spread through insects, wild rabbits, and on surfaces such as clothes and shoes.
- The RWAF says "All pet rabbits – indoors or outdoors – are at risk" of myxomatosis, which is mainly spread by fleas and mosquitoes. It says the main route for RHD is clothes and shoes.
- The PDSA warns that fleas from cats and dogs can spread RHD. The MSD Veterinary Manual says people can carry RHD on contaminated shoes and clothing.
- In a 2025 presentation for FNOVI, the federation of Italian veterinary associations, Antonio Lavazza notes that RHD most often spreads indirectly, through contaminated food, objects and equipment or carried passively by other animals, people included, and that mosquitoes spread myxomatosis from spring to autumn.
- Food can carry it as well. Germany's vaccination commission (StIKo Vet) notes that pet rabbits can pick up myxomatosis from contaminated fresh grass, and the veterinary office of the German district of Cuxhaven advises not feeding pet rabbits plants from meadows where wild rabbits go.

## How often does my rabbit need vaccinating?

Usually at least once a year, starting from a few weeks old, but the exact plan depends on the vaccines your vet uses, and the sources differ.

Here is what each source says. **Your vet decides** which vaccines and which schedule suit your rabbit.

- **PDSA:** the first vaccine at around five weeks old, then a booster every year. In the UK there are two vaccines: one covers all three diseases, the other only RHD2. When both are given, they are spaced two weeks apart.
- **RSPCA:** a combined myxomatosis and RHD vaccine every year from five weeks old, plus a separate RHD2 vaccine at 10 weeks; or a single vaccine against all three diseases every year from five weeks old. Ask your vet for the right course.
- **RWAF (update of 28 May 2026):** all rabbits should now have **two vaccinations a year**: the "triple" vaccine (myxomatosis, RHD1 and RHD2) and a newer vaccine that also covers a **highly virulent strain of RHD2**. That strain has been found in continental Europe but, according to the RWAF, not yet in the UK. The two can be given two weeks or more apart, but the RWAF prefers six months apart, so that your rabbit also gets a six-month health check. It says the two vaccines are not licensed to be given at the same visit.
- **MSD Veterinary Manual:** outside the USA, vaccines against RHD1 and RHD2 are available in several countries; the schedule it gives is vaccination at 10 weeks with yearly boosters.
- **StIKo Vet, Germany (guideline of 2019):** a first course at 4 to 6 weeks old and again 3 weeks later, then myxomatosis boosters every 6 months (every 4 months in areas where it is widespread) and RHD every 12 months, with boosters every 12 months for the combined myxomatosis and RHD vaccine. Because RHD2 also kills young rabbits, it advises starting as early as possible. The guideline gives different intervals for different vaccines.

The RWAF also advises **not vaccinating at the same time as neutering**. Plan the two on separate visits with your vet.

## Are the vaccines available in Italy, Germany and the rest of Europe?

Yes: vaccines against myxomatosis and both types of RHD are available in Europe, and both diseases occur in Italy and Germany.

- The MSD Veterinary Manual says myxomatosis vaccination is an option outside the USA and highly recommended where the disease is common, and that RHD1 and RHD2 vaccines are available in several countries outside the USA.
- **Germany:** StIKo Vet says RHD2 has occurred in Germany since 2013 and that vaccines against it are available; it strongly recommends regular vaccination. The Cuxhaven veterinary office recommends vaccinating pet rabbits regularly against RHD (types 1 and 2) and myxomatosis, which it says occurs in many regions of Germany.
- **Italy:** in the same FNOVI presentation (March 2025), Lavazza reports that RHD2 has been endemic throughout Italy since 2013 and has for some years also been frequent in pet rabbits, and that myxomatosis has been endemic in Europe and Italy for over 75 years.

Ask your vet which ones they use and whether they cover RHD2.

## Does vaccination protect my rabbit completely?

No vaccine is 100 percent effective, but vaccinated rabbits are much better protected, and those that catch myxomatosis usually survive.

The RWAF says vaccination doesn't guarantee protection from myxomatosis, but vaccinated rabbits that catch it usually have a milder illness, sometimes just a single skin lesion, and usually survive. So vaccination works best alongside other steps the RWAF, the PDSA and the Cuxhaven veterinary office suggest:

- insect screens on outdoor runs and hutches, and on windows and doors
- no standing water in the garden, where mosquitoes breed
- regular flea treatment for cats and dogs in the home, with products your vet recommends: the RWAF warns that some are toxic to rabbits
- no contact with wild rabbits, and no fresh plants from meadows where wild rabbits go
- vaccinating new rabbits and keeping them apart from your rabbits for at least three weeks (PDSA)

## Are there side effects?

They are rare and usually mild: tiredness, eating a little less, or a small lump where the injection went in, usually gone within a day or two.

The PDSA says side effects usually pass within 24 to 48 hours. **Call your vet if your rabbit stops eating** after a vaccination, or if you are worried. The MSD Veterinary Manual notes that mild, local reactions to the live myxomatosis vaccine have been reported.

## How much do rabbit vaccinations cost?

Prices vary between practices, so ask yours. The PDSA gives no figures and advises calling your practice for prices.

## When should I take my rabbit to the vet for vaccinations?

As soon as your vet advises for a young rabbit, then for every booster on time, and straight away for any sign of illness.

- **Young rabbits:** the RWAF recommends vaccinating as soon as possible after five weeks old. Ask your vet when to come in.
- **Newly adopted rabbits:** book a check and ask whether the vaccinations are up to date and include RHD2.
- **Boosters:** keep to the dates your vet gives you. If your vet uses two vaccines, ask how far apart they should be.
- **Illness:** go straight away for any of the signs at the top of this page.

If a rabbit dies suddenly and RHD is suspected, the PDSA says the virus can survive for months on bowls, water bottles, walls and grass. Ask your vet how to disinfect before any other rabbit uses the area. If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know about my rabbit's vaccinations?

Your rabbit's age and history, which vaccines it has had and when, how it lives, and any contact with other animals.

- your rabbit's age, and whether it is neutered or will be soon
- which vaccines it has had, and the dates (bring the vaccination record if you have one)
- whether it has ever been vaccinated against RHD2
- whether it lives indoors, outdoors or both, and whether wild rabbits come into the garden
- other rabbits, cats and dogs in the home, and their flea treatment
- whether you feed fresh grass or plants you have picked
- any recent shows, boarding or contact with other rabbits
- any health problems, recent illness or loss of appetite
