## First: call your vet now if an unspayed female shows these signs

If your female dog or cat has **not been spayed** and seems unwell in the weeks after a season, **call your vet today, and go straight in** if she:

- has **pus or bloody discharge** from her vulva (back end)
- is **drinking or weeing much more** than usual
- is **vomiting**
- has a **swollen or bloated tummy**
- is **lethargic**, off her food, or **collapses**

The PDSA says to contact your vet immediately if you see these signs, and that "left untreated, a pyometra can cause death". The MSD Veterinary Manual warns that the signs "can progress rapidly to shock and death". A dog or cat with no discharge can be just as ill, or more so (see open and closed pyometra below).

## What is pyometra?

Pyometra is an infection of the womb (uterus) in which the womb fills with pus. Only females that have not been spayed can get it.

The MSD Veterinary Manual describes it as a bacterial infection of the uterus caused by hormonal changes in unspayed females. After a season the hormone progesterone stays high and thickens the lining of the womb. When this happens over several seasons without a pregnancy, cysts can form in the lining, and bacteria thrive there. The MSD manual adds that medicines containing oestrogen or progesterone can also trigger it.

Male dogs and cats cannot get pyometra, because they have no womb (PDSA).

## When does pyometra happen?

Usually in the weeks after a season, most often in middle-aged or older females, but it can happen at any age.

- **Timing:** the PDSA says pyometra most often develops four to eight weeks after a season. The MSD Veterinary Manual gives four to six weeks. Take any signs seriously in the two months after a season.
- **Age:** the PDSA says any unspayed female dog can get it, and the risk rises after six years of age. The MSD manual says it mainly affects dogs over 5 years old.
- **Cats:** the MSD Veterinary Manual says pyometra is less common in cats than in dogs, but the signs are similar and it is just as serious.

For what a normal season looks like, see our article on [dogs in heat](/animals/dog-in-heat).

## What is the difference between open and closed pyometra?

In open pyometra the cervix is open and pus drains out; in closed pyometra the cervix is shut, nothing drains, and the womb swells with pus.

- **Open pyometra:** the PDSA says blood and pus usually leak from the vulva, so it is easier to notice. The MSD manual describes a pus-like discharge, often containing blood.
- **Closed pyometra:** there is often no discharge at all, and the enlarged womb may make the tummy swell (MSD). The PDSA calls this type "particularly dangerous because you are unlikely to see any discharge", and warns that pressure can build up in the womb until it bursts.

So no discharge does not mean no pyometra.

## Why is pyometra an emergency?

Because the infection can spread through the body, damage the kidneys, and the womb can burst, and without treatment it can kill.

The PDSA says that untreated, pus can leak into the tummy and cause peritonitis (infection inside the tummy), sepsis (a body-wide infection), dehydration, kidney failure and death. The MSD Veterinary Manual says the signs can progress rapidly to shock and death, and that a womb with a closed cervix may burst. The professional version of the manual lists signs of kidney problems, such as drinking and weeing more, among the common signs.

The PDSA adds that the earlier treatment starts, the better the chances of survival and recovery, and that the outlook is worse in older dogs, very unwell dogs, and when the pyometra has been developing for a long time.

## What will the vet do?

Examine her, usually scan the womb and take blood tests, then in most cases operate to remove the infected womb.

- **Tests.** The PDSA says the diagnosis is based on the signs, an examination and an ultrasound scan of the womb, sometimes with blood or urine tests. The MSD manual also lists X-rays and lab tests.
- **Stabilising.** Very ill animals are often given a fluid drip and antibiotics before surgery (MSD), and pain relief (PDSA).
- **Surgery.** The PDSA says most dogs need emergency surgery to remove the womb. For dogs, the MSD Veterinary Manual says spaying (removing the ovaries and uterus) is the recommended treatment in most cases; for cats it calls spaying "the best treatment in most cases". The professional version recommends surgery for all cases unless the owner strongly wants to breed from her.
- **Medical treatment.** Hormone treatment without surgery is only an option in particular cases, such as a young breeding female that is not seriously ill, and the MSD manual says it is riskier when the cervix is closed. The PDSA and the MSD manual both say pyometra often comes back after a later season, so surgery may still be needed. Which treatment is right is your vet's decision.

**Recovery.** The PDSA says dogs may stay in hospital for a day or two, and then need rest at home, a cone or recovery suit, the medicines your vet prescribes, and watching for pain.

## Can pyometra be prevented?

Yes: spaying prevents pyometra, because there is no womb left to become infected.

The PDSA advises discussing with your vet when to spay, and, until then, watching her closely after every season. It notes that in rare cases a "stump pyometra" can develop after spaying if a small piece of ovary was left behind; that tissue then needs to be removed.

Never give hormone medicines to stop or delay a season unless your vet prescribes them, since the MSD manual says such medicines can trigger pyometra.

## When should I take my dog or cat to the vet?

Straight away for any of the signs at the top of this page in an unspayed female, especially in the two months after a season.

Also call your vet if she is simply "not herself" after a season: drinking more, eating less, or quieter than usual. The professional version of the MSD manual says the signs vary and often come on gradually. Our article on [a dog drinking a lot](/animals/dog-drinking-a-lot) explains other causes of extra thirst. If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know about my dog or cat?

When her last season was, what signs you have seen and since when, and whether she has had hormone treatment.

- whether she is spayed, and when her last season started and ended
- any discharge: what it looks like and how much
- whether she is drinking or weeing more, eating less, or has vomited
- whether her tummy looks bigger
- whether she has been mated, or may be pregnant
- any hormone medicines, for example to stop or delay a season
- her age, weight, other health problems and any medicines she takes
