## First, the signs that mean go now

Go to a vet who treats birds now, and call ahead, if your bird:

- is **bleeding**, for example from a broken feather, and the bleeding doesn't stop. The MSD Veterinary Manual says ongoing bleeding of any kind, especially from the wing, beak or foot, is an emergency
- has **wounds** or is damaging its **skin**, not just its feathers. MSD warns that birds with deep or large wounds are at risk of infection and dying
- is also **fluffed up, sleepy, weak** or sitting on the **cage floor**
- is **breathing** with an open beak or a bobbing tail
- has **stopped eating**

Without these signs, book a visit within the week (see below).

## Is my bird moulting, or losing feathers because something is wrong?

Birds normally moult at least once a year; feather loss outside a normal moult is usually caused by a problem that needs a vet.

The MSD Veterinary Manual explains that birds normally lose and replace most of their feathers at least once a year, and often have a partial moult about six months later. Abnormal feather loss, MSD says, can be slow and partial or sudden and complete. VCA Animal Hospitals adds that true feather loss, meaning loss that is not part of a normal moult, is typically the result of disease, and its list of signs of illness includes an unusually long moult.

Clues that feathers are being removed rather than moulted:

- MSD notes that plucked feathers can be shredded or chewed, unlike feathers lost in a normal moult.
- The Tierspital Zürich, the University of Zurich's animal hospital, explains that a bird plucking itself usually starts under the wings and on the chest, while the head stays feathered because the bird can't reach it. If the head is bare too, a partner bird may be doing the plucking.
- MSD describes psittacine beak and feather disease (PBFD), a viral disease, as causing feather loss in places the bird can't reach, such as the top of the head.

VCA says that knowing whether the feathers fall out or are being removed, by your bird or a cage mate, helps narrow down the cause. Watch your bird and, if you can, take photos for your vet.

## Why is my bird pulling its feathers out?

There are medical and behavioural causes, often more than one, and your vet has to rule out illness first.

The MSD Veterinary Manual calls it feather destructive behaviour. It ranges from mildly excessive preening to severe self-mutilation of feathers and skin. Causes include medical conditions and psychological problems such as stress, boredom and sexual frustration, and behavioural causes are usually suspected when no medical cause is found.

Medical causes named by VCA, MSD and the Tierspital Zürich include:

- viral infections, among them PBFD
- liver disease
- parasites
- a poor diet or nutritional deficiencies
- skin infections
- poisoning, injuries, hormonal disorders, kidney problems, and bacterial or fungal infections

On behaviour, the Tierspital Zürich says feather plucking is usually a behavioural disorder, particularly common in parrots such as cockatoos, African greys, macaws and Amazons, and that the cause often lies in how the bird is kept. It names being kept alone as the most common cause. It also names changes in the daily routine or surroundings, an unsuitable partner, boredom, too little exercise, a poor diet with obesity, an unsuitable climate, lack of sleep and fear. VCA says behavioural plucking is seen especially in larger species. The RSPCA adds too little foraging, and a poor diet leading to poor feather condition.

## Why is my budgie or canary losing feathers?

The same rules apply to small birds: a normal moult is expected, but loss outside the moult, misshapen feathers or crusty skin need a vet.

What the MSD Veterinary Manual says about some common worries:

- **Scaly face and leg mites** are common in budgies. They cause a build-up of flaky, dry material on the beak, face and legs, and your vet can confirm them by looking at a skin scraping under the microscope.
- **Feather mites**, despite common belief, rarely affect pet birds.
- **PBFD** has no cure, and infected birds should be kept apart from other birds.

VCA says that if feathers are misshapen and fall out easily, PBFD may be the cause and needs immediate attention.

## What can I do at home for a bird that plucks?

Have your bird checked by a vet first. Then work with your vet on company, routine, diet and enrichment, don't scold your bird, and don't use collars or other products on your own.

- **Don't react.** The RSPCA says that saying "no" or "stop that" when you see your bird self-plucking can actually encourage it. Instead, take yourself out of your bird's sight for a few minutes.
- **Company.** The RSPCA says most birds are social animals who do better with the company of other birds. The Tierspital Zürich says pair or group housing is needed to meet a parrot's social needs, and that it can bring the best results.
- **Routine and enrichment.** The Tierspital Zürich advises making the bird's day as varied and interesting as possible, and the RSPCA mentions foraging.
- **Diet.** Improve it if needed, with your vet's advice (Tierspital Zürich, VCA).
- **Collars.** VCA says they should only be used under the supervision of an avian vet. The Tierspital Zürich notes that a collar can only help for a while and does not remove the cause.

Don't wait for it to pass. The Tierspital Zürich says the outlook depends on how long the problem has lasted: if it started recently and consistent changes are made, the outlook is usually good, but after months or years, treatment is usually unsuccessful.

## When should I take my bird to the vet?

Within the week for any plucking or feather loss outside a normal moult; sooner if the feathers look misshapen, the skin is damaged or your bird seems unwell.

Book a visit with a vet who treats birds if your bird:

- plucks, chews or over-preens its feathers
- loses feathers outside its normal moult, or the moult seems to go on and on
- has misshapen feathers, or feathers that fall out easily (VCA says this needs immediate attention)
- has crusty, flaky material on its beak, face or legs
- has flaky or sore skin, or scratches a lot
- is being plucked by another bird

VCA recommends a thorough work-up by a vet familiar with birds. The Tierspital Zürich describes what that involves: a detailed history and general examination, blood and faecal tests and X-rays, and, it recommends, a feather and skin biopsy and cultures for fungi and bacteria.

## What will the vet want to know about my bird?

When it started, where on the body, whether you have seen plucking, how your bird is kept and fed, and any recent changes.

- when you first noticed it, and where on the body the feathers are missing
- whether you have seen your bird plucking or chewing, or feathers just falling out
- whether another bird could be plucking it
- its diet, including treats
- how it is kept: cage or aviary, time out of the cage, and whether it lives alone or with other birds
- its daily routine, and how many hours of darkness and quiet it gets to sleep
- recent changes: a move, a new person or pet, holidays, a different routine
- signs of sexual behaviour or egg laying
- other signs: itching, changed droppings, eating less, weight loss
- when it last moulted, and photos of the feathers if you have them
