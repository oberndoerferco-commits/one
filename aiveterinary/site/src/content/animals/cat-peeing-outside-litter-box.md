## First: if your cat strains and little or no urine comes out, go now

Go to a vet **now**, day or night, and call ahead, if your cat:

- keeps going to the litter box or to other spots and **strains, but passes little or no urine**
- **cries** while trying to pee
- keeps **licking its genital area**, is restless, or seems more and more unwell
- is also **vomiting**, refusing food or hiding

These can be signs of a **blocked urethra**, the tube that carries urine out of the bladder. International Cat Care says it happens almost exclusively in **male cats**, can cause kidney failure and can be fatal within 2 to 3 days. The Cornell Feline Health Center warns that a completely blocked cat may be less than 24 to 48 hours from death. Our article on [a cat straining in the litter box](/animals/cat-straining-litter-box) explains how to tell a blockage from constipation.

**Call your vet today** if your cat can still pee but there is **blood in the urine**, it pees much more often than usual, or it seems to hurt.

## Is my cat peeing outside the box out of spite?

No: cats don't soil to take revenge; it is a sign that your cat feels unwell, is in pain or stressed, or doesn't like its box.

The Cornell Feline Health Center says house soiling is the most common behaviour problem cat owners report, and that cats probably don't have the kind of thinking needed for revenge. International Cat Care says the same: soiling is a sign that a cat is unhappy, unwell or stressed.

**Please don't punish your cat**, shout at it or rub its nose in the mess. Both organisations warn that punishment makes cats more anxious or fearful and can make the problem worse, and Cornell adds that your cat can't connect the punishment with the mess.

## What medical problems make cats pee outside the litter box?

Bladder and urinary problems are the main ones, along with kidney disease, an overactive thyroid, diabetes, gut problems and painful joints.

Cornell says any condition that interferes with normal peeing or pooing can cause litter box problems, and that cats with a medical condition "may not always act sick". It lists:

- **inflammation of the urinary tract**, including bladder problems
- **kidney disease**, **thyroid disease** and **diabetes**, which can make a cat drink and pee more ([kidney disease](/animals/cat-kidney-disease), [thyroid](/animals/thyroid-dogs-cats), [diabetes](/animals/diabetes-dogs-cats))
- **digestive problems**, such as diarrhoea or constipation
- **age-related problems** with getting around or with thinking (cognition)

Within urinary problems, Cornell says **idiopathic cystitis**, bladder inflammation without a clear cause, is the most common diagnosis. Other causes include bladder stones, urethral plugs and infection. International Cat Care says idiopathic cystitis is linked to stress and that peeing outside the tray is a common sign. Our article on [stress cystitis](/animals/cat-cystitis-stress) has more.

One problem can lead to another: Cornell says a cat with a urinary problem that can't reach the box in time may get used to a new spot, and keep using it after it has recovered.

International Cat Care adds that **older cats with arthritis** may struggle with high-sided trays, stairs or the cat flap.

## Is my cat peeing or spraying?

A cat that pees squats and leaves a puddle, usually on a flat surface; a cat that sprays stands, lifts and quivers its tail and leaves a small amount on an upright surface.

| | Peeing | Spraying (marking) |
|---|---|---|
| Posture | squats | stands, tail up and quivering |
| Where | usually flat: bed, carpet, bath, clothes | usually upright: walls, the back of a chair |
| Amount | a puddle | a small amount |

Cornell says spraying is a way of marking territory or signalling readiness to mate, and is most common in unneutered cats. But 10 percent of neutered males and 5 percent of neutered females spray too, and in homes with more than seven cats, one or more is likely to spray. Triggers include a new cat, cats outside the window, and new furniture or carpet. If you're not sure which it is, Cornell suggests watching, or setting up a camera while you're out.

## Could the litter box be the problem?

Yes: cats can go off a box because of its size, place, litter, cover or cleanliness.

International Cat Care and Cornell agree on most points:

- **Number:** one box per cat, plus one more. A single cat needs two boxes, two cats need three.
- **Size:** International Cat Care's guide is a tray about 1.5 times the length of your cat, so it can turn round.
- **Place:** quiet and private, away from food bowls, and reachable at any time of day. In multi-cat homes, spread the boxes around rather than putting them side by side, where one cat can block another.
- **Litter:** most cats prefer fine, unscented, sand-like litter. Change brands gradually; International Cat Care suggests over a week.
- **Depth:** Cornell suggests about one to two inches (around 2.5 to 5 cm); International Cat Care recommends at least 3 cm, and says some cats like it deeper.
- **Cleaning:** Cornell says to remove waste from clumping litter every day; International Cat Care says to remove clumps and solids at least twice a day and to clean the whole tray at least once a week with mild detergent and hot water. Avoid strong-smelling cleaners and disinfectants that turn cloudy in water: International Cat Care says these usually contain phenols, which are toxic to cats.
- **Cover:** Cornell says most cats prefer an open box; International Cat Care says some prefer covered ones. If your cat isn't using one type, try the other.
- **Liners:** Cornell says many cats don't like them; International Cat Care advises against them.
- **Older or stiff cats:** low sides, a cut-out entrance and soft litter.

## Can stress make my cat pee outside the box?

Yes: stress can trigger both bladder inflammation and soiling, and the most common source is other cats.

International Cat Care says cats feel most vulnerable while they toilet. Other cats are the most common trigger, but a neighbour's dog, sudden loud noises and severe weather can also play a part. A bad experience at the box, such as being given a tablet or grabbed while using it, can put a cat off. Cornell names multi-cat homes and changes in routine, such as moving house, as risk factors for urinary problems.

What can help, according to Cornell and International Cat Care: keep boxes in quiet, safe places; keep routines steady; give your cat climbing places and toys to chase; make sure fresh water is always available; and, for cats bothered by cats outside, block the view.

## How do I clean the spots so my cat doesn't go back?

Clean as soon as possible with a cleaner designed for cat urine, and avoid ammonia or vinegar, which smell like urine to a cat.

International Cat Care recommends an enzymatic cleaner made for cat urine and faeces, and keeping your cat away from the spot until the smell is gone, because a cat can still smell it and may go back. Cornell says odours must be neutralised, not just covered up. Cornell also suggests making the spot less attractive, for example by putting food bowls or toys there, or closing the door to that room.

## When should I take my cat to the vet?

As soon as the soiling happens more than once, and immediately if your cat strains without passing urine.

International Cat Care advises contacting your vet once soiling becomes regular, so that medical causes can be ruled out first; your vet may then suggest a behaviour specialist. Cornell advises acting promptly, because the longer the behaviour goes on, the more likely it is to become a habit. Your vet will usually want to test a urine sample.

## What will the vet want to know about my cat's peeing?

When it started, where and how often, what it looks like, and what has changed at home.

- when it started, and whether it is getting more frequent
- where your cat pees, and whether it squats or stands
- whether your cat still uses the box at all, and for pee, poo or both
- whether you have seen straining, crying, blood in the urine, or more drinking
- your cat's age, sex, whether it is neutered, and any health problems
- the number, place and type of boxes and litter, and how often you clean them
- any changes: a new pet, person or furniture, a house move, building work, cats outside
- how many cats live with you and how they get on
- any medicines your cat takes
