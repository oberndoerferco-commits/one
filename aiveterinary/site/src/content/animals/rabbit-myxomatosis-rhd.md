## First: call your vet now if your rabbit has these signs

**Call your vet straight away**, and tell them on the phone that you suspect myxomatosis or RHD, if your rabbit:

- has **red, swollen or puffy eyes**, or swellings on the face, ears, lips, bottom or genitals
- has **blood around the nose, mouth or bottom**
- is suddenly **lethargic**, feels hot, or is **eating less** or has stopped eating
- has a **companion rabbit that died suddenly** without an obvious cause

Saying this on the phone helps the practice prepare. The RSPCA advises keeping rabbits away from affected rabbits, so keep a sick rabbit apart from your others.

The PDSA says to book an urgent appointment for any of these signs, especially if your rabbit has stopped eating. A rabbit that stops eating is an emergency for many reasons; see [my rabbit is not eating](/animals/rabbit-not-eating).

## What are myxomatosis and RHD?

Two different viral diseases of rabbits. Both are often fatal, and there is no cure for either.

- **Myxomatosis** is caused by the myxoma virus. The MSD Veterinary Manual says it is fatal in domestic rabbits.
- **Rabbit haemorrhagic disease (RHD)**, also called RVHD or viral haemorrhagic disease, is caused by a different virus. There are two types, RHD1 and RHD2. The MSD manual says RHD2 was first found in France in 2010, and the RSPCA says it has been in the UK since 2013.

Neither disease is caught by people (MSD), but people can carry RHD on their shoes and clothes.

## What are the signs of myxomatosis?

Red, swollen eyes, then puffy swellings of the head, ears and genitals, fever, and difficulty eating and drinking.

- The RWAF says the eyes look red and swollen at the start, and later start to scab.
- The MSD Veterinary Manual describes eye inflammation with a milky discharge, lethargy, loss of appetite and a high fever first. Then the eyelids, nose, lips and ears swell, giving a "big head" look, the ears may droop, and the genitals swell. Before death, rabbits have a thick nasal discharge and difficulty breathing.
- The RSPCA says the swellings can cause blindness, and that eating and drinking become harder.

**How fast:** the RSPCA says signs can take up to 14 days to appear, and that the disease often kills within 10 to 14 days. The MSD manual says some rabbits die within 48 hours of the signs starting, in acute outbreaks.

## What are the signs of RHD?

Often none: many rabbits are simply found dead. Some have bleeding from the nose, mouth or bottom, a fever, low energy or loss of appetite first.

The PDSA lists blood around the nose, mouth or bottom, lethargy, eating less, a high temperature, and sudden death; it says infected rabbits often die before their owner notices any symptoms. The RSPCA describes RHD1 as causing high fever, internal bleeding and liver disease, and says the only sign of RHD2 is often sudden death.

**How fast:**

- **RHD1:** the PDSA says it is nearly always fatal within two days of catching it. The MSD manual says rabbits often die 6 to 24 hours after the fever starts.
- **RHD2:** the PDSA says it often develops more slowly and is often fatal within one to two weeks. The RSPCA says it kills fewer rabbits than RHD1, but rabbits of all ages can catch it, while RHD1 does not affect rabbits under six weeks old.
- The MSD manual gives an incubation period of about 3 to 9 days, during which rabbits are already highly contagious, and a death rate of 60 to 90%.

The PDSA warns that rabbits with RHD2 survive longer with symptoms, so they are more likely to spread the disease.

## Why are myxomatosis and RHD emergencies?

Because both kill most unvaccinated rabbits, often within days, and an infected rabbit can pass the virus on.

- **Myxomatosis:** the RWAF says unvaccinated rabbits nearly always die. The RSPCA says there is no specific treatment and recovery is rare, and that euthanasia is usually the kindest option for an infected rabbit. Vaccinated rabbits usually have a milder illness and recover well if a vet treats them quickly (RSPCA, RWAF).
- **RHD:** the PDSA says there is no cure, that RHD is fatal in most cases, and that if symptoms are severe, euthanasia is often the kindest option. The RSPCA says some rabbits recover from RHD2.

Only your vet can tell whether your rabbit can be helped, or whether putting it to sleep is kinder. Do not try to treat a sick rabbit at home.

## How do rabbits catch myxomatosis and RHD?

Myxomatosis mainly through bites from fleas and mosquitoes; RHD mainly through contact with infected rabbits and things they have touched, including people's shoes and clothes. Indoor rabbits can catch both.

**Insects:**

- The RWAF says fleas and mosquitoes that have bitten an infected rabbit are the main route for myxomatosis, and midges and mites are suspected too. The RSPCA and the MSD manual add biting flies.
- The PDSA says insect bites can also pass on RHD, and that fleas from cats and dogs can spread it.

**Contact:**

- Myxomatosis also spreads by direct contact between rabbits, especially through secretions from their breathing (RWAF), and through contaminated objects and surroundings (RSPCA).
- RHD spreads through close contact with infected rabbits, often wild ones, and through the air (PDSA), and indirectly via contaminated objects. The MSD manual says people can carry it on contaminated shoes and clothing.
- The PDSA says the RHD virus can survive for months on surfaces such as bowls, water bottles, walls and grass.

The RWAF says all pet rabbits, indoors or outdoors, are at risk of myxomatosis, and outdoor rabbits especially where wild rabbits come into the garden.

## How can I protect my rabbit?

Vaccinate against myxomatosis, RHD1 and RHD2, and cut down contact with insects, wild rabbits and anything they may have touched.

**Vaccination** is the most important step (RWAF). Our article on [rabbit vaccinations](/animals/rabbit-vaccinations) explains which vaccines cover which disease, how often they are given, and why the advice has recently changed. The PDSA stresses vaccinating against both RHD1 and RHD2. No vaccine gives complete protection, but the RWAF says vaccinated rabbits that catch myxomatosis usually survive.

**Insects:**

- fit insect screens or mosquito netting on hutches and runs (RWAF, PDSA)
- remove standing water where mosquitoes breed, or cover water butts with a sealed lid (RWAF)
- treat cats and dogs in the home for fleas, after checking with your vet that the product is safe around rabbits (RWAF)
- keep housing clean and change bedding often (RSPCA, PDSA)

**Contact:**

- keep wild rabbits out of the garden, for example with double fencing, and prevent nose-to-nose contact (PDSA, RWAF)
- keep your rabbits away from sick rabbits (RSPCA)
- vaccinate new rabbits and keep them apart from your rabbits for at least three weeks (PDSA)
- buy hay and straw from sources with no myxomatosis on the land, or use dust-extracted hay or kiln-dried grass (RWAF)

## What should I do if my rabbit has died of RHD?

Ask your vet how to clean up, throw away what can't be disinfected, and only let fully vaccinated rabbits into the area again.

The PDSA advises throwing away the enclosure and other items, getting your vet's advice on how to disinfect, and only letting fully vaccinated rabbits use the area in future, because the virus survives for months. If a surviving rabbit lost its companion to RHD, bring it and any new rabbit up to date with their vaccinations and wait at least three weeks before introducing them.

## When should I take my rabbit to the vet?

Straight away for any of the signs at the top of this page, and for routine vaccinations as your vet advises.

- **Now:** swollen or red eyes, swellings on the head or genitals, bleeding, sudden lethargy, or not eating.
- **Now, too:** if one of your rabbits has died suddenly, ask your vet about the others, even if they seem well.
- **Planned:** vaccinations and boosters, and a check for any new rabbit before it meets yours.

If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know about my rabbit?

The signs and when they began, its vaccination history, how it lives, and any contact with other rabbits, wild rabbits or insects.

- what signs you have seen and when they started
- when your rabbit last ate, drank and passed droppings
- which vaccines it has had, and the dates, including RHD2 (bring the record)
- whether any rabbit you keep, or one nearby, has been ill or died recently
- whether it lives indoors, outdoors or both, and whether wild rabbits come into the garden
- other pets in the home, and their flea treatment
- whether you feed fresh grass or plants you have picked, and where your hay comes from
- any recent new rabbits, shows, boarding or other contact with rabbits
