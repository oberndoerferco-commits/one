## First: contact a vet now if you see these signs

Contact your vet **immediately**, and go in, if your cat:

- is **breathing with an open mouth**, panting, or struggling to breathe. Cats normally breathe through their nose, so mouth breathing is a sign of real trouble. The PDSA says never wait to see if things improve
- has **pale gums** or collapses
- **can't stop sneezing**, **sneezes blood** or seems to be **in pain**
- is a **kitten** with sneezing, runny eyes and a runny nose, especially if they are flat or not feeding
- has **stopped eating or drinking**. Cats with cat flu can become dehydrated and may need fluids or tube feeding (see [cat not eating](/animals/cat-not-eating))
- is **squinting** or keeping one eye shut, or the eye looks cloudy. Cat flu can cause ulcers on the surface of the eye, which need a vet

## Is it normal for a cat to sneeze?

The odd sneeze is nothing to worry about, but frequent or severe sneezing, or sneezing with other signs, needs a vet.

Dust, perfume or smoke can make a healthy cat sneeze. If your cat is bright, eating normally and has no discharge from the nose or eyes, you can keep an eye on things. If your cat starts sneezing often, sneezes in fits, or you can't tell why it has started, contact your vet.

If your cat had a vaccine given **into the nose** in the last few days, frequent sneezing for a few days afterwards can be expected. Mention it to your vet if you're unsure.

## What is the most common cause of sneezing in cats?

Cat flu, an infection of the nose and throat caused mostly by feline herpesvirus and feline calicivirus.

What we call "cat flu" is an upper respiratory infection. According to International Cat Care, **over 90%** of cases are caused by **feline herpesvirus** (FHV) and/or **feline calicivirus** (FCV). Bacterial infections often follow and can make the illness worse.

### What are the signs of cat flu?

- frequent **sneezing**
- **discharge** from the nose and eyes
- **red or squinting eyes**
- **fever**, lethargy and a **poor appetite**
- with calicivirus, often **ulcers on the tongue** and a sore throat
- with herpesvirus, more severe eye problems, including **ulcers on the cornea**

In kittens, some strains of calicivirus can cause pneumonia, or a short fever with **limping** and painful joints.

Mild cases usually clear within about a week to ten days; severe cases can go on for several weeks.

### Why has my cat with cat flu stopped eating?

A blocked nose means your cat can hardly smell food, and a sore mouth and throat make eating painful. Cats that won't eat or drink may need **fluids by drip** or a **feeding tube**, and poor nutrition slows recovery. So if your cat stops eating, don't wait for it to pass.

### Can my cat still carry cat flu after recovering?

Yes. **Most recovered cats become carriers** and can infect other cats. With herpesvirus, the virus usually stays for life and can flare up again with **stress**, kittening or some medicines, bringing back the sneezing and runny eyes. With calicivirus, most carriers shed the virus for weeks to months, and most clear it within a few months. A carrier can look healthy and still infect others.

## What else can make a cat sneeze?

Something stuck in the nose, growths, tooth and gum disease, irritants and other respiratory infections.

The PDSA also lists:

- **a foreign body**, such as a blade of grass stuck at the back of the nose or throat
- **growths or tumours** in the nose, which can cause discharge
- **dental disease**, which can affect the inside of the nose
- **irritants** such as perfume, dust and smoke
- other **respiratory infections**

If the sneezing doesn't settle, or doesn't look like ordinary cat flu, your vet can look for these causes with an examination and further tests.

## When should I take my sneezing cat to the vet?

Within a day or two if sneezing with discharge goes on for more than a few days, the discharge thickens or is one-sided, your cat eats less, or the eyes are affected.

Book an appointment within a day or two if:

- sneezing with discharge goes on for more than a few days
- the discharge turns thick, yellow-green or bloody
- discharge comes from only one nostril
- your cat is eating noticeably less
- the eyes are runny, crusty or red
- your cat is drooling or seems sore when eating
- the sneezing keeps coming back

## How can I look after my cat with cat flu at home?

Follow your vet's treatment, clean the nose and eyes often, keep your cat warm and calm, offer warmed strong-smelling food and keep them apart from other cats.

Your vet will decide on treatment, which may include eye drops, pain relief suitable for cats, or antibiotics if bacteria are involved. Antibiotics don't work against the viruses themselves. At home:

- **gently clean discharge** from the nose and eyes often. Your vet may suggest saline drops or a mist to loosen crusts
- keep your cat **warm, clean and calm**
- offer **soft, strong-smelling food, slightly warmed**, to tempt them to eat
- if you have other cats, **keep the sick cat in a separate, easy-to-clean room** with their own bowls and litter tray, and ask your vet which disinfectant is safe
- **reduce stress**, which can trigger flare-ups

## What shouldn't I do if my cat is sneezing?

Don't give human medicines, nose drops or eye drops, and don't wait days if your cat isn't eating or is breathing through the mouth.

- **Don't give human cold, flu or fever medicines.** Many contain paracetamol or ibuprofen. Cats are especially sensitive to paracetamol, which can seriously damage their blood and liver, and they are affected by ibuprofen at lower doses than dogs.
- **Don't use human nose drops or eye drops** unless your vet says so.
- **Don't wait days** if your cat isn't eating or is breathing with an open mouth.

## Can vaccination prevent cat flu?

Vaccination against herpesvirus and calicivirus greatly reduces the risk and usually prevents severe disease, although mild infection can still happen.

Ask your vet which vaccination plan suits your cat, including indoor cats.

## What will the vet want to know?

When the sneezing started, what the discharge looks like, whether your cat is eating and drinking, vaccinations, contact with other cats and previous episodes.

- when it started, and whether discharge comes from one nostril or both
- the colour of the discharge, and any blood
- whether your cat is eating and drinking, and how much
- vaccinations and when they were last given
- outdoor access, a new cat at home, or a recent stay in a cattery or shelter
- any similar episodes before, and any recent stress
