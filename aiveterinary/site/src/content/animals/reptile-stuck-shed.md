## First, the signs that mean go now

Old skin that stays on after a shed can tighten around a toe or the tail like a ring. Go to a vet who treats reptiles now, and call ahead, if:

- a toe or the tip of the tail has turned **dark**, or looks **shrivelled or mushy**
- old skin sits **tightly around a toe or the tail** and the part beyond it looks swollen or discoloured
- your snake's eye looks **swollen**, or there seems to be a swelling under the eye cap
- your reptile is also **weak or unresponsive**

VCA vets explain that in bearded dragons, retained skin can form constricting rings around the toes and tail; within a short period the toe or tail tip becomes discoloured (dark), and the dead tissue and infection can spread further up the tail or toes. They also describe tail rot, in which the end of the tail turns black, looks shrivelled and feels mushy. The MSD Veterinary Manual notes that snakes can develop abscesses below the eye cap, the clear protective covering over the eye, which usually need surgery.

If dry skin is still caught around the toes or eyes after the shed, the RSPCA says to consult your reptile vet for advice.

## What does a normal shed look like?

Snakes and some lizards shed their skin in one piece and other lizards in patches; before a shed, a snake's skin turns dull and its eyes cloudy, and it may hide and refuse food.

- **One piece or patches.** "In snakes and some lizards, the process results in shedding the entire layer of skin as a single piece," says the MSD Veterinary Manual; other lizards shed small sections of skin from time to time. The RSPCA says leopard geckos and bearded dragons shed in large pieces.
- **How often.** There is no fixed rule. The RSPCA says young, growing animals shed more often.
- **Before the shed.** The MSD Veterinary Manual says snakes lose their appetite and their skin becomes mildly translucent and dull, especially over the eye caps, which turn opaque. VCA vets describe the eyes as turning a cloudy, blue-grey colour.
- **Behaviour.** The RSPCA says snakes may hide and use a humid hide around a shed, and that corn snakes may behave defensively if handled then, so leave them in peace.

## Why is my reptile's skin not coming off?

Most often because the air is too dry, but mites, poor nutrition, illness, the wrong temperatures and nothing rough to rub against can all lead to a bad shed.

- The MSD Veterinary Manual lists "Low humidity, skin parasites, nutritional deficiencies, infectious diseases, lack of suitable abrasive surfaces, and decreased thyroid function" as factors that may contribute to an abnormal shed.
- For bearded dragons, VCA vets say low humidity can lead to difficult or abnormal shedding of the skin (dysecdysis).
- For snakes, VCA vets call a bad shed "a symptom of an underlying problem", often in the husbandry, such as the wrong temperatures or humidity and/or incomplete nutrition.
- **Mites.** The RSPCA says a snake that spends long periods in its water bowl could have mites, which can show as small black specks on the snake or around the water bowl.

## Why is stuck skin on the toes and tail dangerous?

Because rings of old skin can cut off the blood supply, so that toes or the tip of the tail die and can be lost.

- "Poor shedding on the feet can cut off the blood supply and lead to the loss of toes," warns the RSPCA for leopard geckos.
- VCA vets say the tips of the toes and the end of the tail are most commonly affected in bearded dragons, and that treatment involves removing the ring of dead, retained skin.
- Check the toes and tail tip after every shed, and see your vet if a ring of skin does not come off.

## Why is a retained eye cap a problem?

Snakes have a clear scale over each eye that should come away with the shed; if it stays on, it can damage the eye, so let a vet deal with it rather than picking at it.

- The MSD Veterinary Manual warns: "Be patient; eyecaps should never be forced off because of the possibility of damaging the new one underneath."
- VCA vets say that in the long term, retained eye caps can cause permanent eye damage and blindness, and advise consulting your vet about how best to treat your snake.
- The RSPCA says retained eye caps "will need removing as soon as possible", and that "A reptile specialist vet can do this safely."
- In leopard geckos, the RSPCA says to consult your reptile vet if you still see dry skin caught around the eyes.

## How can I help my reptile shed well?

Keep the humidity right for the species, offer a humid hide and rough surfaces, try a short soak in shallow, slightly warm water for a small patch of stuck skin, and never pull skin off.

- **Measure the humidity** with a hygrometer. The RSPCA says the humidity at the cool end of a royal python's vivarium "should normally be around 50–60 percent", and for corn snakes around 40–50 percent. Other species need other levels, so check a care sheet for yours.
- **Raise it a little before the shed.** Once the skin and eyes turn opaque, the MSD Veterinary Manual says the humidity should be slightly increased, for example by misting, or by offering a hide box with moist sphagnum moss or a container to soak in.
- **A humid hide.** For leopard geckos, the RSPCA describes a hide with a waterproof bottom containing damp moss, sprayed with clean water every few days to keep it moist.
- **Something rough.** The MSD Veterinary Manual says large, moderately abrasive rocks or other rough surfaces to rub on help to ease a normal shed.
- **A short soak.** For stuck shed, the RSPCA suggests bathing a snake in slightly warm water, and a soak in shallow, lukewarm water for a bearded dragon. The MSD Veterinary Manual describes warm water at 77°F to 85°F (25°C to 29°C). Keep the water shallow and stay with your reptile.
- **Never pull.** "Do not pull off old skin if it seems stuck as it can tear the new skin underneath," says the RSPCA. VCA vets suggest asking your vet about the best way to raise humidity, such as soaking or misting.

## When should I take my reptile to the vet for a shedding problem?

As soon as you can if old skin stays over a snake's eyes or around a lizard's toes, tail tip or eyes, straight away for the signs at the top of this page, and soon if bad sheds keep happening.

Take your reptile to a vet:

- if a snake still has its **eye caps** after the shed: the RSPCA says they need removing as soon as possible
- if **dry skin stays around the toes or eyes** after the shed (RSPCA)
- if bad sheds **keep coming back**: VCA vets call a bad shed a symptom of an underlying problem, and say a snake should be examined by an experienced vet familiar with reptiles if humidity changes do not solve it
- if you see signs of **mites**
- if your reptile is also **not eating or losing weight** (see [my reptile is not eating](/animals/reptile-not-eating))

Not every vet treats reptiles. The MSD Veterinary Manual advises asking about a vet's experience with reptiles when you choose one.

## What will the vet want to know about my reptile's shed?

The species and age, how this and earlier sheds went, the humidity and temperatures, the hides and surfaces in the enclosure, its diet, any mites, and what you have already tried.

- The species and age
- When the shed started, and whether the skin came off in one piece or in patches
- How earlier sheds went
- Your humidity readings and how you measure them, the temperatures, and whether there is a humid hide
- The substrate, and whether there are rough surfaces to rub against
- Its diet and any supplements
- Whether you have seen mites
- What you have already tried
- Photos of the affected toes, tail or eyes

If a toe or the tail tip has turned dark or your snake's eye looks swollen, stop reading and call a vet who treats reptiles now.
