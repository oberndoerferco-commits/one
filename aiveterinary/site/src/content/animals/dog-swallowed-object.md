## First: call your vet now, and go straight in if you see these signs

If your dog has swallowed a sock, a toy, a piece of plastic, a corn cob or anything else that isn't food, **call your vet or an emergency vet now**, even if your dog seems completely normal. The PDSA's advice is to contact your vet immediately and to "never wait to see if a problem develops". A swallowed object can block the gut, and a gut blockage is a serious, painful condition that can kill.

Go straight to the nearest clinic, and ring on the way, if your dog:

- is **vomiting**, especially again and again, or can't stop vomiting
- has a **painful or swollen tummy**, or doesn't want it touched
- is **lethargic**, weak or collapses
- has **stopped eating**
- is **straining** or not passing any poo, or has diarrhoea
- has swallowed something **sharp, spiky or large**, which the PDSA warns can tear the gut
- has a **string, thread or ribbon** hanging from its mouth or bottom (don't pull it)

Have this ready when you call: **what** your dog swallowed (a matching sock, the rest of the toy or the packaging helps), **how big** it was, **when** it happened, and your dog's **weight**.

## Which objects do dogs swallow most often?

Bones, corn cobs, socks, underwear, children's toys, dummies and bouncy balls are among the most common.

That list comes from the PDSA, whose vets see more than 2,700 pets a year that have eaten something harmful. The Cornell Riney Canine Health Center adds rocks and other clothing. The MSD Veterinary Manual names string, yarn, fabric and sewing needles as risky items, and says young, large-breed dogs are more likely to have a blockage from something they have eaten. The PDSA adds that young dogs and dogs that have done it before are more at risk.

For cooked bones, see our article on [chicken bones](/animals/dog-ate-chicken-bones).

## Why can a swallowed object be dangerous for my dog?

It can block the stomach or intestines so that food, fluid and waste can no longer pass, and it can damage or tear the gut wall.

- **Blockage.** Cornell describes gut foreign bodies as emergencies that often need surgery. The MSD Veterinary Manual warns that a dog with a blocked small intestine may be unable to stop vomiting, and that without treatment it can die from shock caused by fluid loss "within a short period of time".
- **Tearing.** The PDSA warns that large, sharp or spiky items can tear the gut. A torn gut can lead to infection inside the tummy (peritonitis), and Cornell adds sepsis, a body-wide infection. Both are life-threatening.
- **String, ribbon and other long, thin things.** Cornell calls these linear foreign bodies "especially dangerous": they can cut holes in the intestines.

## Will the sock or toy pass on its own?

Sometimes a small, smooth object does, but only your vet can judge that, usually with X-rays, and many swallowed objects need to be removed.

The MSD Veterinary Manual says that for small, smooth objects, the vet may take several sets of X-rays over hours to days to see whether the object moves on. Cornell says smaller objects that don't cause a complete blockage may be managed in hospital with fluids and close monitoring, and that surgery is needed if the object doesn't pass. The professional version of the MSD manual says surgery is needed if such an object has not passed within 36 to 48 hours, or sooner if the dog gets worse.

Watching for the sock to come out at home is not the same thing: that monitoring happens with X-rays, in a clinic. The PDSA's advice is to call your vet straight away rather than wait to see whether a problem develops.

## Should I make my dog vomit?

No: don't try to make your dog sick at home. If it is safe for what was swallowed, your vet can do it.

The PDSA puts it simply: "Never try to make your dog sick at home." The ASPCA warns that trying to make a pet vomit at home can be dangerous, and lists salt, gagging your pet or putting things down its throat, and oil among the unsafe methods.

According to the PDSA, if your dog gets to the vet very quickly, the vet may give an injection to make your dog vomit, but only if that is safe for the object your dog swallowed. That decision belongs to your vet.

## What should I do right now?

Take away anything else your dog could swallow, work out what is missing, and call your vet.

1. **Remove the rest** of the object and anything similar, so your dog can't swallow more.
2. **Work out what it was:** what material, roughly how big, how many pieces, and when it happened. If you're not sure, tell your vet what has gone missing. The MSD Veterinary Manual says missing household objects such as toys should be reported.
3. **Don't pull** on any string, thread or ribbon you can see at the mouth or bottom. It may still be caught further inside.
4. **Call your vet** and go in if they tell you to. Bring the packaging, or a matching sock or toy, if you have one.
5. **Don't give any medicines** unless your vet tells you to.

## What will the vet do?

Usually an examination, X-rays or an ultrasound scan, blood tests, and then removal of the object, with a camera through the mouth or by surgery.

- **Tests.** The PDSA says your dog may be admitted for X-rays and a scan. Cornell adds blood tests to check for dehydration and other problems.
- **Endoscopy.** If the object is still in the stomach, Cornell describes removing it under anaesthetic with a flexible camera tube passed through the mouth. The professional version of the MSD manual says endoscopy is mainly useful for objects in the stomach and should not be used when a string-like object is suspected.
- **Surgery.** Cornell says most dogs with a gut foreign body need surgery, either to open the stomach or the intestine. If part of the intestine is badly damaged, that part may be removed and the healthy ends joined.
- **Supportive care.** A fluid drip, pain relief and antibiotics are usual (PDSA, Cornell).

**Recovery.** The PDSA says your dog may need to stay in hospital for a few days. Cornell says the first 3 to 5 days after surgery are the riskiest time for the intestinal stitches to leak, so watch closely for vomiting, pain, low energy or loss of appetite, and call your vet if you see any of them. Your vet will tell you what and how to feed.

**Outlook.** The PDSA says a dog treated quickly, without complications, has a good chance of recovering well, but that complications during and after surgery are quite common. The MSD Veterinary Manual also says dogs tend to do well if the blockage is diagnosed and treated quickly.

## How can I stop my dog swallowing things again?

Keep small, chewable things out of reach, supervise play, and give toys your dog can't break up and swallow.

Cornell's advice is to keep small objects out of reach, avoid toys and bones that are easy to swallow, supervise playtime and train your dog not to scavenge. For dogs that keep doing it, or that have already needed surgery more than once, Cornell suggests asking about basket muzzle training. The PDSA advises never giving bones as treats and choosing safe, indestructible toys instead.

## When should I take my dog to the vet?

As soon as you know or suspect your dog has swallowed something it shouldn't, even if it seems well.

Also call your vet if you didn't see your dog swallow anything but it has signs that could mean a blockage. The PDSA lists:

- vomiting, which is often one of the first signs
- diarrhoea or constipation
- a painful tummy
- low energy
- eating less
- weight loss

If your dog vomits now and then but is otherwise well, our article on [dog vomiting](/animals/dog-vomiting) explains when it can wait. If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know about what my dog swallowed?

What it was, how big, when, whether your dog has vomited or pooed since, and your dog's weight and health.

- what the object was, what it was made of, and how big it was (bring the packaging or a matching item if you can)
- whether anything sharp, or anything string-like, could have been part of it
- how many pieces may be missing
- when it happened, or when you last saw the object
- whether your dog has vomited, what came up, and whether any of the object came out
- when your dog last ate and last passed normal poo
- your dog's weight, age and any health problems
- whether your dog has swallowed things or had gut surgery before
- any medicines your dog takes
