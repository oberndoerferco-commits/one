## First: go to a vet now if you see these signs after a season

Being in season (in heat) is not an illness. But in the weeks afterwards, an unspayed female dog can develop **pyometra**, a womb full of pus. The MSD Veterinary Manual says it usually appears **4 to 6 weeks after a season**, mostly in dogs over five. Go to a vet or [emergency clinic](/emergency) **straight away**, and phone ahead, if during that time your dog:

- is **lethargic** and eating less or nothing
- is **drinking and weeing much more** than usual
- is **vomiting**
- has a **discharge of pus**, sometimes bloody, from her vulva
- has a **swelling belly**, even with no discharge at all
- has a fever or seems very weak

If the cervix is closed, the pus can't drain out. The womb fills up unseen and there is no visible discharge. The MSD Veterinary Manual warns that pyometra can quickly progress to shock and death, so don't wait and see.

Also phone your vet if a season lasts **more than about 40 days**, if tissue bulges out of the vulva during a season, or if your dog has been mated by accident.

## How often does a dog come into season?

Most unspayed females come into season once or twice a year, every 4 to 13 months, about every 7 months on average.

The MSD Veterinary Manual gives an interval between seasons of **4 to 13 months**, with an average of about **7 months**. Every dog has her own rhythm. Note the start and end date of each season: it helps your vet later too.

## What are the stages of a season, and how long do they last?

Pro-oestrus, oestrus, dioestrus and anoestrus; the visible season usually lasts two to three weeks.

The figures below are from the MSD Veterinary Manual.

**Pro-oestrus:** lasts about **9 days** on average, sometimes only 2 days, sometimes up to 3 weeks. The vulva swells and becomes firm, and there is a **bloody discharge**. Male dogs find her very interesting, but she won't yet allow mating.

**Oestrus (the "standing" phase):** also about **9 days** on average, anywhere from 3 days to 3 weeks. The vulva softens, and the discharge often lessens and turns pale and straw-coloured. She is now willing to mate, and ovulation happens in this phase.

**Dioestrus:** in dogs that aren't pregnant, usually about **2 months**. Her hormones behave as if she were pregnant, and this is when both false pregnancy and pyometra can develop.

**Anoestrus:** normally **1 to 6 months**, a resting phase until the next season.

So the visible season, pro-oestrus and oestrus together, usually lasts around two to three weeks, shorter or longer in some dogs.

## What should I watch out for while my dog is in season?

Keep her on a lead outside, avoid unneutered males, and tell your training class, kennels or anyone with a male dog in good time.

- Keep her **on a lead** outdoors and away from unneutered males, even once the bleeding eases. The fertile days often come after it lessens.
- Tell your training class, boarding kennels or housemates with male dogs in good time.
- Season pants can protect your home, but take them off regularly so she can keep herself clean.

If your dog has been mated by accident, talk to your vet. Vets can end a pregnancy, but the MSD Veterinary Manual says around **60%** of accidentally mated dogs don't become pregnant, so pregnancy should be confirmed before any treatment.

## Is a false pregnancy dangerous for my dog?

Usually not: it's common, and the signs usually settle without treatment within 1 to 3 weeks.

After every season without pregnancy, a dog's hormones go through a "pregnancy-like" phase. In some dogs this shows clearly; a UK study found signs commonly appear **six to eight weeks after a season**. Signs can include:

- enlarged mammary glands, sometimes with milk
- weight gain
- nesting, and collecting and "mothering" toys
- restlessness, less appetite or activity, licking her tummy
- guarding objects, sometimes with aggression

The MSD Veterinary Manual says it usually settles **without treatment within 1 to 3 weeks**. The UK study says treatment is warranted if the signs are extreme or **last longer than 4 weeks**; talk to your vet if so.

How you can help:

- **Don't milk her** or massage the glands. The MSD Veterinary Manual says this only stimulates more milk.
- Avoid stroking or brushing her tummy.
- If she keeps licking her mammary glands, your vet may suggest a cone (buster collar).

Be alert if she seems **unwell** during this time, or is vomiting, drinking a lot or has a discharge. Then your vet needs to rule out pyometra.

## How is pyometra treated?

Usually with surgery to remove the womb and ovaries, after an examination with ultrasound and blood tests.

Your vet will examine your dog, usually with an **ultrasound scan** and **blood tests**. In most cases the recommended treatment is an operation to remove the womb and ovaries (a spay). Many dogs are already weak by then and are stabilised first with a fluid drip and antibiotics. Treatment with medicines alone is an option only in selected cases, and your vet will decide what's right.

**Don't give your dog painkillers from your own medicine cabinet**; your vet decides on pain relief. See our guide to [human painkillers and pets](/animals/nsaid-painkillers-dogs-cats). Our [visit brief](/visit-brief) helps you note when her last season started and ended.

## Should I have my dog spayed, and when?

Spaying prevents seasons and pregnancy; ask your vet whether it suits your dog, and plan it for the resting phase, not during a false pregnancy.

Whether spaying is right for your dog is a conversation to have with your vet. Timing matters: the MSD Veterinary Manual and the UK study recommend operating in **anoestrus**, the resting phase, and **not during a false pregnancy** or dioestrus. Spaying during dioestrus can itself trigger a false pregnancy.
