## Go to a vet now if your older, thin cat

Thyroid disease is usually checked calmly, within a week. But go to a vet **straight away**, and call ahead, if an older cat that has been losing weight:

- is breathing **fast or with difficulty** while resting
- suddenly seems **blind**, bumping into things
- suddenly **can't use one or both back legs** and seems in severe pain
- collapses

An untreated overactive thyroid can damage the heart and raise blood pressure. International Cat Care explains that high blood pressure often shows no signs until it causes **sudden blindness**, that heart disease mostly shows as **difficult or rapid breathing**, and that a blood clot from the heart can cause **sudden paralysis of the back legs** with severe pain. See our [emergency page](/emergency).

## How do thyroid problems differ in dogs and cats?

Dogs usually get an underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism), cats an overactive one (hyperthyroidism).

The thyroid gland in the neck makes hormones that set the pace of the body's metabolism.

- In **dogs**, the usual problem is a gland that makes **too little** hormone (**hypothyroidism**). The MSD Veterinary Manual says the two most common causes in adult dogs are inflammation of the gland, which is likely to be immune-mediated, and the gland shrinking for no known reason.
- In **cats**, it's the opposite: a gland that makes **too much** (**hyperthyroidism**). International Cat Care says it is usually caused by a **benign** (non-cancerous) change in one or both glands. Malignant tumours are rare, under 1 to 2% of cases.

An underactive thyroid in cats and an overactive one in dogs do happen, but they are uncommon.

## What are the signs of an underactive thyroid in dogs?

Everything slows down: weight gain without eating more, tiredness, seeking warmth and coat and skin problems.

The MSD Veterinary Manual says it is most common in dogs **4 to 10 years old**, usually in medium to large breeds. Signs include:

- **weight gain without a bigger appetite**
- dullness, tiredness, not wanting to exercise
- seeking warmth
- hair loss, usually **symmetrical on both sides** of the body and not itchy, sometimes on the top of the tail
- skin infections
- in some dogs, a puffy, "tragic" facial expression
- in unneutered females, irregular or absent seasons or infertility

These signs come on slowly and are easy to put down to age.

## What are the signs of an overactive thyroid in cats?

Everything speeds up: weight loss despite a big appetite, drinking more, restlessness and a fast heart rate.

International Cat Care says it mainly affects middle-aged and older cats, and is rare in cats **under 7 years old**. Signs include:

- **weight loss despite a good or increased appetite**
- drinking more
- restlessness
- a fast heart rate
- a poor, unkempt coat
- vomiting or diarrhoea in some cats, and often heat intolerance
- a minority instead become lethargic and weak and eat less

Your vet may be able to feel the enlarged gland in the neck, though not always.

## How are thyroid problems diagnosed?

With a blood test for the thyroid hormone T4; in dogs, more tests are often needed because the result is harder to read.

**Cats.** International Cat Care says the diagnosis is confirmed with a blood test for **T4**, sometimes with **free T4**. Your vet will usually also check liver values, **kidney function** and **blood pressure**, because these can already be affected.

**Dogs.** Reading the result is trickier. The MSD Veterinary Manual explains that other illnesses lower T4 even when the thyroid is healthy, and so do some medicines, including steroids. Greyhounds and some other sighthounds normally have lower T4 than other breeds. So your vet may add other tests, such as TSH, free T4 or thyroid antibodies, and sometimes suggests a treatment trial.

## How are thyroid problems treated?

Dogs take replacement thyroid hormone for life; cats can have tablets or gel, radioactive iodine, surgery or an iodine-restricted diet.

**Dogs.** The missing hormone is replaced with a tablet, usually for life. The MSD Veterinary Manual says energy and alertness usually improve **within 2 weeks**, while skin, coat and weight may take **up to 3 months**. A blood test after **4 to 8 weeks** helps your vet adjust the dose; once stable, checks are usually **once or twice a year**. Food can affect how the tablet is absorbed, so give it the same way every day and follow your vet's instructions. Too much hormone is rare but can cause drinking and urinating more, weight loss, panting, nervousness or a fast heart rate: if you notice these, call your vet.

**Cats.** There are four options. The choice depends on your cat's age, kidneys, other illnesses and what works for your household:

| Option | What it involves |
|---|---|
| Anti-thyroid medicine (tablets, liquid, or a gel rubbed on the skin) | Daily, for life; controls but doesn't cure. Side effects such as poor appetite or vomiting usually settle in the first weeks; rarer problems include severe facial itching |
| Radioactive iodine | Curative in most cats, up to 95%; a short hospital stay at a specialist clinic |
| Surgery | Removes the affected gland; the main risk is damage to the parathyroid glands, which control blood calcium |
| Iodine-restricted diet | Works only if your cat eats nothing else: no other food, treats or prey |

## What monitoring is needed?

In cats, T4, kidneys and blood pressure before and after starting treatment; in dogs, blood tests plus keeping track of weight and coat.

In cats, the MSD Veterinary Manual warns that bringing thyroid levels back to normal **can unmask kidney disease**, even when kidney tests looked normal beforehand. International Cat Care notes that medicine is often used first, even when surgery or radioactive iodine is planned. Kidney function and blood pressure need ongoing checks.

In dogs, as well as blood tests, weigh your dog regularly and note changes in the coat.

## Can a pet with a thyroid problem live well?

Yes: both conditions usually respond well to treatment, though in cats a lot depends on the kidneys and heart.

The MSD Veterinary Manual says treatment failure in hypothyroid dogs is uncommon. International Cat Care says most treated hyperthyroid cats show a complete reversal of signs, but kidney function and blood pressure still need monitoring. That's one more reason not to wait.
