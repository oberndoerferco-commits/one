## Go to a vet today if your rabbit

The PDSA advises contacting your vet **straight away** about any problem with your rabbit's teeth or gums, and never waiting to see if it gets better. Go **today**, out of hours if necessary, and call ahead, if your rabbit:

- is **not eating**, or eating much less than usual, including picking food up and dropping it
- is passing **fewer, smaller or no droppings**
- is **drooling**, or has a wet chin or chest
- has a **swelling** on the jaw, cheek or under the eye
- is sitting **hunched**, grinding its teeth or seems dull
- has front teeth so long it **cannot close its mouth**

A rabbit that won't eat and has no droppings is an **emergency**: the Rabbit Welfare Association says it should see a vet within the shortest possible time, and that waiting until tomorrow will be too late. A rabbit that stops eating is at risk of **gut stasis**, where the gut slows down or stops. The PDSA warns that untreated dental disease can lead to it, and it can be life-threatening. If your rabbit has stopped eating, read [my rabbit is not eating](/animals/rabbit-not-eating) and see our [emergency page](/emergency).

## Why do rabbit teeth keep growing?

All of a rabbit's teeth, front and back, grow throughout its life and are normally worn down by chewing.

The MSD Veterinary Manual explains that the incisors at the front and the premolars and molars at the back all keep growing. Chewing, especially chewing hay, normally wears them down as fast as they grow. When that balance is lost, the teeth become too long.

According to the PDSA, rabbits have **28 teeth**. You can usually see only the two front incisors. The back teeth can't be seen without instruments, and that is often where the problems are.

## Why do a rabbit's teeth become overgrown?

Mostly because of a diet too low in fibre, but also inherited misalignment and injury.

- **Not enough fibre.** The PDSA names a low-fibre diet as the main cause of dental disease. The MSD Veterinary Manual says rabbits fed mainly on **pellets** instead of hay can develop overgrown teeth, because pellets don't wear the teeth down as well.
- **Muesli-style mixes.** PDSA vets don't recommend them: rabbits pick out the sugary pieces and leave the rest.
- **Inherited misalignment (malocclusion).** The MSD Veterinary Manual calls it one of the most common inherited problems in rabbits. The upper and lower teeth don't meet, so they wear unevenly. It can show up as early as **3 to 8 weeks** of age.
- **Injury.** Young rabbits can damage their teeth biting cage wire, and the PDSA notes teeth can break during fights or when gnawing on something hard.

## What happens when the teeth get too long?

Eating and grooming become hard and painful, the back teeth form sharp spurs, and abscesses can develop.

- **Overgrown front teeth** make it hard to eat, drink, groom and close the mouth. The PDSA notes that when the front teeth are overgrown, the back teeth often are too.
- **Overgrown back teeth** form **sharp spurs** that dig into the tongue and cheeks and cause painful sores.
- **Tooth root abscesses** are pockets of pus around a tooth root. The MSD Veterinary Manual says they often affect several teeth and often come back after treatment. The PDSA says many never fully resolve, which is why early treatment matters.

## What are the signs of dental problems in rabbits?

Weight loss, a dirty bottom, soft droppings, weepy eyes, drooling, a smaller appetite, tooth grinding and a lumpy jaw.

The PDSA lists signs that often creep up slowly:

- weight loss
- a **dirty bottom**, because grooming becomes difficult and painful
- diarrhoea or soft droppings
- **weepy eyes**, drooling or a runny nose
- a reduced appetite
- tooth grinding
- a bumpy or painful jaw
- low energy

Rabbits hide pain well, so the PDSA warns that dental disease is often not noticed until it is severe.

## Can I trim my rabbit's teeth myself?

No: trimming at home can crack the root, break the tooth or cut it too short and expose the nerve.

It may look like a quick fix, but **don't do it**, not even with nail clippers. The PDSA explains that home trimming can **crack the tooth root**, causing severe pain and long-term problems, **fracture the tooth**, or cut it **too short and expose the nerve**, which may mean the tooth has to be removed. The MSD Veterinary Manual lists teeth trimmed too short as one cause of **abscesses**. You also can't see the back teeth at home, which are often the real problem.

Don't give your rabbit any medicine your vet hasn't prescribed, including human painkillers or leftover antibiotics from another pet. The MSD Veterinary Manual warns that some antibiotics given by mouth can trigger serious gut disease in rabbits.

## What will the vet do?

Examine the whole mouth, usually under anaesthetic, trim and file the teeth, remove damaged ones if needed and give pain relief.

- **A full mouth check.** The PDSA says a few calm rabbits can be examined awake, but most need an **anaesthetic** for a full examination. The MSD Veterinary Manual adds that x-rays or a CT scan are part of a full dental exam.
- **Trimming and filing** overgrown teeth and spurs under anaesthetic, so your rabbit can eat again.
- **Removing teeth** that are damaged, growing at the wrong angle or linked to abscesses. The PDSA notes this is complex surgery because of the rabbit's small mouth and long roots.
- **Pain relief**, and sometimes antibiotics for abscesses or mouth infections.
- Possibly **syringe feeding** for a short time, and help keeping the chin dry and the bottom clean.

Many rabbits with dental disease need **ongoing check-ups and treatment for life**. The PDSA suggests talking openly with your vet about this, including the cost. Our [visit brief](/visit-brief) can help you note what you've seen before the appointment.

## How do I prevent overgrown teeth?

Unlimited hay or grass, fresh greens, very few pellets, no muesli, weekly tooth checks and regular vet checks.

The best prevention is the right diet. The PDSA recommends, every day:

- **hay or grass**, ideally unlimited, and at least a bundle **as big as your rabbit**
- about **one adult-sized handful** of greens, vegetables and herbs **twice a day**
- just **one tablespoon of nuggets** once a day (twice a day if your rabbit weighs **over 3.5 kg**)
- no muesli-style mixes
- fresh water at all times

If you need to change the diet, the PDSA advises doing it **gradually over 2 to 4 weeks**.

Then keep an eye on things:

- **check the front teeth at least once a week**, as the PDSA suggests, and watch that your rabbit is eating, passing normal droppings and not losing weight
- have your vet check the teeth **at least once a year**, ideally a few times a year

A rabbit that spends its day chewing hay is looking after its own teeth. If anything seems off, call your vet: with dental problems, the earlier the better.
