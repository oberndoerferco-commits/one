## First: go to a vet now if your cat is hot and has any of these signs

Call your vet or an emergency clinic and go straight in if your cat feels hot and:

- has **collapsed**, is very weak or is hard to rouse
- is **panting** heavily, drooling or vomiting, especially after being in a hot place: this may be **heatstroke**. International Cat Care says to contact your vet immediately if you suspect it; Cornell's advice is to move your cat somewhere cool, gently wet its fur with cool (not very cold) water, and call your vet right away
- has **muscle tremors** or twitching: the PDSA lists poisons such as insecticides and slug pellets as causes of tremors and a high temperature
- has been given **any human painkiller or fever medicine**, even one tablet (see below)

If your cat is hot, quiet and off its food but none of the above apply, **call your vet today**. The PDSA's advice is simply: "Contact your vet if you think your cat has a high temperature."

## Do warm ears mean my cat has a fever?

Not on their own. Warm ears can be a sign, but only a thermometer tells you whether your cat's temperature is high.

The PDSA says a cat with a high temperature may feel hot to the touch, "especially their feet, ears or face", but it lists this together with changes in behaviour: hiding away, not wanting to eat, shivering, breathing faster than usual, low energy and not seeming themselves. So look at the whole cat, not just its ears: a cat that is hiding, shivering or won't eat needs a vet, whatever its ears feel like.

## What is a normal temperature for a cat?

About 38 to 39 °C, measured in the rectum. Sources give slightly different ranges.

- The **MSD Veterinary Manual** gives a normal rectal temperature for cats of **38.1–39.2 °C** (100.5–102.5 °F).
- The **PDSA** gives **38–39 °C**.

The Merck Veterinary Manual says most true fevers fall between about **39.5 and 41.1 °C**. Temperatures of 41.1 °C or more can occur with heatstroke or seizures and are dangerous.

## Should I take my cat's temperature at home?

Only if your vet asks you to and your cat tolerates it. Never force it.

The Schwarzman Animal Medical Center calls a rectal temperature the "gold standard", and warns that ear thermometers made for people don't always match rectal readings, and that measuring under the armpit does not give repeatable results. It also notes that taking a rectal temperature is stressful for a cat. If your cat struggles, stop: a vet can measure it safely, and a missing reading should not delay a call to your vet.

## Is it a fever or heatstroke?

They are different. In a fever the body raises its own temperature on purpose; in heatstroke the cat overheats from outside.

The Merck Veterinary Manual explains that in a true fever the brain's "thermostat" is reset to a higher setting. Heatstroke and seizures also raise body temperature, but the thermostat is not reset, so they are not true fevers and can push the temperature to dangerous levels. That difference matters for what you do: Merck warns that in a true fever, cooling methods such as water baths "work against the body's own regulatory mechanisms". So don't try to cool a feverish cat yourself. Only start cooling if heatstroke is likely, as described above, and call your vet on the way. See also our article on [heatstroke](/animals/heatstroke).

## Can I give my cat paracetamol, aspirin or ibuprofen?

No. Never give a cat any human painkiller or fever medicine.

The Merck Veterinary Manual says cats are "especially susceptible" to paracetamol (acetaminophen) because they can't break it down safely. Toxic by-products build up and damage the red blood cells and liver: within a few hours the gums can turn brown or muddy, and the face and paws may swell. Merck says cats are also "very susceptible" to aspirin and especially susceptible to ibuprofen. If your cat has swallowed any of these, call your vet or an emergency clinic now. Our article on [painkillers for dogs and cats](/animals/nsaid-painkillers-dogs-cats) explains more.

## Why might my cat have a fever?

Usually an infection, but there are many possible causes, some minor and some serious.

The PDSA lists:

- infections and abscesses, especially **cat bite abscesses**
- pain or inflammation, for example from a urine infection
- poisons and toxins
- heatstroke
- stress
- reactions to medicines
- obesity, because overweight cats lose heat less easily

International Cat Care lists a high temperature among the signs of **cat flu**, together with sneezing, eye and nose discharge, lethargy and loss of appetite; see [cat sneezing](/animals/cat-sneezing). The Merck Veterinary Manual says infections are the most common cause of fever in cats, and also names inflammation, immune diseases and cancer.

## What can I do until my cat sees the vet?

Keep your cat quiet and comfortable, offer water and food, and don't give any medicines.

- Let your cat rest somewhere quiet, with water nearby.
- Offer food. International Cat Care suggests soft, strong-smelling food, gently warmed, to tempt a cat with cat flu that won't eat.
- Note when it last ate, drank, peed and pooed.
- Don't give any human or leftover pet medicines, and don't bath or cool your cat unless you suspect heatstroke.

## What will the vet do?

Examine your cat, take its temperature, look for the cause and treat it.

The PDSA says treatment "depends entirely on the cause". If the cause isn't obvious, the Merck Veterinary Manual describes blood tests (including for FeLV and FIV), urine tests and often X-rays, and sometimes ultrasound or further tests. Cats with fever can become dehydrated and stop eating, so your vet may give fluids or fever-reducing medicine made for cats. The PDSA adds that it is "very common to never find the cause but the high temperature to get better in a short space of time".

## When should I take my cat to the vet for a fever?

Today if your cat feels hot and is quiet, hiding or not eating; immediately if it has any of the go-now signs above.

Also call your vet if your cat seemed better but the temperature or tiredness keeps coming back, or if it has stopped eating; see [my cat is not eating](/animals/cat-not-eating). If you need an out-of-hours clinic, see [emergency help](/emergency).

## What will the vet want to know about my cat's temperature?

What you noticed and when, any reading you took, what your cat is eating and drinking, and anything it could have been exposed to.

- when you first noticed your cat felt hot or seemed unwell
- any temperature reading, how and when you took it
- whether your cat is eating, drinking, peeing and pooing normally
- any vomiting, diarrhoea, sneezing, eye or nose discharge, or fast breathing
- any wounds, lumps, limping or fights with other cats
- whether your cat goes outdoors, and whether it could have been shut in a hot space
- any possible contact with poisons, such as slug pellets or insecticides
- any medicines, including human medicines, your cat has had
- vaccination and parasite treatment history
- your cat's age, and any health problems
