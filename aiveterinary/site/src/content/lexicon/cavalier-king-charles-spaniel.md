## How long do Cavaliers live?

About 10 to 12 years, depending on the study. A 2024 UK study of 13,782 Cavalier King Charles Spaniels found a median lifespan of 11.8 years, slightly shorter than the 12.0 years of crossbred dogs (McMillan et al.). VetCompass life tables, built from UK vet records, put a Cavalier's life expectancy at birth at 10.45 years (Teng et al.). The Royal Kennel Club gives the breed's lifespan as over 12 years.

## How big does a Cavalier get?

Small. The FCI breed standard gives a weight of 5.4 to 8 kg and prefers a small, well-balanced dog within that range. It asks for a muzzle of about 3.8 cm, a shallow stop and eyes that are large but not prominent; the Royal Kennel Club explains that the Cavalier was bred back from the flatter-faced King Charles Spaniel to the "old type" with a longer muzzle and flatter skull.

## Why do Cavaliers get heart disease?

Mitral valve disease is the breed's best-known problem. One of the heart's valves slowly wears and stops closing properly, so with every beat some blood flows backwards. Over time the heart enlarges and fluid builds up in the lungs (UFAW).

How common is it? The UFAW genetic welfare database says mitral valve disease is about 20 times more common in Cavaliers than in other breeds. Murmurs increase by about 10% a year, so that half of Cavaliers have one by the age of five and nearly all by ten. In UK vet records, Cavaliers had over 40 times the odds of a diagnosis compared with crossbred dogs, and made up a third (32.4%) of all dogs diagnosed (Mattin et al.). There is no genetic test yet.

It does not mean every Cavalier is ill early. The Royal Kennel Club says the disease starts earlier in Cavaliers than in other breeds, and that many dogs diagnosed with it continue to live to a good age. Dogs with a murmur but no heart failure usually seem normal at home. Most develop heart failure within several years of the disease starting (UFAW).

Signs of heart failure listed by UFAW include coughing, panting heavily and struggling for breath on exertion, reluctance to sit or lie down, weight loss and fainting during exercise. A dog that is struggling to breathe at rest needs a vet straight away. Ask your vet to listen to your Cavalier's heart at every check.

## What is syringomyelia?

Fluid-filled cavities (syrinxes) form in the spinal cord, because the skull is too small for the brain or misshapen (UFAW). The Royal Kennel Club says some owners call it "neck scratcher's disease": the dog scratches at the air near its neck, often when excited or on a lead.

The signs vary. Some dogs show none. Others have severe, lasting pain around the head, neck and front legs, yelp after sudden changes of posture (when jumping down or being picked up), are sensitive to touch around the neck, and may become weak or wobbly (UFAW). Medicines can help some dogs, but not all.

It is common on scans. UFAW cites an MRI survey of 555 Cavaliers whose owners saw no signs: 25% of one-year-olds and 70% of dogs aged six or more had syringomyelia. A UK and Dutch screening programme estimated the lifetime risk at 55%. Not all of these dogs have pain: one US survey found clinical signs in 3.4% of Cavaliers.

Diagnosis is by MRI. There is no genetic test. UFAW advises breeding only from scanned dogs that are clear, with repeat scans at 2–3 and at 6 years, because some dogs clear at 12 months become affected later. The Royal Kennel Club is running a screening project for the breed with the British Veterinary Association.

## What other health problems do Cavaliers have?

The PDSA also lists:

- **Curly coat/dry eye syndrome** (CC/DE), which affects skin, coat, nails and eyes and causes severe dry eye; there is a DNA test
- **Episodic falling** (EF), episodes of muscle stiffening that leave the body rigid, often during exercise, excitement or stress; there is a DNA test
- **inherited eye problems** such as cataracts, now much less common because breeders test (Royal Kennel Club)
- **slipping kneecaps** (luxating patellas), **ear problems** and **dental disease**

If your dog limps or skips on a back leg, see [limping in dogs](/animals/dog-limping).

## Which health tests matter for a Cavalier puppy?

The Royal Kennel Club lists as good practice before breeding: heart grading under the Kennel Club heart scheme for Cavaliers, and DNA tests for curly coat/dry eye and episodic falling. As best practice it adds eye testing under the BVA/KC/ISDS Eye Scheme. For syringomyelia, the PDSA points to the BVA/KC scheme for Chiari malformation and syringomyelia. Ask to see the parents' heart grades and MRI results, and look them up in the Royal Kennel Club's Health Test Results Finder.

## Is a Cavalier right for me?

The FCI standard describes a sporting, affectionate and absolutely fearless dog, friendly and non-aggressive, with no tendency towards nervousness. That only tells you what breeders aim for: every dog is an individual. The Royal Kennel Club gives up to 1 hour of exercise a day and grooming more than once a week.

Think about the breed's health first: heart disease is very likely as the dog ages, and syringomyelia can cause lasting pain. Plan for regular heart checks, and ask for the parents' heart and MRI results.
