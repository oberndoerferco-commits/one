## Which rabbits are lops?

Any rabbit bred to have ears that hang down instead of standing up: the Mini Lop, Dwarf Lop, Holland Lop and French Lop, among others. The research on lops usually groups them by ear carriage rather than by breed, so this page covers them together. Lops are the most common recognised rabbits in UK vet records: 26.4% of rabbits seen in 2013 were recorded as lops (O'Neill et al., 2020), and in 2019, 57.05% of rabbits with usable breed information were lop-eared (O'Neill et al., 2024).

## How long do lop rabbits live, and how big do they get?

Jackson et al. (2025), summarising a large UK study of vet records, report that lop-eared rabbits had shorter lifespans than erect-eared rabbits, but that their causes of death did not differ. Size depends on the breed: in 2013 records, lops overall had a median adult weight of 2.4 kg, Miniature Lops 1.8 kg and Dwarf Lops 1.9 kg (O'Neill et al., 2020).

## Why do lop ears cause problems?

A rabbit's ear canal is a tube of cartilage rings. The Rabbit Welfare Association & Fund (RWAF) compares a hanging ear with a cardboard kitchen roll folded in half: the tube closes and the sections separate. The canal narrows, less air gets in and wax cannot drain. Wax can then push out between the cartilage rings under the skin and form a mass at the base of the ear, which may burst, spread around the head and become impossible to remove.

Several sources point the same way:

- The RSPCA cites a study in which about 25% of lop-eared rabbits had ear conditions, against 10% of erect-eared rabbits.
- In a study of 30 rabbits at a rescue centre, the 15 lops had narrowed ear canals, more wax and redness, and reacted with signs of pain more often when their ears were examined (Johnson and Burn, 2019). The study was small, and the authors raise the question of pain and possible deafness.
- The German vets' animal welfare association TVT names lop rabbits among the breeding forms with welfare problems, because of a high tendency to ear inflammation.

Head tilt or head shaking can be a sign of ear disease; see [head tilt in rabbits](/animals/rabbit-head-tilt).

## Do lops have more teeth problems?

It is not clear. The small rescue study found more front-tooth problems, overgrown and sharp back teeth and more dental treatment in lops (Johnson and Burn, 2019). Two larger studies did not: in UK vet records of 161,979 rabbits, lop ears were not linked with higher odds of dental disease (Jackson et al., 2024), and in 435 pedigree show rabbits examined by vets, lop ears were not a risk factor for any dental abnormality (Jackson et al., 2025). The authors of the larger studies say diet, housing and regular dental checks matter more than ear shape. All rabbits need a diet that is mostly hay; see [overgrown teeth in rabbits](/animals/rabbit-overgrown-teeth).

## What else is more common in lops?

**Eyes and tear ducts.** Lops in the show-rabbit study had about 4 times the odds of eye discharge (Jackson et al., 2025). In 2019 vet records, lop-eared rabbits had 2.0 times the risk of tear duct disease of erect-eared rabbits (O'Neill et al., 2024). The RSPCA says tear duct problems are commonly reported in lops. Entropion, where the eyelids roll in, has been suggested to be common in French Lops (O'Neill et al., 2020).

**Dirty bottom.** Lops had 2.4 times the risk of a dirty bottom (O'Neill et al., 2024). A soiled bottom attracts flies, so check it often: see [flystrike in rabbits](/animals/rabbit-flystrike).

**Short face.** The RWAF counts all lop breeds as brachycephalic (short-faced), with the same risks for teeth and tear ducts as other short-faced rabbits.

## What should I check?

The RWAF advises feeling around the base of the ears regularly for lumps, because ear-base abscesses are very painful and easy to miss under the fur. See a rabbit-savvy vet straight away if you find one. Also watch for:

- head tilt or head shaking, which can come from ear infections
- weepy eyes
- drool, or avoiding hay and hard food
- a dirty, matted bottom

## Is a lop rabbit right for me?

The RSPCA encourages adopting rather than buying and does not recommend rabbits with extreme features; it notes that crossbreeds can also suffer from problems caused by lop ears. The RWAF says many lop-eared rabbits are waiting in rescue centres and that adopting one is a good idea, as long as you know what to look for and have a rabbit-savvy vet. Their basic needs are the same as for any rabbit: see our [rabbit fact sheet](/animals/rabbit).
