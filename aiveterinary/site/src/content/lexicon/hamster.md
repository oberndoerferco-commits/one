## How long do hamsters live?

Pet hamsters usually live from 18 months to 3 years, according to the MSD Veterinary Manual. The PDSA gives about two to three years and notes that larger species such as Syrians tend to live longer than smaller ones such as Russian dwarfs.

MSD's table lists Syrian hamsters at 18–36 months, Russian dwarfs at 18–24 months and Roborovski dwarfs at 36–48 months. The German veterinary animal-welfare association TVT gives 2–3 years for Syrians and 1.5–3 years for dwarf hamsters.

## Syrian or dwarf hamster: what is the difference?

Syrian (golden) hamsters are the larger species and must live alone; "dwarf hamster" covers several smaller species of the genus *Phodopus*. The TVT names the Djungarian, Campbell's (and their hybrids) and Roborovski hamsters as the dwarfs most often kept as pets.

## Can hamsters live together?

Syrian hamsters cannot: the RSPCA says Syrian and Chinese hamsters aren't naturally sociable and should always live on their own. The TVT adds that Syrians tolerate no other hamster in their territory except for mating.

Dwarf hamsters are less clear-cut. The RSPCA says they can sometimes live in pairs or trios if the group was formed early in life and stays stable, but that even dwarf hamsters don't need other hamsters to be happy. The PDSA says domesticated Russian dwarf hamsters should not be housed together because fights often occur, and the TVT strongly advises keeping all dwarf hamsters alone, since they are solitary in the wild. Fights between cage-mates usually happen at night, when hamsters are active (RSPCA).

## Are hamsters nocturnal?

Yes, mostly. Hamsters tend to sleep during the day and are active in the evening and at night (PDSA). The TVT describes pet Syrian hamsters as nocturnal; dwarf hamsters are mainly active at dusk and at night, though some are also awake by day.

Let a sleeping hamster sleep, and put the enclosure somewhere quiet. The RSPCA advises keeping it away from things that give off ultrasound, such as televisions, computer screens and vacuum cleaners.

## What do hamsters eat?

Hamsters eat both plant food and some animal protein (TVT). The PDSA recommends hamster pellets, which contain the nutrients a hamster needs, plus timothy hay for fibre, with fresh fruit, vegetables and herbs only in small amounts.

The TVT recommends a seed and grain mix made for the species, constant access to hay and fresh water, a little fresh food every day, natural branches to gnaw, and a small portion of animal protein once a week. It says a vegan diet is not appropriate for hamsters. Sweet treats such as yoghurt drops often contain sugar, molasses or honey and should be avoided. Dwarf hamsters digest carbohydrates less well than Syrians and are genetically prone to diabetes, so the TVT advises not feeding them fruit at all.

Hoarding food is normal: the TVT says hamsters that cannot build a store become stressed, so check the hoard daily for spoiled food but don't empty it completely.

## How big should a hamster cage be?

At least 100 × 50 × 50 cm (length × width × height), and bigger is better. This is the TVT minimum for both Syrian and dwarf hamsters, and the PDSA quotes the same 100 × 50 cm floor as a minimum. The TVT notes that studies show hamsters do best from about one square metre of floor.

- **Deep bedding:** at least 25 cm, according to the PDSA and the TVT sheet for Syrians, which prefers 40 cm so hamsters can dig tunnels.
- **Hiding places:** at least three (for food, sleeping and toilet), with entrances wide enough for full cheek pouches (TVT). The RSPCA says a hamster always needs a suitable place to hide.
- **Wheel:** solid running surface and back wall; at least 30 cm across for Syrians, at least 25 cm for dwarfs (TVT). The PDSA gives 20 cm as the minimum for dwarfs; the larger TVT figure is the safer choice.
- **Sand bath:** at least 20 × 20 cm of fine, round-grained sand such as chinchilla sand (TVT).
- **Avoid:** wire cages, which the TVT says are not injury-proof and can't hold deep bedding; hamster balls and harnesses, which it calls contrary to animal welfare.

## What temperature do hamsters need?

The TVT gives 18–24 °C as the comfortable range for Syrian and dwarf hamsters, and the PDSA advises 18–21 °C, away from draughts and direct sunlight.

Higher temperatures risk heatstroke (TVT). Cold, darkness or a lack of food can make a hamster fall into torpor: it goes stiff and does not react, and owners often think it has died. The TVT says hamsters should not be kept in unheated rooms in winter or left alone for several days. The RSPCA says hamsters should be kept indoors because the weather is too cold for them to survive outside.

## How do I handle a hamster?

Gently, and on the hamster's terms. The TVT says hamsters are not cuddly pets: touch them only when they seek contact, and don't catch them with your hand, because that frightens them and they may bite.

Instead, guide the hamster into a cardboard tube or a plastic or glass container. Tame hamsters can be allowed to walk onto your hand and then be lifted. Hamsters have no sense of height and fall easily, from a table or a hand (TVT), so make sure they can't drop.

## How do I know my hamster is healthy?

Look at your hamster every day while it is active, and do a weekly check of the front teeth, claws, all body openings, the coat and its weight (TVT). Weighing is easiest with the hamster in a container on a kitchen scale.

The front teeth grow continuously (PDSA), by up to 3.5 mm a week according to the TVT, and are worn down by gnawing. Small weight changes are normal because of full or empty cheek pouches, but larger changes with empty pouches need a vet (TVT). A pouch that stays full on one side can be blocked.

## When should a hamster see a vet?

Without delay if your hamster has diarrhoea or a wet tail, is drooling, keeps its eyes closed, has a scruffy dull coat, changed sleeping times or does not want to move. The TVT says such animals should go to a vet experienced with rodents without delay, and that diarrhoea can quickly be fatal if untreated.

The TVT also lists signs of pain: squeezed-shut eyes, ears and whiskers held flat, a stiff, high-legged walk or a hunched back. One of these signs is enough to have the cause checked by a vet. Skin and coat problems are mostly caused by mites or skin fungi (TVT).

## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

**EU:** there are no harmonised EU travel rules for hamsters. The European Commission's pet travel FAQ says the EU pet passport is only issued for dogs, cats and ferrets. Since 22 April 2026 the EU rules for travelling with pets come from Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/131; its detailed rules are for dogs, cats and ferrets and for pet birds entering from outside the EU, and it does not mention rodents. The Animal Health Law, Regulation (EU) 2016/429, lists "rodents and rabbits other than those intended for food production" in Part B of its Annex I, and for Part B species the Commission says there are no harmonised EU rules: check with the EU country you are travelling to for its national rules. The previous rules, Regulation (EU) No 576/2013, applied until 21 April 2026.

**Germany:** the TVT figures above are recommendations by a veterinary association, not a statutory minimum. The TVT considers some breeding forms, such as hairless hamsters, Rex hamsters and coats too long to groom, to be torture breeding (*Qualzucht*) under § 11b of the German Animal Welfare Act.

**Italy:** the sources we opened set no Italian minimum cage size for hamsters; your vet can tell you about local rules.
