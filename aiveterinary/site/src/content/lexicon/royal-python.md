## How long do royal pythons live?

On average 15 to 20 years in captivity, according to the MSD Veterinary Manual, and some live longer. The RSPCA says royal pythons can live longer than 20 years, and the German veterinary animal-welfare association TVT gives an average of 20 years, with a reported maximum of more than 47.

A royal python (also called a ball python) is a commitment of decades.

## How big do ball pythons get?

Around 130 to 150 cm, depending on the source. The RSPCA says around 150 cm, with differences between males and females; the TVT gives an average of 130 cm, with males usually smaller and lighter, and females weighing on average 1,500 to 2,500 g at 120 to 130 cm.

## Why won't my ball python eat?

Often stress, a new home, the season or an upcoming shed; weight loss is what tells you it is a problem. The RSPCA says royal pythons have a reputation for going off their food.

- **New home.** The RSPCA advises giving your python time to get used to its new environment.
- **Normal fasting.** The TVT says refusing food is common in royal pythons, and that no action is needed when the cause is physiological, such as a seasonal fasting period or pregnancy. The RSPCA links seasonal fasting to brumation. Royal pythons usually don't eat just before and during a shed (TVT).
- **Weigh your snake.** The TVT advises checking the weight regularly: weight loss is a sign that the fasting is not normal.
- **Stress is the usual cause.** The TVT lists hatchlings, wild-caught or farmed snakes that have not settled, transport, moving the snake, vibration, too much handling, too few hiding places, night temperatures that are too low, a vivarium that is too bright, and sharing with other snakes. Unsuitable prey (smelling of urine, or not warm enough) is possible too.
- **Illness.** The RSPCA says royal pythons are prone to respiratory infections and can get mouth rot, and that weight loss or diarrhoea can be signs of internal parasites.
- **What not to do.** The TVT says force-feeding is rarely sensible and hardly ever works. If you can't find and fix a husbandry problem, see a reptile vet. The RSPCA also says to consult a reptile vet if feeding suddenly stops, and to avoid buying a snake that has not been feeding well.

More on this in [My reptile is not eating](/animals/reptile-not-eating).

## What do royal pythons eat?

Rodents, ideally frozen and then thawed: that is what the RSPCA recommends, and the TVT also prefers frozen food. Offer prey slightly wider than the widest part of the snake's body, with tongs, inside the vivarium (RSPCA).

- **How often.** The RSPCA feeds young snakes every five or six days and adults every 7 to 14 days, depending on the snake's weight.
- **When.** The TVT advises offering food in the evening or at night, when the snake is active.
- **Picky eaters.** The TVT says many royal pythons are imprinted on a particular rodent or prey size, and that gerbils or multimammate mice are often preferred because, unlike European mice, they don't smell strongly of urine.
- **Live prey.** If live prey is ever used, the TVT says it must not stay in the vivarium longer than a few minutes.
- **Obesity.** The TVT warns that unsuitable feeding of half-grown and adult snakes can lead to fatty (liver) disease.

## What vivarium and temperature does a royal python need?

A secure, well-ventilated vivarium with a basking zone at 30–32 °C and a cool end at 24–26 °C, with non-light-emitting heat such as a ceramic heater at night (RSPCA). The TVT gives a range of 26–32 °C with a local warm spot of 35 °C, and recommends lowering the temperature by about 3 °C at night.

- **Size.** The RSPCA asks for at least a third of the snake's length for the width and the height: a 120 cm snake needs at least 120 × 40 × 40 cm. The TVT quotes the German agriculture ministry's expert report, for up to two snakes: length of the longest snake × half its length × three-quarters of its length (L × W × H).
- **Escape-proof.** The RSPCA says the vivarium must be secure to prevent escape.
- **Tight, dark hides.** The TVT says royal pythons need several dark, narrow hides in different temperature zones that let them touch the walls on all sides.
- **Humidity.** About 50–60 % at the cool end, misted to around 80 % and allowed to drop in between (RSPCA). The TVT gives 60 %, and around 80 % in the hides.
- **Light.** The RSPCA recommends a low-output reptile UVB tube and a timer for 12 hours of light a day.
- **Burns.** Keep lamps and bulbs out of the snake's reach (TVT), and put a guard around the heat lamp so the snake cannot get burnt (RSPCA).

## Can two ball pythons live together?

No: royal pythons are naturally solitary and should be housed by themselves (RSPCA). The TVT says they should preferably be kept alone and lists sharing a vivarium with other royal pythons among the causes of stress that stop them eating.

## How do I handle a royal python?

Support its whole body and never grab it; handle for about 10–15 minutes at a time (RSPCA). Wait 48 hours after feeding, and avoid handling it during a shed or straight after you have handled prey.

Hatchlings are more likely to strike and bite (RSPCA). The TVT describes royal pythons as very sensitive animals that show discomfort by refusing food or leaving their hide in daylight. Like all reptiles, they can carry salmonella: the TVT advises normal hygiene and extra care around small children, pregnant women and people with weak immune systems.

## How do I know my royal python is healthy?

A healthy royal python is active and inquisitive, with clear, bright eyes, and it sheds its skin in one piece (RSPCA). Weigh it regularly, because weight loss is often the first sign of a problem (TVT).

- **Weight loss or diarrhoea** may mean internal parasites (RSPCA).
- **Whistling breath or gasping** can mean a respiratory infection (RSPCA). The TVT says royal pythons often suffer from diseases of the airways and mouth, and from fungal disease and parasites.
- **Skin.** The MSD Veterinary Manual describes a classic necrotic dermatitis (scale rot) in ball pythons that can develop even when they are kept in ideal conditions.
- **Stuck shed.** Retained eye caps need a reptile vet (RSPCA), and the MSD Veterinary Manual says eye caps should never be forced off. See [Stuck shed in reptiles](/animals/reptile-stuck-shed).
- **Inclusion body disease.** According to the MSD Veterinary Manual, boas and several python species are most often affected; signs include stargazing, regurgitation and loss of weight. There is no treatment and it is always fatal, and infected snakes or their offspring must not be sold.

## When should a royal python see a vet?

When it loses weight, stops eating and you can't find a husbandry cause, or if you have any concern about its health: the RSPCA says to see your reptile vet urgently. The MSD Veterinary Manual advises a prompt first examination by a vet after you get a reptile, and a health check every year.

All treatment should be done by a reptile vet or under a reptile vet's guidance (TVT). The MSD Veterinary Manual advises asking about experience with reptiles when you choose a vet.

## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

**EU:** the royal python is listed in CITES Appendix II and in Annex B of the EU wildlife trade rules (Species+, the official CITES species database). Buy only captive-bred snakes with paperwork: the MSD Veterinary Manual says captive-bred reptiles make the best pets and are less likely to bite than wild-caught animals. Never release a pet reptile into the wild (MSD Veterinary Manual).

**Germany:** owners of specially protected animals must be able to prove their legal origin and legal possession to the authority (§ 46 BNatSchG). For Annex B species, the Traunstein district office says proof of origin is enough, for example a purchase contract with a breeder's confirmation; make sure you get the papers from the seller. Berlin's Pankow district office lists documents such as an official breeding confirmation. Protected vertebrates generally have to be registered with the local nature-conservation authority; some species that breed easily in captivity are exempt (Annex 5 of the Federal Species Protection Ordinance, BArtSchV), so ask your authority whether this applies to your snake. Dangerous-animal rules are set locally: in 2014 Saxony's higher administrative court upheld a decision that, under Leipzig's police ordinance, keeping royal pythons needs a permit. Releasing animals into the wild needs permission (§ 40 BNatSchG).

**Italy:** according to ISPRA, the ban on releasing non-native species into the wild is confirmed by DPR 102/2019 (article 12, paragraph 3 of DPR 357/97). The sources we opened don't describe any further Italian registration rules for royal pythons, so ask the competent authorities before you buy.
