## How long do corn snakes live?

Around 10 to 15 years: that is the figure from both the RSPCA and the MSD Veterinary Manual. A corn snake is a commitment of well over a decade.

The German veterinary animal-welfare association TVT gives a longer upper figure: with good care, up to 20 years in a vivarium.

## How big do corn snakes get?

Most grow to around 150 cm, and males are normally smaller than females (RSPCA). The TVT says corn snakes can reach up to 180 cm, but most stay under 150 cm.

## Can corn snakes live together?

No. Keep a corn snake on its own: the RSPCA says they lead a solitary life in the wild. The TVT adds that even if corn snakes get on with other snake species, they should not share a vivarium, and that snakes should be fed separately because squabbles over food can lead to bites or even cannibalism.

## What do corn snakes eat?

Whole dead mice. The RSPCA recommends prey slightly wider than the snake's widest part, offered with tongs inside the vivarium; corn snakes don't need supplements as long as the prey is of good quality.

- **Dead, not live.** The TVT says switching corn snakes to dead prey usually works without problems.
- **How often.** The sources differ. The RSPCA starts hatchlings on a "pinky" every five to six days and moves up to one adult mouse every 7 to 14 days. The TVT says that, except for very young snakes, corn snakes should be fed no more than once a month. Weigh your snake regularly so it doesn't become underweight or obese (RSPCA), and ask your reptile vet what suits your snake.
- **Water.** Clean, fresh water at the cool end, changed at least daily, in a bowl big enough to bathe in (RSPCA).

## What vivarium and temperature does a corn snake need?

A secure, escape-proof vivarium with a warm basking end at 28–30 °C and a cool end at 20–24 °C, dropping to 16–20 °C at night (RSPCA). Every heat source needs a thermostat, and the RSPCA says to record the temperatures daily because thermostats aren't accurate.

- **Size.** The RSPCA asks for at least a third of the snake's length for the width and the height: a 150 cm snake needs a vivarium at least 150 cm long, 50 cm wide and 50 cm deep. Hatchlings start in a smaller vivarium.
- **Escape-proof.** The vivarium must be secure to prevent escape and free from hazards that might cause injury (RSPCA).
- **Hides and humidity.** At least two hides, one at each end, humidity around 40–50 % and a "humid hide" with moistened moss (RSPCA).
- **Burn risk.** Guard heat lamps (RSPCA); the TVT says lamps must be out of the snake's reach because of the risk of burns.
- **Light.** The RSPCA recommends a low-output reptile UVB tube over the hot end, on a timer for 12 hours a day.

## Why is my corn snake not eating?

Often because it is about to shed or it is the cooler time of year; but weight loss is the warning sign. The RSPCA says a corn snake in brumation (a natural energy-saving phase in the cooler months) may eat less but should not lose weight.

The TVT recommends giving corn snakes a winter rest, but only healthy snakes, after a reptile vet has examined a fresh droppings sample for parasites. It also says: if a corn snake refuses food for several weeks and loses weight outside the mating season and the winter rest, never force-feed it; take it to a vet to find the cause. This applies to young snakes too. Read more in [My reptile is not eating](/animals/reptile-not-eating).

## What happens when a corn snake sheds?

A healthy snake sheds its skin in one complete piece, more often when young (RSPCA). Before a shed, the MSD Veterinary Manual says snakes lose their appetite, the skin turns dull and the eye caps go cloudy; they can also be more irritable.

If the shed comes off in pieces, the RSPCA suggests bathing the snake in slightly warm water and not pulling the skin off. Retained eye caps need a reptile vet as soon as possible (RSPCA). See [Stuck shed in reptiles](/animals/reptile-stuck-shed).

## How do I handle a corn snake?

Gently and with the whole body supported, one hand near the head and the other near the tail, for about 10–15 minutes at a time (RSPCA). Never grab a snake.

The RSPCA also advises waiting 48 hours after feeding, not handling around shedding, letting a new snake settle for about a week, and leaving a snake alone if it pulls its head back into an "S" shape, a defensive posture.

Reptiles can carry salmonella, so wash your hands before and after handling the snake or its equipment (RSPCA). The MSD Veterinary Manual says children under 10, pregnant women and elderly people are more susceptible, and advises not to clean the vivarium or its contents in the kitchen.

## How do I know my corn snake is healthy?

A healthy corn snake flicks its tongue often and has clear, bright eyes and smooth skin without blisters (RSPCA). When choosing a snake, the MSD Veterinary Manual looks for a rounded body, clear eyes, a clean vent and no wheezing, mucus or bubbles at the nostrils.

Warning signs named by the RSPCA include abnormal droppings (possible internal parasites), mouth rot, blisters (which can mean the snake is kept too damp), and mites: a snake spending unusually long periods in its water bowl, or small black specks on the snake or near the bowl. The RSPCA also names metabolic bone disease; see [Metabolic bone disease in reptiles](/animals/reptile-metabolic-bone-disease).

## When should a corn snake see a vet?

Soon after you get it, then every year: the MSD Veterinary Manual advises a prompt first examination by a vet and an annual check-up with a faecal examination. If you have any concerns about your snake's health, the RSPCA says to see your reptile vet urgently.

Not every practice treats snakes. The MSD Veterinary Manual says reptiles respond best to treatment from a vet familiar with their needs, so ask about experience with reptiles when you choose one.

## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

**Never release a corn snake.** The MSD Veterinary Manual says never to release a pet reptile into the wild, even if the species is native to your area; if you can no longer keep it, contact an animal-welfare organisation instead.

**Italy:** according to ISPRA, the Italian environment agency, DPR 102/2019 confirms the ban on releasing non-native species and populations into the wild (article 12, paragraph 3 of DPR 357/97); exceptions need an authorisation from the environment ministry (MASE). Some towns add their own rules: Verona's animal-welfare regulation, for example, discourages feeding live animals to other animals unless a vet certifies it is necessary, for example because the animal cannot get used to dead prey.

**Germany:** under § 40 of the Federal Nature Conservation Act (BNatSchG), releasing animals into the wild needs permission from the competent authority. The sources we opened don't say whether any German state's dangerous-animal rules cover corn snakes; ask your local authority before you buy.
