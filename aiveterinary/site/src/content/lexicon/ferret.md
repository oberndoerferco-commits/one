## How long do ferrets live?

Usually 6 to 10 years. The MSD Veterinary Manual gives a typical life span of 6 to 10 years and notes that many lives are shortened by accidents or illness; the RSPCA says around six years, and up to 10 if a ferret stays in good health.

So a ferret is a commitment of many years. Safe housing, the right food and regular vet checks, all covered below, give it the best chance of a long life.

## How big do ferrets get?

Ferrets are about the size of a small cat, and males are much bigger than females. The MSD Veterinary Manual says a male (hob) can weigh up to 2 kg and a female (jill) up to 1.2 kg, and that ferrets reach full size at 5 to 6 months and count as adults at one year.

Weight can change with the seasons: the PDSA says it is normal for a ferret to put on around 40% of its body weight for winter.

## Do ferrets need a companion?

Yes, in most cases. The PDSA says ferrets are highly social animals that need the company of other ferrets and can get lonely and stressed if kept alone, and it recommends pairs or small groups.

- **Plan for more than one.** The RSPCA advises planning to keep more than one ferret, ideally no more than four together, preferably littermates.
- **Match them carefully.** The RSPCA notes that unneutered males may be more aggressive, and that most ferrets are sociable but still need their own space. Repeated hiding, biting or fighting are signs that a group is not getting on.
- **A ferret on its own** needs companionship from daily time with you, says the RSPCA.

## How much do ferrets sleep and play?

A lot of both. The RSPCA says a healthy ferret may sleep 18 to 20 hours a day, and that ferrets are playful and intelligent and need mental and physical stimulation when they are awake.

The PDSA adds that ferrets quickly learn your routine and tend to play in the mornings and evenings. The MSD Veterinary Manual says ferrets need at least 2 to 4 hours a day outside their cage to stay healthy, supervised or in a ferret-proofed area.

## What do ferrets eat?

Meat. The RSPCA calls ferrets strict carnivores that need a well-balanced, meat-based diet, and says they can't be vegetarians.

- **High protein, low carbohydrate.** The MSD Veterinary Manual recommends a diet low in carbohydrate, moderate in fat and high in protein, usually a commercial ferret food. The RSPCA suggests asking your vet about a complete commercial diet.
- **No starchy or sugary foods.** The RSPCA says ferrets are thought to be unable to digest large amounts of fibre or starch, such as bread and cereals. The MSD Veterinary Manual advises against sugary foods such as honey, raisins and fruit.
- **Not dog or cat food.** The PDSA says dog and cat food is not suitable for ferrets.
- **Little and often.** Ferrets have a high metabolism and a short digestive tract and need to eat frequently (MSD), so food should be available throughout the day (RSPCA, PDSA).
- **Fresh water at all times.** The RSPCA warns that ferrets become seriously ill without it.

## How should I house a ferret?

In a large, secure, dry and draught-free space with places to sleep, play and explore. The PDSA recommends a minimum floor space of 10 m² per pair of ferrets, the bigger the better, plus several hours of play time outside the enclosure every day.

- **Sleeping places:** a secure spot with dust-free bedding, such as an old T-shirt, a hammock or a sleeping box (RSPCA).
- **A toilet area:** ferrets can be litter-trained and prefer to go in one spot, away from where they eat and sleep. The MSD Veterinary Manual advises against clumping cat litter, which may cause breathing problems.
- **Ferret-proofing:** ferrets chew things while exploring, so the RSPCA advises removing poisonous plants and electrical wires.
- **Indoors or outdoors:** the PDSA says ferrets can live inside or outside, but don't cope well with extreme temperatures.

## Can ferrets cope with heat?

Badly. Ferrets can't sweat to cool down, so the PDSA warns they are at risk of heatstroke in hot weather; the MSD Veterinary Manual says they are sensitive to heat and humidity because they have no sweat glands.

The sources give slightly different comfortable ranges. The PDSA says 15–24 °C, protected from direct sunlight, wind and rain; the RSPCA says around 15–21 °C; the MSD Veterinary Manual says 18–23 °C is usually comfortable and never over 90 °F (about 32 °C). In summer, give plenty of shade and cool areas (PDSA), and keep the temperature comfortable in vehicles too (RSPCA). Ferrets handle cold better than heat, but the MSD Veterinary Manual notes they may eat up to twice as much in the cold.

## Do ferrets bite?

They can. The PDSA says ferrets are often very friendly if they have been handled from a young age, but can give a very hard bite if they feel threatened; nipping now and then is normal.

The RSPCA says ferrets handled every day from an early age can form strong bonds with their owners.

## How do I keep my ferret healthy?

Register with a vet and book regular checks. The PDSA says every pet ferret should be seen at least every 6 to 12 months for a general health check, and sooner if you notice any sign of illness.

- **Distemper.** The PDSA says canine distemper is nearly always fatal in ferrets, even with treatment, and advises yearly vaccination for ferrets kept in the UK. Ask your vet what applies where you live.
- **Colds and flu.** The RSPCA says ferrets are highly susceptible to several strains of human influenza.
- **Neutering and seasons.** The PDSA explains that a female ferret can develop a life-threatening condition, hyperoestrogenism, and that surgical neutering may raise the chance of adrenal disease later in life. Talk to your vet about the options: see [female ferret in heat](/animals/female-ferret-in-heat) and [hair loss and adrenal disease](/animals/ferret-hair-loss-adrenal-disease).

## When should a ferret see a vet?

Immediately if your ferret has collapsed, has pale or bluish gums, is bleeding heavily, is choking or may have heatstroke. These are among the signs the MSD Veterinary Manual lists for seeing a vet immediately.

See a vet immediately if your ferret has:

- a weak pulse or a quiet heartbeat, or bluish or white gums or tongue
- heavy bleeding, bite marks, or puncture wounds to the chest or belly
- broken bones, burns, or a body temperature above 40.5 °C
- choking, or a rectum that protrudes from the bottom
- extreme lethargy, seizures, or staggering
- bloody, foul-smelling or uncontrollable diarrhoea, thick black droppings, or straining without passing droppings or urine
- stopped eating and passing droppings: MSD says this is what happens with a gut blockage; see [my ferret won't eat or is throwing up](/animals/ferret-not-eating-vomiting)
- weakness, wobbliness, dragging back legs or a seizure, which MSD links to low blood sugar from an insulinoma; see [my ferret is weak, wobbly or has collapsed](/animals/ferret-weak-wobbly-collapsed)

MSD advises seeing a vet within 24 hours for, among other things, vomiting or diarrhoea lasting more than 24 hours, a sudden change in behaviour or a ferret that sleeps more than usual and won't play. In hot weather, think of heatstroke first (PDSA). Not every practice sees ferrets often, so find a vet who knows them before you need one.

## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

Ferrets are one of the three species, with dogs and cats, covered by the EU pet passport. To travel with a ferret between EU countries, including Italy and Germany, it needs a microchip, a valid rabies vaccination and an EU pet passport.

- **The rules since 22 April 2026** come from Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/131: identification with an injectable transponder (microchip, Article 7), a complete primary course of rabies vaccination at least 21 days before the journey, or a valid booster (Article 8), and a passport issued in the EU country where the owner habitually lives (Article 11).
- **Old passports:** passports on the previous model stay valid if they were issued before 1 January 2028 (Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/705, Article 6).
- **Young ferrets** under 12 weeks without a rabies vaccination, or 12 to 16 weeks old whose vaccination is not yet 21 days old, may travel only to countries that allow it, and only under extra conditions (Regulation (EU) 2026/131, Article 9).

The details, including extra rules of some countries, are in [travelling with a dog, cat or ferret inside the EU](/animals/travelling-eu-pets) and on our [travel page](/travel).
