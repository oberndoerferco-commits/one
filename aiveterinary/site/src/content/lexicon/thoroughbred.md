## Where does the Thoroughbred come from?

The Thoroughbred was bred for racing in Britain and Ireland from imported Arab, Turk and Barb stallions crossed with local mares (McGivney et al., 2020). The Arab Horse Society of Great Britain traces the modern Thoroughbred to three Arabian horses brought to England in the 18th century: the Godolphin Arabian, the Byerley Turk and the Darley Arabian. Today it is a global breed of about 500,000 horses.

We found no published lifespan study for Thoroughbreds kept as riding or companion horses.

## Is the Thoroughbred inbred?

Increasingly so. Because the breed started from few founders, its genetic diversity is very low compared with other modern breeds. A study of 10,118 Thoroughbreds from Europe, Australasia, North America, South Africa and Japan found a highly significant rise in inbreeding over the last five decades, as a few popular sire lines are used more and more. The authors think current breeding practices are unlikely to halt this, and that the breed should be monitored (McGivney et al., 2020).

## What is lung bleeding (EIPH)?

Exercise-induced pulmonary haemorrhage is bleeding from the lungs during hard exercise. An expert consensus of the American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine says it occurs in the majority of Thoroughbred and Standardbred racehorses (Hinchcliff et al., 2015). Most of it is hidden: only 0 to 6.2% of horses with EIPH have a nosebleed after exercise, and the rest is found by looking into the airways with an endoscope or testing a sample from them. The risk of nosebleeds is partly inherited (heritability 0.23 to 0.27) and was about 2.8 times higher in horses that had raced for two or more years than in their first year.

A horse that bleeds from the nose after exercise should be seen by a vet; there are other, rarer causes too.

## Do Thoroughbreds get stomach ulcers?

Very often, especially in training. According to the European College of Equine Internal Medicine, ulcers of the upper, squamous part of the stomach are most common in Thoroughbred racehorses: 37% of untrained horses have them, rising to 80 to 100% within two to three months of starting race training. Ulcers of the lower, glandular part were found in 47 to 65% of Australian Thoroughbred racehorses (Sykes et al., 2015).

Fast work raises the pressure in the abdomen and pushes acid up onto the unprotected lining. Ulcers have been linked with poor appetite or "fussy" eating, poor body condition and poorer performance; one study also found a link with a rough coat. In one study of Thoroughbreds in training, horses with some turnout had fewer squamous ulcers, especially if turned out with other horses; another study found no effect.

## What does this mean if I take on a Thoroughbred?

Many Thoroughbreds kept for riding have come out of racing, where stomach ulcers and lung bleeding are very common. If your horse eats poorly, loses condition or seems unhappy in work, ask your vet whether a stomach scope is worthwhile.
