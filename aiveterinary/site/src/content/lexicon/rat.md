## How long do pet rats live?

Pet rats usually live about 1.5 to 3 years, according to the MSD Veterinary Manual. The German veterinary animal-welfare association TVT gives a life expectancy of 2–4 years.

Adult females typically weigh 350–450 g and males 450–650 g, and rats are about 9–11 inches (23–28 cm) long without the tail (MSD Veterinary Manual).

## Do rats need a friend?

Yes, always. The RSPCA says rats are social and need the company of other rats; living alone, they can get depressed and develop abnormal behaviour. Keep them in pairs or small same-sex groups.

- **Groups:** the TVT recommends same-sex groups of mixed ages and says rats must not be kept alone, even though they bond closely with their owner and recognise them by smell and voice.
- **New rats:** young rats usually settle into an existing group without problems, most easily two at a time; with adult rats it doesn't always work (TVT).
- **Mixed sexes:** rats breed very fast, so the RSPCA and the TVT say males in mixed groups must be neutered.
- **Other species:** the TVT says rats cannot be kept together with other animals.

## Are rats nocturnal?

Rats are normally active in the morning and evening and sleep in the daytime, according to the RSPCA, which advises not disturbing them while they sleep. They don't sleep in one long block, though: the TVT describes a wake–rest rhythm of about 1.5 to 2.5 hours.

Rats are curious and intelligent and get tame quickly. The TVT suggests about 2 hours a day for play and activity with them, plus supervised free-roaming time in a rat-proofed room without cables, open windows or poisonous plants.

## What do rats eat?

Rats eat mainly plant food but also some animal food (TVT). A commercial rat mix, usually grains and vegetable flakes, can be topped up with fresh vegetables, fruit or cat grass and, if the mix doesn't contain it, a little animal protein such as hard-boiled egg, hard cheese or feeding insects.

- **Gnawing:** rats need gnawing material all the time, such as fresh unsprayed twigs and untreated wood, to wear down their teeth (TVT).
- **Foraging:** the TVT suggests hiding food in cardboard tubes or scattering it in fresh bedding so the rats have to work for it, as they would in nature.
- **Weight:** rats that move too little and get too much food tend to become overweight (TVT).
- **Water:** renew drinking water every day (TVT).

Rats cannot vomit, so even small amounts of toxic food, plants or chemicals can be fatal (RSPCA). Give only medicines a vet has prescribed for that rat.

## How big should a rat cage be?

For up to three rats, at least 100 × 50 × 100 cm (length × width × height), or as a tower 80 × 50 × 120 cm, each with several levels, according to the TVT. The levels must be solid: raised levels made of wire mesh are not suitable.

- **Inside:** water, a heavy food bowl, several sleeping houses, climbing branches, ladders, ropes, hammocks and resting boards on different levels; wooden and cardboard tubes as hiding places (TVT).
- **No wheels:** the TVT says commercially sold wheels are all too small for rats and risk injuring their long tails.
- **Bedding and nests:** small-animal bedding, hay or straw on the floor, and plain unscented paper tissue for nests (TVT).
- **Place:** quiet, draught-free and not too bright, ideally at table height, where other pets can't disturb them (TVT). Albino rats need a darker room to protect their light-sensitive eyes.

The RSPCA notes that rats gnawing the cage bars may be bored or frustrated, and the cage may be too small.

## What temperature do rats need?

The MSD Veterinary Manual gives a cage temperature range of 18–26 °C. Draughts matter as much as heat: the TVT says rats are very prone to respiratory disease and must never be exposed to draughts.

## How do I pick up a rat?

Put one hand gently round the chest and support the hindquarters with the other. Never lift a rat by the tail, because the tail is easily injured (TVT).

The TVT advises that children under 10 should only care for rats with an adult supervising, because handling them needs a gentleness small children usually don't yet have.

## How do I know my rat is healthy?

Check your rats every morning and evening. The RSPCA says a healthy rat moves well, breathes easily, sits normally, and has bright eyes and a shiny coat.

- **Lumps:** run your fingers over each rat to feel for lumps, and weigh them regularly (RSPCA). The TVT says rats often develop tumours, partly hereditary, which can burden them a lot depending on the organ affected.
- **Red staining around the eyes and nose:** a gland near the eye produces a red secretion. Small amounts can happen with stress; large amounts can signal a serious problem (RSPCA). The TVT says rats produce more of it under ongoing stress, for example from group tensions, a small cage or illness.
- **Coat:** a lot of scratching and grooming, restlessness and hair loss can mean lice or rat mites, which need treatment from a vet (TVT).
- **Teeth:** rat teeth grow continuously (RSPCA); regular vet check-ups confirm they are growing correctly.

Rats hide pain well, the RSPCA says, so watch closely and ask your vet if you are unsure.

## When should a rat see a vet?

Immediately if you think your rat has eaten something toxic: rats cannot vomit, and even small amounts can be fatal (RSPCA). Contact a vet too if your rat:

- is **not eating or drinking**, has **diarrhoea** or a **sudden weight change** (RSPCA)
- is **sneezing, wheezing or breathing unusually**, or has **discharge from the eyes or nose** (RSPCA); the TVT says rats are very prone to respiratory disease
- is **less active, sleeps a lot, sits hunched or stiff, walks unsteadily** or guards a painful spot (RSPCA)
- has **bald patches**, chews the cage bars over and over, or is overweight (RSPCA)
- **avoids the other rats** (RSPCA)

## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

**EU:** there are no harmonised EU travel rules for rats. The European Commission's pet travel FAQ says the EU pet passport is only issued for dogs, cats and ferrets. Since 22 April 2026 the EU rules for travelling with pets come from Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/131; its detailed rules are for dogs, cats and ferrets and for pet birds entering from outside the EU, and it does not mention rodents. The Animal Health Law, Regulation (EU) 2016/429, lists "rodents and rabbits other than those intended for food production" in Part B of its Annex I, and for Part B species the Commission says there are no harmonised EU rules: check with the EU country you are travelling to for its national rules. The previous rules, Regulation (EU) No 576/2013, applied until 21 April 2026.

**Germany:** the TVT figures above are recommendations by a veterinary association (dated April 2014), not a statutory minimum. In the TVT's view some breeding forms, such as Rex, hairless, patchwork and tailless rats, are torture breeding (*Qualzucht*), and it calls running or jogging balls a matter of animal-welfare concern.

**Italy:** the sources we opened set no Italian minimum cage size for rats; your vet can tell you about local rules.
