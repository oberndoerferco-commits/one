## What is a Friesian?

A horse breed from the Netherlands, known for its black coat, long mane and tail, feathered legs and gentle nature (FHANA). The original and largest registry is the Royal Friesian Horse Studbook (KFPS), founded in 1879. Its breeding goal is a functionally built utility horse with the Friesian breed features that is healthy and vital, has a talent for sport, a reliable character and is workable.

There is no published lifespan study for Friesians that we could find.

## How big is a Friesian?

Large. To earn the studbook's Star predicate, mares must measure at least 156 cm and geldings and stallions 158 cm (FHANA). A 2016 study gives the breed standard's minimum as 1.53 m (Leegwater et al.). Friesians mature slowly: the breed society says many are not worked hard until after the age of four, and that they reach their anaerobic threshold sooner than other breeds, so work should be built up in shorter periods.

## Why does inbreeding matter in Friesians?

The breed went through periods with very few horses, and a few influential stallions had many offspring in a closed studbook. Pedigree data show an inbreeding rate of 1.5% per generation, above the 1% limit the FAO sets to keep genetic variation in a breed (Ducro et al., 2015). Two inherited diseases are well understood:

**Dwarfism.** Affected foals have short legs, ribs that bend inwards and lax flexor tendons, so the fetlocks sink and the gait becomes abnormal. It is recessive: a foal is only affected if it inherits the variant from both parents. In one group of 177 Friesians, 22 were carriers (Leegwater et al., 2016).

**Hydrocephalus.** Fluid builds up inside the foal's brain. It often leads to stillbirth and a difficult birth for the mare. It is also recessive. Of horses tested commercially, 13.3% of 60 stallions and 17.3% of 805 mares were carriers (Ducro et al., 2015).

DNA tests exist for both. The breed society advises testing all mares, comparing the result with the stallion's, and never breeding a carrier to a carrier.

## What other health problems are more common?

**Megaoesophagus.** The gullet (oesophagus) becomes greatly enlarged, which can lead to choke and death. In 852 horses examined after death at a US diagnostic laboratory, all 6 with megaoesophagus were Friesians, although only 17 Friesians were in the group (Komine et al., 2014).

**Rupture of the aorta.** The main artery from the heart can tear in young Friesians, usually at the aortic arch rather than at its root as in other breeds (Ploeg et al., 2017). The tear often forms a false aneurysm that breaks through into the lung artery, and affected horses may live for weeks to months before it is found (Saey et al., 2016). Standard heart scans and post-mortems can miss it, so vets need to look for it specifically.

**Other points from the breed society:** some Friesians cannot sweat properly (anhidrosis) and need help to stay cool in hot, humid weather; retained placentas are more common than in other horses; the long feathers need dry pasture to avoid leg infections; and Friesians are stoic and mask pain.

## What does this mean for an owner?

Because Friesians hide pain, the breed society advises knowing your horse well and calling the vet at the first sign that it is not acting normally. Keep a record of your horse's own normal temperature, pulse and breathing to compare with; see [vital signs in horses](/animals/horse-vital-signs). Signs of colic always need a vet: see [colic in horses](/animals/horse-colic). If you plan to breed, ask for the DNA results of both parents.
