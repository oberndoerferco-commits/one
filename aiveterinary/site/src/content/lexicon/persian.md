## How long do Persian cats live?

In UK vet records of 3,235 Persians (VetCompass, 2013), the median lifespan was 13.5 years, and the most common causes of death were kidney disease (23.4%), cancer (8.5%) and mass-related disorders (8.0%) (O'Neill et al., 2019). VetCompass life tables for cats that died in 2019 put a Persian's life expectancy at birth at 10.93 years, against 11.89 years for crossbred cats (Teng et al.). The GCCF, the UK's main cat registry, gives 14 to 16 years.

## How big does a Persian cat get?

The GCCF describes a medium to large, heavily boned cat with a short, cobby body, a broad chest and short, thick legs. It gives 6–12 lb (about 2.7–5.4 kg) for females and more than 12 lb for males. Adult Persians in UK vet care weighed 3.9 kg on average.

## What health problems do Persian cats have?

In the VetCompass study, 64.9% of Persians had at least one disorder recorded in a year. The most common were:

- **coat disorders** such as matting: 12.7%
- **dental (periodontal) disease**: 11.3%
- **overgrown nails**: 7.2%
- **eye discharge**: 5.8%

By group, skin (17.9%), eye (15.3%) and dental problems (12.3%) led. The authors say these problems are typical of the breed's appearance and call for more owner awareness to manage and prevent them.

**Polycystic kidney disease (PKD)** is inherited: cysts grow in the kidneys and eventually cause kidney failure. The GCCF says it was found in more than a third of Persian and Exotic Shorthair cats in the 1990s; studies collected by UFAW reported it in 36–49% of Persians, and International Cat Care says up to 50% or more may have been affected in the past. A DNA test now lets breeders avoid it, and in many countries PKD has become much rarer. Since 1 January 2016, the GCCF requires PKD tests for Persians on its breeding register: ask to see the parents' PKD certificates. **Progressive retinal atrophy (PRA)**, an eye disease, also occurs, and there is a DNA test for it (GCCF).

For kidney disease in older cats, see [kidney disease in cats](/animals/cat-kidney-disease); for runny or sore eyes, see [eye problems in cats](/animals/cat-eye-problems).

## Why does the flat face cause problems?

Modern Persians are brachycephalic: the skull and face are shortened. The GCCF itself says this face shape may make individual cats prone to breathing difficulties, skin and eye problems. The UFAW genetic welfare database lists:

- **breathing problems** (brachycephalic airway obstruction), with discomfort, distress, disrupted sleep and restricted activity
- **dental problems** from misshapen jaws, needing frequent vet care
- **trouble grooming**
- **constant tear overflow** onto the face, causing sore, inflamed and infected skin
- **bulging eyes**, which raise the risk of painful corneal ulcers and sequestra and can lead to partial loss of sight

The more extreme the face, the higher the risk; UFAW says all cats with the two most severe face types are likely to have at least some of these conditions, which tend to last or come back throughout life. Surgery and medicines can relieve some of them but are unlikely to cure them.

The RSPCA lists noisy breathing, and collapse or fainting after play, among the signs of breathing problems in flat-faced cats, and advises keeping them cool and out of the sun, cleaning and drying skin folds daily, and taking them for regular vet and dental checks. Flat-faced cats may also have more difficult births.

## Is a Persian cat right for me?

The GCCF describes Persians as sweet and gentle, preferring a calm home and gentle handling, and happy to stay on the ground rather than climb. Their long coat needs combing every day with a metal comb, and their eyes need wiping daily.

Think about the face, too. International Cat Care names extreme, very flat-faced Persians as an example of a breed in which the look itself is harmful, and believes such traits should not be continued by breeding. If you choose a Persian, look for a cat with a less extreme face and open nostrils (the GCCF standard asks for nostrils that allow free passage of air), and ask for the parents' PKD results.

## Rules in Germany

German law bans breeding vertebrates when it is to be expected that the offspring will suffer pain, suffering or harm because inherited body parts or organs are missing, unsuitable or altered (§ 11b Tierschutzgesetz). The law describes the harm and does not list breeds.
