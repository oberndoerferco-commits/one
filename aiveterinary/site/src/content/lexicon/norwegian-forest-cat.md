## How long do Norwegian Forest Cats live?

VetCompass life tables for cats that died in 2019 in the UK put the Norwegian Forest Cat's life expectancy at birth at 9.95 years, against 11.89 years for crossbred cats (Teng et al., 2024). That figure rests on only 15 deaths, the minimum for a breed to be analysed on its own, so its margin of error is wide (7.55 to 12.35 years). The GCCF, the UK's main cat registry, says the usual lifespan is 14 to 16 years.

## How big does a Norwegian Forest Cat get?

Large. The GCCF describes a big, long, muscular, heavy-boned cat that stands high on its legs. It matures slowly and is not considered fully grown until about four to five years. Males weigh 12 to 16 pounds or more (about 5.4 to 7.3 kg), females 9 to 12 pounds (about 4.1 to 5.4 kg).

## How much grooming does the coat need?

Less than many longhaired breeds. The semi-long coat has a dense woolly undercoat under a smooth, water-repellent top coat. The GCCF calls it fairly low maintenance, though the thick winter coat needs more attention, and dead coat should be combed out in the spring moult to stop knots forming. The PDSA agrees that the breed should not need daily care, with a little extra grooming in spring.

## What health problems do Norwegian Forest Cats have?

The GCCF calls the breed generally robust and healthy. The main inherited problem is **glycogen storage disease type IV**: affected kittens are stillborn or die shortly after birth, although some develop normally until four or five months of age. Both parents must be carriers for a kitten to be affected, and a DNA test finds carriers. The breed's advisory committee recommends testing all breeding cats where the disease may be in the lines. International Cat Care lists this disease among the more common inherited disorders of cats.

The PDSA also lists **polycystic kidney disease (PKD)**, in which cysts in the kidneys grow until the kidneys fail; there is a genetic test (see [kidney disease in cats](/animals/cat-kidney-disease)). The GCCF mentions some anecdotal evidence that the breed is prone to urinary problems and advises care with diet; for signs to watch for, see [FLUTD in cats](/animals/cat-flutd).

## Is a Norwegian Forest Cat right for me?

The GCCF describes a gentle, friendly cat that enjoys family life without demanding attention, can be reserved with strangers and has a quiet voice. It has a lot of energy, loves climbing and interactive games, and needs plenty of toys and a strong climbing frame. It gets on with respectful children, other cats and cat-friendly dogs. Ask for the parents' GSD IV test results.
