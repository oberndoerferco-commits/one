## How long do Border Collies live?

Longer than most dogs. A 2024 UK study of 20,886 Border Collies found a median lifespan of 13.1 years, against 12.0 years for crossbred dogs (McMillan et al.). VetCompass life tables, built from UK vet records, put a Border Collie's life expectancy at birth at 12.10 years, the third highest of the 18 breeds studied after the Jack Russell Terrier and the Yorkshire Terrier (Teng et al.). The Royal Kennel Club gives the breed's lifespan as over 12 years.

## How big does a Border Collie get?

Medium-sized. The FCI breed standard gives an ideal height at the withers of 53 cm for males, slightly less for females, and describes an athletic dog with "sufficient substance to give impression of endurance", with a body slightly longer than it is tall. The standard gives no weight.

## What health problems do Border Collies have?

**Epilepsy:** the UFAW genetic welfare database lists the Border Collie among the breeds with a particularly high rate of idiopathic epilepsy, repeated seizures without another cause that can be found. Most dogs have their first seizure between 1 and 4 years of age. The disease cannot be cured and needs lifelong treatment; it is probably caused by several genes, and there is no test to find carriers. A seizure that does not stop, or several in a row, is an emergency.

**Eyes:** the Royal Kennel Club recommends eye examinations before breeding, including gonioscopy, which looks for an abnormal drainage angle in the eye that raises the risk of glaucoma. The PDSA explains glaucoma as raised pressure in the eye that can cause extreme pain and loss of the eye. Collie eye anomaly, an abnormality at the back of the eye, can be found with a DNA test.

**Other inherited diseases** with DNA tests include ceroid lipofuscinosis (a cell storage disorder that causes early death), trapped neutrophil syndrome (which weakens the immune system), Imerslund-Gräsbeck syndrome (an inherited blood disorder), sensory neuropathy (a nerve disease) and Raine syndrome (Royal Kennel Club, PDSA).

**MDR1:** dogs with this gene variant are more sensitive to some commonly used medicines (PDSA). If your dog has been tested, tell your vet the result.

**Heart and joints:** in a VetCompass study, Border Collies had higher odds of degenerative mitral valve disease than crossbred dogs (Mattin et al.). The PDSA also lists hip dysplasia. If your dog limps or is stiff, see [limping in dogs](/animals/dog-limping).

## Which health tests matter for a Border Collie puppy?

The Royal Kennel Club lists as good practice before breeding an eye test with gonioscopy under the BVA/KC/ISDS Eye Scheme. As best practice it adds DNA tests for collie eye anomaly, ceroid lipofuscinosis, Imerslund-Gräsbeck syndrome, MDR1, trapped neutrophil syndrome, sensory neuropathy, goniodysgenesis and glaucoma, and Raine syndrome; hip and elbow scoring under the BVA/KC schemes; and a hearing test (BAER), including litter screening. Ask to see the parents' results.

## How much exercise does a Border Collie need?

A lot. The Royal Kennel Club gives more than 2 hours a day and a large garden. The PDSA says at least two hours, with off-lead running in a safe area and plenty of training and games for the mind, and warns that a bored Collie may chew anything in reach.

## Is a Border Collie right for me?

The breed was made to work sheep, and the Royal Kennel Club says its activity and intelligence mean it needs to be engaged to lead a fulfilled life. The FCI standard describes a tenacious, hard-working, very trainable dog, keen, alert and intelligent, neither nervous nor aggressive. That only tells you what breeders aim for: every dog is an individual. Plan for hours of exercise and mental work every day, grooming more than once a week, and ask for the parents' eye, hip, elbow and DNA results.
