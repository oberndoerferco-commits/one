## How long do guinea pigs live?

Most guinea pigs live about 5 to 8 years, according to the MSD Veterinary Manual. That makes them a commitment of many years, not a short-term pet.

Other sources give similar ranges: the PDSA says four to eight years on average, and the German veterinary animal-welfare association TVT says 6 to 8 years, rarely up to 12.

## Do guinea pigs need a friend?

Yes. Guinea pigs are social animals and should live with at least one other friendly guinea pig. The RSPCA warns that guinea pigs left on their own for long periods can develop abnormal behaviour and may suffer.

- **Good pairings:** the RSPCA suggests a neutered male with one or more females, two females, or brothers raised together. Males should always be neutered if they live with females.
- **Groups:** the PDSA goes further and recommends groups of three or more; the TVT says at least two, better a group.
- **Not with rabbits:** the RSPCA says rabbits and guinea pigs don't make ideal companions, because rabbits may bully or injure guinea pigs. The MSD Veterinary Manual adds that some diseases can spread between the two species, and the TVT names respiratory infections that can be fatal for guinea pigs.

## What do guinea pigs eat?

Mostly hay: unlimited good-quality grass hay should be available at all times, with fresh vegetables every day and a small amount of guinea pig pellets (MSD Veterinary Manual). The PDSA says hay and grass should make up 80–90% of the diet.

Vitamin C is the one thing guinea pigs cannot do without. They cannot make it themselves, so it has to come from their food, and a lack of it causes scurvy. The MSD Veterinary Manual notes that the vitamin C in pellets breaks down over time, so pellets should be bought fresh and topped up with vitamin C–rich vegetables. Rabbit pellets are not a substitute: they don't contain enough vitamin C for guinea pigs. Read more in [Guinea pig vitamin C deficiency](/animals/guinea-pig-vitamin-c).

Fresh water must always be available. Some garden plants and household foods are dangerous; see [Plants and foods that are dangerous for rabbits and guinea pigs](/animals/rabbit-guinea-pig-toxic-plants).

## How big should a guinea pig enclosure be?

The TVT recommends at least 2 m² of floor space for two to four guinea pigs, plus at least 0.5 m² for each additional animal, and says a pure cage set-up is not appropriate for them.

- A cage can be part of the enclosure, but the TVT says only as a retreat that stays open all the time, and at least 120 × 60 × 50 cm.
- Give each guinea pig its own shelter: the TVT asks for at least one sleeping house per animal, each with two exits; the RSPCA says one more hiding place than there are guinea pigs.
- Use a solid floor. The MSD Veterinary Manual says to avoid wire-mesh floors because guinea pigs have delicate feet.
- Daily exercise outside the cage is essential, in a safe area without cables or other chewing hazards (MSD Veterinary Manual).

## What temperature do guinea pigs need?

Guinea pigs do best in a cool, even temperature. The RSPCA gives 17–20 °C as ideal, says temperatures above 26 °C can cause heatstroke, and below 15 °C they can become chilled.

The TVT calls guinea pigs heat-sensitive and warns that fatal heatstroke can occur above 28 °C, so outdoor enclosures need constant shade. Keep indoor housing away from radiators, sunny windows and draughts (RSPCA).

## How do I pick up a guinea pig?

Calmly, with both hands: one hand supports the chest and the other the hindquarters (MSD Veterinary Manual). Guinea pigs rarely bite when handled calmly, and handling them from a young age helps them get used to it.

The TVT adds that guinea pigs are not cuddly animals: stroke them only when they come to you, and never lift one by the scruff or with one hand. A guinea pig that freezes is frightened, not relaxed.

## How do I know my guinea pig is healthy?

Watch your guinea pigs every day, and check their front teeth and nails every week (RSPCA). Guinea pigs hide illness: the RSPCA says they feel pain but show few outward signs of it, and the MSD Veterinary Manual says they often hide signs of illness until they are very sick.

- **Daily:** look for signs of illness or injury, such as not eating, being quieter than normal or hiding more (RSPCA). In warm weather the RSPCA advises checking the fur and skin around the rear end twice a day.
- **Regularly:** the TVT recommends checking weight, front teeth, claws, all body openings and the coat.
- **Yearly:** the MSD Veterinary Manual advises a check-up at least once a year with a vet experienced with guinea pigs.

Their teeth grow throughout life and are worn down by chewing hay. The MSD Veterinary Manual notes that the back teeth often cause problems even when the front teeth look normal.

## When should a guinea pig see a vet?

Straight away if your guinea pig stops eating, eats or drinks less, or passes fewer or no droppings: the RSPCA says to consult a vet immediately, because the animal could be seriously ill. The TVT puts it plainly: refusing food is always an emergency.

Go now, and call ahead, if your guinea pig:

- has **stopped eating** or is eating much less (see [My guinea pig is not eating or pooping](/animals/guinea-pig-not-eating))
- has **fewer droppings or none**
- has **difficulty breathing**: the MSD Veterinary Manual says to seek care immediately (see [Guinea pig sneezing or breathing heavily](/animals/guinea-pig-sneezing))
- is **drooling**, which can point to dental disease (MSD Veterinary Manual)
- seems to be **in pain, ill or injured** (RSPCA)

See a vet soon, too, for bald patches or scaly skin, which the TVT says are often caused by fungi or mites and need prompt veterinary care; skin fungus can also spread to people. More in [Guinea pig losing hair or scratching](/animals/guinea-pig-hair-loss-mites).

## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

**EU:** there are no harmonised EU travel rules for guinea pigs. The European Commission's pet travel FAQ says the EU pet passport is only issued for dogs, cats and ferrets, and that a guinea pig is not covered. Since 22 April 2026 the EU rules for travelling with pets come from Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/131; its detailed rules are for dogs, cats and ferrets and for pet birds entering from outside the EU, and it does not mention rodents. The Animal Health Law, Regulation (EU) 2016/429, lists "rodents and rabbits other than those intended for food production" in Part B of its Annex I, and for Part B species the Commission says there are no harmonised EU rules: check with the EU country you are travelling to for its national rules. The previous rules, Regulation (EU) No 576/2013, applied until 21 April 2026.

**Germany:** the TVT housing figures above are recommendations by a veterinary expert panel, written against the duties of § 2 of the German Animal Welfare Act (TierSchG), not a statutory minimum.

**Italy:** the sources we opened set no Italian minimum enclosure size for guinea pigs; your vet can tell you about local rules.
