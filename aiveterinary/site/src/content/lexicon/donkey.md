## How is a donkey different from a horse?

A donkey is not a small horse. Donkeys and horses are closely related equids, but the Swiss Federal Food Safety and Veterinary Office (BLV) explains that they differ in origin, needs and behaviour: horses come from the temperate steppes of Eurasia, domestic donkeys from the hot, dry, stony regions of East Africa.

- **They hide pain.** The BLV and the University of Florida (UF/IFAS) describe donkeys as stoic: they show pain or discomfort very little, so illness is often noticed late, and pain can be mistaken for stubbornness.
- **They stand their ground.** Where a horse instinctively flees from danger, a donkey tends to stop, assess the situation and then decide how to react (BLV). UF/IFAS says donkeys rely more on freezing and fighting than on flight.
- **They need less food energy.** Donkeys use food very efficiently and need less protein and sugar than horses (BLV).
- **They are more prone to hyperlipaemia,** a dangerous metabolic disorder (see below).
- **They feel the wet more.** A donkey's coat has little undercoat, soaks through and dries slowly, and unlike horses, donkeys have no marked layer of fat under the skin to insulate them (BLV).
- **Their hooves are different:** narrower and more oval, so they must be trimmed differently from a horse's (BLV).
- **Their body condition is judged differently.** The BLV says the large, round "pot belly" is normal in donkeys, not a sign of being overweight, and that donkey-specific body condition scores, such as The Donkey Sanctuary's, should be used.

## Does a donkey need a companion?

Yes, ideally another donkey. The Defra code of practice says donkeys have particular social needs and can become ill, potentially fatally, if they are suddenly separated from a companion they have bonded with.

The BLV explains that donkeys form very close, stable bonds with individual companions, and UF/IFAS that separating a bonded pair can cause extreme stress and loss of appetite, which can lead to hyperlipaemia. Keep bonded donkeys together, also at the vet: UF/IFAS says a bonded companion should be present for examination and treatment, and the Defra code advises planning for the extra costs of moving a bonded pair, for example to a veterinary hospital.

## What do donkeys eat?

Mostly straw. The Defra code says donkeys should have constant access to feed-quality straw, with access to grass, hay or haylage restricted according to the individual donkey, and does not recommend grazing muzzles for donkeys.

- **Low sugar.** Food rich in easily digested sugars, as in fresh young grass or short frozen grass in winter, can lead to serious metabolic disease and laminitis, says the BLV. It suggests a firm-surfaced paddock as an alternative or addition to grazing.
- **No overfeeding.** The Defra code warns that obesity in donkeys can cause hyperlipaemia, which can be fatal, so take extra care that a donkey does not become overweight.
- **No crash diets either.** The BLV advises reducing food in a controlled way, never withdrawing it abruptly.
- **Fresh water every day.** Donkeys tolerate thirst well, but the BLV says they need about 8–10 litres per 100 kg of body weight a day, and that wet grass alone is not enough.

## How much space and shelter does a donkey need?

The Defra code says each donkey needs at least 0.2 to 0.4 hectares (half an acre to an acre) of grazing, and a roofed shelter with solid walls and a clean, dry area to stand on, because donkeys are not well adapted to wet and cold conditions.

The BLV recommends that donkeys have access at all times in winter to a dry, draught-free shelter that protects them from rain, snow and wind, even if they are out for only a few hours. It also warns that wet or muddy ground over long periods can lead to hoof disease such as thrush and hoof abscesses.

## How should I handle a donkey?

With patience and calm, clear signals. The BLV says donkeys cannot be driven like horses: pressure or force can make them resist or refuse, and their wait-and-see reaction to danger is an adaptation, not stubbornness.

## What is hyperlipaemia in donkeys?

A life-threatening metabolic disorder in which the body mobilises too much fat. It can follow when a donkey eats less or stops eating, and the BLV gives a mortality rate of 50 to 80%.

- **Why donkeys:** they store and mobilise energy very efficiently, which makes them prone to dangerous fat mobilisation when they take in too little energy (BLV). UF/IFAS adds that donkeys cannot efficiently regulate the release of fat.
- **At most risk:** obese, pregnant, nursing and older donkeys (BLV).
- **Triggers:** sudden changes in the amount or quality of food (UF/IFAS), stress such as separation from a bonded companion (UF/IFAS), and obesity (Defra code).
- **What to do:** a donkey that is not eating needs a vet immediately (see below). UF/IFAS says hyperlipaemia requires rapid treatment and is potentially fatal.

## How often do donkey hooves need trimming?

Regularly, generally every 6 to 10 weeks, depending on how much natural wear the hooves get, says UF/IFAS. Because donkey hooves differ from horse hooves, they need someone who knows how to trim them (BLV).

The BLV notes that hooves affected by chronic laminitis grow differently and need trimming and correcting at shorter intervals.

## When should a donkey see a vet?

Immediately if your donkey stops eating or only pretends to eat. The BLV calls this always a serious emergency, because of the risk of hyperlipaemia, and the Defra code says loss of appetite in a donkey can be life-threatening in a very short time, so veterinary advice should be sought immediately.

Call your vet straight away, too, if your donkey:

- is severely lame, especially in the front legs, or stands rocked back on its hind legs: the BLV lists these as signs of acute laminitis, which UF/IFAS calls very painful and potentially fatal
- shows signs of pain or discomfort, such as reluctance to move, pawing, rolling, faster breathing or sweating, or is reluctant or unable to stand (Defra code)

Because donkeys hide pain, even small changes count: a change in demeanour, a stiff gait, reluctance to move or changes in appetite or droppings (BLV, Defra code). ## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

For EU law, a donkey is an equine animal like a horse, so the same identification, passport and food-chain rules apply, in Italy, Germany and every other EU country.

- **Donkeys count as equines:** Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/963 defines an "equine animal" as a kept animal of the genus Equus, including horses, asses and zebras (Article 2).
- **Food-chain status:** equines are deemed to be intended for slaughter for human consumption unless they are irreversibly excluded by a signed entry in the passport (Article 38), and the vet must check this status before certain treatments (Article 39).
- **Medicines:** a vet may use medicines outside their authorisation under the rules for animals not kept for food only if the passport declares the animal not intended for slaughter for human consumption (Regulation (EU) 2019/6, Article 112(4)).
