## How long do cockatiels live?

Up to 20 years, according to the MSD Veterinary Manual; LafeberVet, a veterinary information site on exotic animals, gives a mean lifespan of 12 to 15 years and says up to 25 years has been reported.

That makes a cockatiel a long commitment. LafeberVet describes cockatiels as sociable birds. The MSD Veterinary Manual adds that they are vocal but relatively quiet compared with macaws or cockatoos, and that rarer colour mutations may be more fragile.

## How big is a cockatiel?

About 32 cm long and 80 to 125 g, on average 90 g, according to LafeberVet.

LafeberVet explains how to tell the sexes apart:

- **males** have a yellow forehead, throat, crest and cheek patches, with orange ear patches, and solid black feathers under the wings
- **females** have much less yellow and a barred pattern under the wings
- **young birds** look like females until they mature at about 6 months

## Should cockatiels be kept in pairs?

Yes. The Leipzig University bird clinic says cockatiels live in large flocks in Australia, a pair is the acceptable minimum, and keeping one cockatiel alone for good is not appropriate.

- The Leipzig clinic recommends a male with a female, or two males, as two females often don't get along.
- The RSPCA lists cockatiels among the colony-breeding species that can live in groups, as long as the birds get on and have room to fly and to get away from each other.

## How big should a cockatiel cage be?

LafeberVet gives a cage at least 50–60 cm long and wide; the Leipzig bird clinic recommends an aviary of at least 1.5 × 1 × 1 m for a pair, with 50% more floor area for each further pair.

- The RSPCA bases cage size on wingspan: if your bird spends most of its time in the cage, height, width and depth should each be at least twice its wingspan.
- For a group of more than two birds of 50 to 100 g, such as cockatiels, the RSPCA says to add 10% to the cage for every bird over the first two.
- Neither replaces free flight: the Leipzig clinic calls cockatiels long-distance fliers that need to fly every day, and the RSPCA asks for at least six hours a day out of the cage.
- LafeberVet advises at least two perches, far enough apart for the bird to fly or glide between them.

## What do cockatiels eat?

The RSPCA recommends about three-quarters pellets and one quarter washed fruit and vegetables, with some seeds each day but no more than a tenth of the diet; the Leipzig bird clinic suggests 70% seeds and 30% greens, vegetables and fruit.

The sources disagree on seeds, so ask your bird vet:

- **LafeberVet:** all-seed diets lack protein, vitamins and minerals, including calcium and vitamin A; a varied diet makes obesity less likely.
- **RSPCA:** small parrots such as cockatiels need some seeds each day, but no more than a tenth of the diet, plus free access to pellets, vegetables and fruit.
- **Leipzig bird clinic:** 70% seeds for large parakeets and 30% vegetables, greens and fruit, with grit and a vitamin and mineral supplement. Millet sprays only once a week, and no honey sticks at all.

Offer fresh water every day (Leipzig). Never give **avocado**: the RSPCA calls it highly poisonous to parrots. The Leipzig clinic also rules out alcohol, tobacco, salty or spiced food, caffeine, sugary or fatty foods such as chocolate, peanuts and raw cabbage, and warns that cockatiels often become overweight, with liver, heart and circulation, and musculoskeletal problems, when fed too much or too much energy-rich food.

## What temperature do cockatiels need?

The Leipzig bird clinic gives 18–25 °C; in an outdoor aviary, the frost-free shelter should not stay below 10 °C in winter.

The MSD Veterinary Manual says birds usually do well at temperatures that are comfortable for people. Keep the cage away from windows, air conditioning and the kitchen, where smoke or cooking fumes can harm birds (MSD). The MSD Veterinary Manual for vets says poisoning by PTFE gas, released when certain non-stick pans are overheated, is common in pet birds kept in or near kitchens, and that often the only sign is sudden death. It also lists avocado, cleaners, sprays and many household products as poisons for birds.

## How do I know my cockatiel is healthy?

A healthy bird is bright and alert with clear eyes, active and not huddled or fluffed up, says the MSD Veterinary Manual; because birds hide illness, any change from normal is a reason to call the vet.

The MSD Veterinary Manual lists these signs of illness:

- fluffed-up feathers, sleeping more than usual with closed eyes (see [bird fluffed up and sleepy](/animals/budgie-fluffed-up))
- not being active, talking or singing less
- sitting low on the perch or at the bottom of the cage, weakness or losing balance
- wheezing or tail bobbing while breathing
- changes in droppings (see [bird droppings and diarrhoea](/animals/budgie-diarrhoea))
- eating or drinking more or less than usual, or throwing up food

Plucked feathers need a vet: see [bird feather plucking](/animals/bird-feather-plucking). The Leipzig clinic recommends checking your birds daily and weighing them weekly if you can.

## When should a cockatiel see a vet?

Go now if your cockatiel is huddled, weak, unresponsive or lying on the cage floor, which the MSD Veterinary Manual calls an emergency; otherwise the Leipzig clinic recommends a check-up at least once a year.

Go to a vet who treats birds now, and call ahead, if your cockatiel:

- is huddled, weak, unresponsive or lying on the cage floor (MSD)
- is bleeding and the bleeding doesn't stop (MSD)
- has difficulty breathing, such as wheezing or tail bobbing with each breath (MSD)
- is losing its balance (MSD)
- has breathed fumes from an overheated non-stick pan, smoke or sprays: birds' airways are very sensitive to fumes (MSD)
- is a hen that strains or sits on the floor with a swollen belly: see [egg binding](/animals/budgie-egg-bound)

On the way, think warmth, darkness and a secure carrier, with at least three sides covered by a towel (MSD).

Have a new cockatiel examined by a specialised bird vet, and keep it in quarantine for several weeks (Leipzig). Blood tests at check-ups can find diseases before your bird shows serious signs (MSD).
