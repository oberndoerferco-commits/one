## How long do betta fish live?

Usually 3 to 5 years when kept in good conditions, according to the care sheet of Unusual Pet Vets, an Australian group of veterinary clinics.

## Can two betta fish live together?

Two males, never: RSPCA Australia says two male bettas must not share a tank, because one will die. Unusual Pet Vets explains that the name comes from the males' aggression towards each other, and the University of Florida says males must be separated into their own containers from 3 to 4 months of age to avoid injury.

- **Females** are often more peaceful with each other and with other calm species, but can occasionally be aggressive (RSPCA Australia).
- **Males and females** should only be put together for breeding (RSPCA Australia).
- **Other fish.** RSPCA Australia advises beginners not to keep a male with other fish; snails or large shrimps can be suitable. A community tank with small peaceful fish needs at least 40 litres, and RSPCA Australia advises having a spare tank ready.

## What temperature does a betta need?

Warm water, 24–26 °C, kept steady with a submersible aquarium heater: RSPCA Australia says room temperature is usually too cold and fluctuates too much, and lamps are not a reliable heat source. The MSD Veterinary Manual says a heater is necessary for tropical freshwater fish.

The University of Florida says bettas do best in warm water and poorly in cold water; in one studied wild habitat in Thailand the water averaged 29.9 °C. Unusual Pet Vets recommends around 26 °C and placing the tank away from windows and air conditioners.

## What size tank does a betta need? Can a betta live in a bowl?

A bowl is not enough. RSPCA Australia says small tanks and bowls don't give a betta enough space for its physical and mental needs, and are too small for a suitable heater; it recommends 20 litres or more, with 10 litres as the absolute minimum, and a lid, because bettas are excellent jumpers.

- **Unusual Pet Vets** recommends 15–20 litres for a single betta.
- **Germany:** the veterinary animal-welfare association TVT points out that the German agriculture ministry's expert report on keeping ornamental fish names 54 litres as the minimum for keeping fish, and says no fish species *has* to be kept in a smaller tank.
- **A different view:** a University of Florida fact sheet, written largely from fish-farming experience, says males can thrive in small containers if water quality and nutrition are well managed. We follow the more cautious welfare advice above.
- **Gentle filter.** Use a filter sized for the tank with adjustable, low flow (RSPCA Australia); the University of Florida says bettas prefer calm water and that strong filtration may harm them.

## What do bettas eat?

Quality betta pellets or granules once a day, with occasional frozen foods such as brine shrimp or daphnia: RSPCA Australia advises feeding only what the fish eats in under two minutes. Avoid mammal fats such as beef heart or offal.

Overfeeding is common. Unusual Pet Vets says a betta's eye is roughly the size of its stomach, which is a useful guide to portion size.

## How do I keep a betta's water clean?

With a filter and regular partial water changes: RSPCA Australia recommends changing 10–25 % of the water every week, cleaning the gravel at the same time, and treating new tap water with a water conditioner to neutralise chlorine and chloramine. Rinse the filter in old tank water, never in tap water.

The MSD Veterinary Manual advises testing pH, ammonia, nitrite and nitrate with a test kit every two weeks.

## How do I know my betta is healthy?

A healthy betta has clear, bright colours and holds its fins erect (RSPCA Australia). Warning signs include sinking or bobbing at the surface, lumps, wounds, clamped fins, trailing droppings, staying in a corner for long periods, constant hiding, or lethargy.

Unusual Pet Vets lists loss of bright colour, uneven or thin fins, weight loss and not eating as common signs of illness. The RSPCA warns that the very heavy tails of "rose-tailed" bettas can cause health problems.

## When should a betta see a vet?

When it hides all the time, is lethargic or doesn't respond: RSPCA Australia says such a fish is likely stressed or ill and needs appropriate veterinary care, from a fish vet or with advice from an experienced aquarist.

Vets who treat fish do exist. Unusual Pet Vets says several of its vets see fish, and recommends health checks every 6–12 months. The University of Florida says a proper diagnosis with a fish vet is key, and warns that many drugs and chemicals can do more harm than good when used inappropriately. The RSPCA also advises a specialist fish vet for diagnosing and treating fish disease.

## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

**EU:** the sources we opened name no EU-wide rule just for keeping bettas; the national rules below apply.

**Italy:** according to ISPRA, the Italian environment agency, DPR 102/2019 confirms the ban on releasing non-native species and populations into the wild (article 12, paragraph 3 of DPR 357/97); exceptions need an authorisation from the environment ministry (MASE). Town rules can go further: Verona's animal-welfare regulation bans round or curved-walled tanks, sets a minimum of 30 litres, and bans giving animals away as prizes or gifts in public places and at fairs.

**Germany:** under § 40 of the Federal Nature Conservation Act (BNatSchG), releasing animals into the wild needs permission from the competent authority. The 54-litre figure above comes from the federal expert report that the TVT quotes; it is guidance, not a law.
