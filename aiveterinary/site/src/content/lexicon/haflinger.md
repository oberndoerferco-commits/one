## Where does the Haflinger come from?

From the mountains of South Tyrol. According to the Haflinger Horse Breeding Association Tyrol, the breed began in 1874 with the birth of the stallion 249 Folie, out of a Tyrolean mountain mare and by an Arab stallion; every Haflinger traces back to him through seven stallion lines. It was first a pack and draught horse on steep mountain farms and is now bred as an all-round leisure horse for riding and driving, found in more than 60 countries.

We found no published lifespan study for Haflingers.

## How big is a Haflinger, and what is it like?

The breed society's breeding goal gives a height of 140 to 155 cm. Every shade of chestnut, from light to dark, with a light mane and tail is wanted; head markings are desirable, leg markings are not. Because the Haflinger is bred as a leisure horse, the society says an excellent character and a balanced temperament matter most, and it lists calm, composure and being good with children among the breed's qualities. These are the breed society's aims; individual horses vary.

The society also praises the breed's "Leichtfuttrigkeit": Haflingers do well on little feed. So keep an eye on your horse's weight; to see why that matters, read about [laminitis in horses](/animals/horse-laminitis).

## What is the eye cancer risk?

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is the most common cancer of the horse's eye. It often starts at the edge of the cornea and can spread over the eye, harm vision and cost the horse the eye (Bellone et al., 2017). Haflingers are affected more often than other horses. Pedigree studies point to a recessive risk factor, and a variant in the DDB2 gene, which helps repair DNA damaged by ultraviolet light, was strongly associated with the cancer. The same variant raises the risk of SCC on the third eyelid: 88% of 25 affected Haflingers carried two copies (Singer-Berk et al., 2018).

A study in Switzerland and Austria found two copies in 6 of 9 Haflingers with eye SCC. Among 113 healthy Haflingers, 6% carried two copies and 37% one copy, which means the variant is common in the breed. Not every horse with two copies develops cancer, and not every case carries them, because other factors play a part. The authors recommend taking the DDB2 result of breeding horses into account so that high-risk matings are avoided (Schäfer et al., 2023).

For an owner, that means looking at your horse's eyes regularly, including the third eyelid in the inner corner, and showing any lump or sore on or around the eye to your vet early.

## What is PSSM1?

Polysaccharide storage myopathy type 1 is a muscle disease caused by a variant in the GYS1 gene; it occurs in many breeds, including Haflingers. In a group of 50 healthy Austrian Haflingers, 9 (18%) carried one copy and none carried two. Carriers had higher muscle enzymes in the blood after exercise (Schwarz et al., 2011). A later study measured muscle activity in Haflingers and Noriker horses with the variant but no signs of disease and found results suggesting fewer working muscle fibres (Zsoldos et al., 2021). If your horse seems to have muscle problems during or after work, ask your vet. For the needs of all horses, see our [horse fact sheet](/animals/horse).
