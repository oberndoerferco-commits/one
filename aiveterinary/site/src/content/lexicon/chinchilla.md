## How long do chinchillas live?

Chinchillas live about 10 to 15 years on average, and some pet chinchillas have reached 20, according to the MSD Veterinary Manual.

The RSPCA gives 10–20 years, and the German veterinary animal-welfare association TVT says chinchillas can live more than 20 years.

## Do chinchillas need a friend?

Yes. The RSPCA says chinchillas live in colonies of over 100 animals in the wild and therefore must not be kept singly as pets. The TVT calls keeping a chinchilla alone unacceptable: at least a pair, better a group.

The TVT recommends same-sex groups, or neutering the male in a mixed pair, and says chinchillas are best introduced to each other before they reach sexual maturity.

## Are chinchillas nocturnal?

Yes. Chinchillas sleep most of the day and are active and playful at night (MSD Veterinary Manual); the TVT describes them as active at dusk and at night.

Plan your time with them for the evening, and put the cage somewhere quiet: the TVT says chinchillas startle very easily and are sensitive to noise.

## What do chinchillas eat?

Mostly hay. The RSPCA says chinchillas need a high-fibre diet based on grasses and hay, and the TVT says good, dry, dust- and mould-free hay must always be freely available, with special chinchilla pellets only in small amounts.

The TVT is strict about the rest: chinchillas may only get high-fibre, low-energy and above all sugar-free food, because of the risk of diabetes. It advises against fruit, fresh vegetables, sunflower seeds, nuts and old bread, and against food made for other rodents or rabbits. It also warns that fresh or wilted greens often cause diarrhoea in chinchillas, which can be fatal. Dried rose hips or dried vegetables are suitable occasional treats. Any change of diet should be made very slowly. Fresh water must always be available.

## How big should a chinchilla cage be?

Big, and above all tall. The TVT recommends a gnaw-proof aviary with at least 3 m³ of space for two chinchillas, at least 50 cm wide and 150 cm high, and at least 0.5 m³ more for each extra adult.

- **Levels:** chinchillas are very active climbers and jumpers. The MSD Veterinary Manual advises large cages with several levels, ramps and places to climb; the TVT suggests shelves on at least three levels. The RSPCA says there should be no uninterrupted drop of more than 60 cm.
- **Floor:** the RSPCA advises solid flooring as well as mesh, because standing on wire for long periods can damage their feet.
- **Hiding places:** hay-filled nest boxes and hiding places at all times (RSPCA).
- **Exercise:** chinchillas need daily exercise to stay healthy (MSD Veterinary Manual), supervised and away from electrical cables (RSPCA).

## Why do chinchillas need dust baths?

Dust baths keep their fur clean and dry. The MSD Veterinary Manual advises letting your chinchilla roll in chinchilla dust for about 10 minutes every day, and not leaving the dust in the cage so long that it gets dirty with droppings and food.

The RSPCA recommends a shallow tray of clean fine sand or chinchilla dust for daily bathing, cleaned or sieved after use. The TVT also counts a sand bath with special sand as a basic requirement.

## Can chinchillas get wet?

Chinchillas should be kept dry: they clean their fur in dust, not water. The TVT explains that chinchilla skin has no sweat or sebaceous glands, so their fur does not protect them against moisture, which puts them at risk of colds.

The RSPCA warns that chinchillas can become ill in hot, wet, humid, draughty or dirty conditions. For the same reason the TVT rejects keeping chinchillas only outdoors, for example on a balcony.

## What temperature do chinchillas need?

Cool and dry. The RSPCA advises keeping chinchillas indoors in a dry, well-ventilated, draught-free room, ideally at 10–18 °C. The TVT gives 15–21 °C as the optimal temperature, says temperatures above 28 °C are harmful, and sets a limit of 55% relative humidity.

Chinchillas come from cold, dry, windy mountains and are adapted to that climate (RSPCA, TVT); their fur gives no protection against high temperatures, so they overheat easily (TVT). Keep the cage out of direct sun and away from radiators (TVT).

## How do I handle a chinchilla?

Gently, never by the fur or tail. The TVT explains that when a chinchilla is grabbed by the back, it reflexively sheds tufts of fur, and that holding it by the tail can make the skin of the tail come off.

Chinchillas handled often while young can become tame and form a bond with you; if they are not handled regularly when young, they can become nervous and fearful (MSD Veterinary Manual). They rarely bite, and generally only when stressed. The TVT says patient, never forced contact can make them hand-tame.

## How do I know my chinchilla is healthy?

Check your chinchillas every day, in the evening or at night when they are active (RSPCA). Chinchillas aren't good at showing pain and may suffer a lot before you notice, the RSPCA says, so small changes matter.

- **Eating and droppings:** the TVT advises checking the droppings every day for smell, colour and consistency; any change can mean a digestive problem.
- **Body:** the RSPCA lists sore feet, wetness around the eyes or mouth and fur loss as signs of illness or injury.
- **Teeth:** chinchilla teeth grow constantly, and chewing lots of abrasive hay helps wear them down (RSPCA).
- **Check-ups:** the RSPCA suggests a vet check-up once a year.

## When should a chinchilla see a vet?

Promptly if your chinchilla eats or drinks differently, passes fewer droppings, or is quieter than usual: the RSPCA says such a chinchilla could be seriously ill and is very likely to be ill or in pain.

Contact a vet immediately if you think your chinchilla is in pain, ill or injured (RSPCA). Signs to act on include diarrhoea or changed droppings, which the TVT says point to a digestive problem, and wetness around the eyes or mouth, sore feet or fur loss (RSPCA).

## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

**EU:** there are no harmonised EU travel rules for chinchillas. The European Commission's pet travel FAQ says the EU pet passport is only issued for dogs, cats and ferrets. Since 22 April 2026 the EU rules for travelling with pets come from Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/131; its detailed rules are for dogs, cats and ferrets and for pet birds entering from outside the EU, and it does not mention rodents. The Animal Health Law, Regulation (EU) 2016/429, lists "rodents and rabbits other than those intended for food production" in Part B of its Annex I, and for Part B species the Commission says there are no harmonised EU rules: check with the EU country you are travelling to for its national rules. The previous rules, Regulation (EU) No 576/2013, applied until 21 April 2026.

**Germany:** the TVT housing figures above are recommendations by a veterinary association (dated September 2012), not a statutory minimum.

**Italy:** the sources we opened set no Italian minimum cage size for chinchillas; your vet can tell you about local rules.
