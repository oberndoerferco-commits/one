## How long do rabbits live?

A rabbit can typically live for 8 to 12 years when it is kept in a safe, stimulating home, according to the RSPCA. That makes a rabbit a commitment of a decade or more, not a short-term pet.

A long life depends on the basics on this page: company, space, the right food and regular vet care.

## Do rabbits need company?

Yes. Rabbits are highly social and need the company of other rabbits; the Rabbit Welfare Association & Fund (RWAF) says rabbits should never be alone, and the RSPCA says they must not live on their own unless a vet advises it.

- **Another rabbit, not another species.** Human company does not replace a rabbit partner. The RWAF recommends strongly against keeping a rabbit with a guinea pig.
- **Neutered pairs.** To live in pairs, rabbits must be neutered, says the RWAF. The RSPCA suggests a neutered male and a neutered female as a good combination, and notes that neutering reduces fighting in both sexes.
- **Introductions take care.** Bonding two rabbits is a process; your vet or a rescue centre can advise.

## What do rabbits eat?

Mostly hay or grass. The RWAF recommends about 85% hay or grass, 10% leafy green vegetables and 5% pellets, with unlimited good-quality hay as the foundation of a healthy diet.

- **Hay, every day.** The RSPCA advises a bundle of good-quality hay at least as big as your rabbit, every day. Chewing hay helps wear down teeth that never stop growing.
- **Leafy greens** daily, in safe amounts: a large handful of washed greens, herbs and weeds, says the RSPCA.
- **Pellets only in small amounts.** The RWAF says they should make up a very small part of the diet. The RSPCA does not recommend muesli-style foods, because rabbits pick out the bits they like.
- **Fresh, clean water** must be available all day.
- **No sudden changes.** The RSPCA warns that sudden diet changes can upset a rabbit's digestion and make it ill.

Some plants and foods are dangerous: see [plants and foods that are dangerous for rabbits and guinea pigs](/animals/rabbit-guinea-pig-toxic-plants).

## How much space does a rabbit need?

The RWAF recommends a single enclosed area of at least 3 m × 2 m and 1 m high for two average-sized rabbits, available at all times, not just for limited periods. A single rabbit needs the same space as two.

- **A hutch is not enough.** The RWAF says a hutch should never be the sole or main accommodation; the sleeping area within the enclosure should be at least 1.8 m × 0.6 m × 0.6 m.
- **Room to behave like a rabbit.** The RSPCA says rabbits should be able to hop, run, jump, dig, stretch out fully when lying down, and stand on their hind legs without their ears touching the roof.
- **Hiding places.** Rabbits are a prey species. The RSPCA suggests as many hiding places as there are rabbits, plus one.
- **Indoors or outdoors,** the same needs apply. Rabbits are most active in the early morning, late afternoon and overnight (RSPCA), so they need access to their space around the clock.

## Can rabbits cope with heat and cold?

Rabbits are vulnerable to heat. The RSPCA gives an ideal range of 10–20 °C and advises plenty of shade as temperatures head towards 20 °C or when it is sunny.

The RWAF puts it more starkly: the risk rises above 26 °C, and from 28 °C the risk of heatstroke is high. Signs of heatstroke include panting or rapid breathing, weakness or confusion, a wet nose or mouth, and lying stretched out and unresponsive. Move the rabbit somewhere cool and call a rabbit-savvy vet straight away: the RWAF warns that heatstroke can be fatal quickly. Domestic rabbits are also more sensitive to cold than wild rabbits, says the RSPCA, so outdoor housing must work in all weathers.

## How should I pick up a rabbit?

Carefully, and only when needed. The RSPCA says never to pick a rabbit up by its ears or scruff, and warns that a rabbit's fragile spine can be seriously, even fatally, damaged if it feels insecure and struggles while held.

Not all rabbits like being held, as the MSD Veterinary Manual notes.

## How do I know my rabbit is healthy?

Watch your rabbits every day: eating, droppings, behaviour and weight. The RSPCA notes that a change in weight is often the first sign of illness.

- **Daily:** appetite, normal round droppings, normal activity.
- **Twice a day in warm weather:** check the fur and skin around the bottom and tail. Soiled fur attracts flies, and the RSPCA warns that flystrike is often fatal.
- **At least weekly:** front teeth and nails, which grow quickly (RSPCA).
- **At least once a year:** a vet check-up, vaccinations and parasite treatment (RSPCA).

Rabbits need vaccination against myxomatosis and both types of rabbit haemorrhagic disease (RHD and RHD2); your vet will advise how often (RSPCA). The RSPCA strongly encourages neutering, and the MSD Veterinary Manual explains that spaying removes the risk of cancer of the uterus, which is common and often fatal in rabbits over 3 years old.

## When should a rabbit see a vet?

Straight away if your rabbit stops eating or passes fewer droppings: the RSPCA says it could be seriously ill, and gut stasis can rapidly become life-threatening.

Contact a vet immediately if your rabbit:

- is eating less or nothing, or is passing fewer, smaller or no droppings: see [my rabbit is not eating](/animals/rabbit-not-eating)
- is sleepy, lethargic or limp (RSPCA)
- has fly eggs or maggots around its bottom (flystrike)
- shows signs of heatstroke in hot weather (RWAF)
- may have eaten something poisonous: the RSPCA says never watch and wait

Book a vet visit if the teeth look overgrown or misaligned: only a vet should correct them (RSPCA). Our articles on [overgrown teeth](/animals/rabbit-overgrown-teeth) and [rabbit diarrhoea](/animals/rabbit-diarrhoea) explain those problems in more detail.

Not every practice is used to rabbits. Find a rabbit-savvy vet before you need one.

## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

Rabbits do not get an EU pet passport: the European Commission says it is only issued to pet dogs, cats and ferrets whose owner lives in the EU.

Pet rabbits are one of the species listed in Part B of Annex I to Regulation (EU) 2016/429, for which there are no harmonised EU rules (European Commission). Before you travel with a rabbit, to Italy, Germany or any other EU country, check the destination country's national rules.
