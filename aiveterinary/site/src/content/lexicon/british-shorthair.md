## How long do British Shorthairs live?

The figures differ a lot by source. The GCCF, the UK's main cat registry, calls the British Shorthair a long-lived cat with a life expectancy of 14 to 20 years. UK vet records give lower numbers: VetCompass life tables for cats that died in 2019 put the life expectancy at birth of British cats (a group that also includes British Blue and British Longhair) at 9.58 years, against 11.89 years for crossbred cats (Teng et al.). An earlier VetCompass study of 69 British Shorthairs found a median age at death of 11.8 years and listed the breed among those with reduced longevity (O'Neill et al.).

## How big does a British Shorthair get?

The GCCF gives 4.1–7.7 kg (9–17 lb) for males and 3.2–5.4 kg (7–12 lb) for females. Males are clearly larger than females and mature males develop prominent jowls. The breed is slow to mature and takes up to five years to reach full physical maturity.

## What health problems do British Shorthairs have?

- **Weight gain:** the GCCF says the British has a slow metabolism, so take care that a well-padded, muscular body does not turn to fat.
- **Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM):** a heart disease in which the heart muscle thickens; in time it can lead to heart failure or blood clots. The GCCF says it can be a problem in the breed and that there is no DNA test for it in British cats yet. The UFAW genetic welfare database says British Shorthairs are predisposed, but knows of no data on how many are affected; many affected cats show signs by two or three years of age.
- **Polycystic kidney disease (PKD):** once a problem in the breed, now under control thanks to DNA tests, according to the GCCF. Under the GCCF's British Shorthair policy, kittens can only be registered for breeding if both parents have been PKD-tested (vet-verified tests since 2025), or if testing on every line of their pedigree shows they are clear.
- **Haemophilia B:** a rare inherited disorder in which blood does not clot properly, reported in British Shorthairs; males are affected more often than females (UFAW). Signs usually appear before 6 months of age, for example prolonged bleeding after vaccination or neutering.

Since 1 March 2025, British Shorthairs imported onto the GCCF breeding register also need a valid negative ALPS test (GCCF).

## Is a British Shorthair right for me?

The GCCF describes the British as patient and tolerant, a bit lazy when mature, and not a talkative breed. It may not like being carried around as an adult. It is suitable as an indoor-only cat if it gets enough stimulation; if it goes outside, the GCCF advises supervision, as its trusting nature can leave it vulnerable. Grooming is easy: the short, dense coat needs brushing during moulting, the British Longhair daily. Feed kitten food for at least the first year, and keep an eye on its weight.
