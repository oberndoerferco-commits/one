## What is an Arabian horse?

One of the oldest recognised horse breeds, developed by Bedouin tribes on the Arabian Peninsula (Brooks et al., 2010). The Arab Horse Society of Great Britain says Arabian bloodlines are found in almost every breed of riding horse, and that the modern Thoroughbred descends from three Arabian horses imported into England in the 18th century. It describes the breed's beauty, intelligence, athleticism, versatility and kindness. That is how the breed society sees it; individual horses vary.

We found no published lifespan study for Arabians.

## Which inherited diseases affect Arabians?

The Arabian Horse Society of Australia names four conditions specific to the breed that usually end in death or euthanasia: severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID), cerebellar abiotrophy (CA), lavender foal syndrome (LFS) and occipital atlanto-axial malformation (OAAM). The first three are recessive: a foal is only affected if it inherits the variant from both parents. Horses with one copy appear normal (Bernoco and Bailey, 1998).

**SCID.** Affected foals lack working B and T cells, the white blood cells that build immune responses, so they cannot fight infections (Ayad et al., 2021). In 1998, 8.4% of 250 randomly chosen US Arabians were carriers; with random mating that would mean about 1 affected foal in 567 (Bernoco and Bailey, 1998).

**Cerebellar abiotrophy (CA).** Nerve cells (Purkinje cells) in the cerebellum, at the back of the brain, die off. Foals become wobbly, lift their forelegs too high, have head tremors and do not blink when a hand moves towards the eye. Signs appear between a few days and about six months of age. CA occurs almost only in Arabians, and about 20% of nearly 11,000 horses screened by the UC Davis laboratory carried the variant (Scott et al., 2017).

**Lavender foal syndrome (LFS).** A seizure disorder that is fatal soon after birth. Affected foals have seizures, arch their neck and back, paddle their legs and cannot stand or suckle normally (Brooks et al., 2010). The coat colour is diluted, and most die within hours or days (Ayad et al., 2021). Most cases have been in Egyptian Arabians, where about 10.3% of horses were carriers (Brooks et al., 2010).

## How common are carriers?

It varies a lot between countries:

- **South Africa:** among purebred foals born in 2009/10, 11.7% carried LFS and 5.1% CA (Tarr et al., 2014).
- **Poland:** 808 elite Arabians were all free of LFS; three carried SCID, the first reported in Poland (Bugno-Poniewierska et al., 2019).
- **Middle East and North Africa:** 5.7% of 177 Arabian, Barb and Arab-Barb horses carried SCID (Ayad et al., 2021).

## Does testing help?

Yes. In South Africa, SCID carriers fell from 6.4% of foals born in 2004/5 to 3.4% of foals born in 2009/10 after a DNA test came into use (Tarr et al., 2014). The Arabian Horse Society of Australia requires stallions at stud to be tested, and a mare must have SCID, LFS and CA results on file before her foals can be registered. It points out that carriers can safely be bred to clear horses, so breeders can keep good lines without producing affected foals.

## What does this mean for an owner?

If you plan to breed, ask for the DNA results of both parents. A foal that is weak, keeps getting infections, has seizures or is wobbly needs a vet straight away.
