## Why do Scottish Folds have folded ears?

Because of a gene that disturbs how cartilage develops. The UFAW genetic welfare database explains that the ear cartilage cannot support the weight of the ear, so it flops forward from about three to four weeks of age. The same defect, called osteochondrodysplasia, affects cartilage throughout the body, including the growth plates and joints of the limbs. International Cat Care puts it simply: the genetic defect that folds the ears also causes severe abnormalities of the bones and painful arthritis.

The breed goes back to a white farm cat with folded ears found in Scotland in 1961 (CFA). The GCCF, the UK's main cat registry, recognised it for a few years but had excluded it by 1974 because of crippling deformities of the limbs and tail, and FIFe, an international cat federation, bans it too (UFAW). The CFA in the United States still recognises the breed.

## Is every Scottish Fold affected?

Yes. According to UFAW, all cats with folded ears develop osteochondrodysplasia to some extent; there are no healthy carriers. Cats with two copies of the gene are severely affected: bone changes show on x-rays from seven weeks of age, and crippling, painful arthritis develops early in life. Cats with one copy were once thought to be spared, but they are affected too, to varying degrees; some show significant disease from six months of age and some have to be euthanased for welfare reasons. UFAW says straight-eared kittens from a mating of a fold with a straight-eared cat do not have the gene or the disease.

Signs include short, wide limbs, a short, thick tail that is stiff at the base, swollen wrists and ankles, lameness, an abnormal, stiff gait and reluctance to move or jump. International Cat Care adds that affected cats may not curl up comfortably and may have accidents outside the litter tray if climbing in hurts.

## Can it be treated?

There is no cure. A vet can help manage the pain, and some cats are helped by surgery (UFAW). At home, International Cat Care suggests low beds or beds with steps, raised food and water bowls, a low-sided litter tray, ramps to favourite spots and rugs on slippery floors. UFAW says that stopping breeding from any cat with folded ears would end the disease within a generation. International Cat Care considers continued breeding unethical, because the only reason for it is that some people like the look.

## What is a Scottish Fold like?

The CFA, a US registry that recognises the breed, describes a medium-sized, rounded cat with a dense, plush coat (there is also a long-haired variety) that needs weekly combing. It calls them comfortable homebodies, quiet but not placid, with even temperaments, and says they do not do well when left alone for long. There is no reliable lifespan figure for the breed: the UK VetCompass life tables analysed only the 12 breeds with 15 or more deaths, and the Scottish Fold was not among them (Teng et al., 2024).

## Rules in Germany

§ 11b of the German Animal Welfare Act bans breeding vertebrates when it is to be expected that the offspring will suffer pain, suffering or harm because inherited body parts or organs are missing, unsuitable or altered. The law itself names no breeds. An expert report on how to interpret § 11b, written for the federal agriculture ministry in 1999, lists the Scottish Fold among breeds with folded ears caused by the Fd gene, notes cartilage and bone damage in affected cats (and that some cats with one copy have normal ears but can still pass the gene on), and recommends a breeding ban for cats with Fd-gene folded ears. The report was written as a guideline for breeders and the authorities; it is not law.
