## How long do Lionheads live?

There is no study of the breed on its own. In UK vet records from 2013, rabbits of all breeds that died had a median age at death of 4.3 years (370 rabbits; O'Neill et al., 2020). For rabbits in general, see our [rabbit fact sheet](/animals/rabbit).

## How big does a Lionhead get?

Small. In the same records, 361 Lionheads (5.7% of the rabbits seen) had a median adult weight of 2.1 kg, the same as the median for all rabbits (O'Neill et al., 2020).

## How much grooming does a Lionhead need?

A lot. The RSPCA lists Lionheads, bred for their long fur or "mane", among the rabbits that commonly have inherited health issues or extreme features. The US rabbit charity Rabbit.org warns that the long fur around the head and back easily mats, tangles and traps moisture. Mats pull painfully at the skin, hide sores and make it almost impossible for the rabbit to groom itself. It advises:

- brushing every day to stop mats forming and to remove loose fur before the rabbit swallows it
- checking the chin, tail and flanks for hidden tangles or dirt
- never bathing the rabbit; spot-clean the fur gently with a damp cloth if needed

Neglected grooming, it says, can lead to flystrike, digestive blockages and skin infections.

## Why is flystrike such a risk?

Flies lay eggs on soiled or damp fur, and the maggots hatch within hours. The Rabbit Welfare Association & Fund (RWAF) says to check your rabbit's bottom every day, more than once a day in summer, and to phone your vet immediately if you find maggots: flystrike is a true emergency, day or night. Read more in [flystrike in rabbits](/animals/rabbit-flystrike).

## Is the Lionhead a short-faced breed?

The RWAF counts the Lionhead among the brachycephalic (short-faced) breeds. It says a flattened head crowds the back teeth, can push the front teeth out of line and makes the tear duct twisted and easier to block. In UK vet records from 2019, short-faced rabbits had higher odds of a dirty bottom, tear duct problems and coat disorders, but lower odds of obesity, not eating and gut stasis, than medium-faced rabbits (O'Neill et al., 2024). In numbers, short-headed rabbits had 2.6 times the risk of a dirty bottom and 3.5 times the risk of tear duct disease.

For teeth the picture is mixed: in a large study of 161,979 rabbits, a short face was not linked with higher odds of dental disease, which affected 15.36% of all rabbits (Jackson et al., 2024). See [overgrown teeth in rabbits](/animals/rabbit-overgrown-teeth).

## Is a Lionhead right for me?

Only if you can groom every day. Rabbit.org notes that many Lionheads end up in rescues because buyers did not expect the work. The RWAF says lots of Lionheads are waiting for homes and that adopting one is a good idea, as long as you know what to look for and see a rabbit-savvy vet. A Lionhead that eats less or stops passing droppings needs a vet straight away: see [my rabbit isn't eating](/animals/rabbit-not-eating).
