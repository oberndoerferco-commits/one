## How long do Hermann's tortoises live?

A Hermann's tortoise can live for decades: the RSPCA gives a potential longevity of 50–100 years for Hermann's and the related Mediterranean tortoises. Plan for a tortoise that may outlive you.

The RSPCA points out that this long life makes tortoises vulnerable if they end up living longer than their owner, and that research found most pet tortoises of the commonly kept species die prematurely, often from a poor diet lacking calcium and vitamin D3, or from mistakes around hibernation. In the wild, Hermann's tortoises live from the Balkan Peninsula through Italy to Iberia (RSPCA).

## Should my tortoise hibernate?

Hermann's tortoise is a hibernating species, but only a tortoise in excellent health should hibernate. If you have any doubt, the Tortoise Protection Group says not to risk it and to overwinter the tortoise awake instead.

Late October is when this decision is made. The Bundestierärztekammer (German Federal Veterinary Chamber) notes that tortoises become sluggish and eat less as the days get shorter in October, and recommends a check-up at the vet first, including a test of the droppings for parasites. The RSPCA also says a vet should check that the tortoise is healthy and of a suitable size and weight to go without food in the cold.

What the sources say about a safe hibernation:

- **Who should not hibernate:** any tortoise that is underweight or ill (British Chelonia Group). Do not hibernate a tortoise that is less than 100% healthy (Tortoise Protection Group).
- **Preparation:** winding down takes several weeks (Tortoise Protection Group). The gut takes 4–6 weeks to empty, so feeding stops before hibernation (British Chelonia Group). The Bundestierärztekammer describes reducing light and temperature step by step over about three weeks and stopping food, while still offering drinking water and lukewarm baths.
- **Temperature:** 4–8 °C, with 5 °C as the ideal in a fridge (British Chelonia Group); a constant 4–6 °C (Bundestierärztekammer). Tortoises kept below freezing can lose their eyesight or their lives (British Chelonia Group). The Bundestierärztekammer calls a separate fridge the best method, because the temperature can be set exactly and checked with an electronic thermometer.
- **Weight checks:** the British Chelonia Group says an adult loses about 1% of its pre-hibernation weight a month (10 g a month for a 1000 g tortoise) and that a drastic weight loss or passing urine means something is wrong. The Bundestierärztekammer says to weigh and check the tortoise every 14 days and that it must not lose more than 10% of its body weight in total.
- **Length:** usually three to five months; for tortoises in their first year, a shortened hibernation of six to eight weeks is enough (Bundestierärztekammer).
- **Waking:** all tortoises should start eating within 1 week of waking up; if they do not, get veterinary help (Tortoise Protection Group).

A tortoise that does not hibernate needs a warm environment and the same care as the rest of the year (RSPCA). For indoor overwintering, the British Chelonia Group gives 26 °C by day and 18–22 °C at night, never below 15 °C, with full-spectrum light for 13–14 hours to prevent hibernation.

There is a step-by-step guide in our article on [tortoise hibernation](/animals/tortoise-hibernation).

## What do Hermann's tortoises eat?

Hermann's tortoises eat plants: a diet high in fibre, vitamins and minerals but low in fat and protein, built on weeds and flowers rather than shop vegetables.

- **Good foods:** dandelions, chickweed, clover, plantains, white dead-nettle and sow thistle (RSPCA).
- **Avoid:** peas and green beans (too much protein), foods high in oxalic acid such as cabbage, broccoli and spinach, and lettuce, which is mostly water (RSPCA). Dog and cat food is not natural for tortoises and can be harmful, especially in young ones (British Chelonia Group).
- **How often:** young tortoises daily, older ones every other day (RSPCA).
- **Calcium:** grated cuttlebone on the food plus a vitamin and mineral supplement (RSPCA). The RSPCA says to sprinkle the supplement daily; the British Chelonia Group says two or three times a week. Ask your vet which suits your tortoise.
- **Water:** always clean drinking water in a shallow bowl that is also used for bathing (RSPCA).
- Wash all plants, and beware of weed killers and slug pellets on wild food (RSPCA, British Chelonia Group).

## What housing and temperature does a Hermann's tortoise need?

A Hermann's tortoise needs both an outdoor pen and an indoor enclosure: the RSPCA gives at least 180 × 120 cm indoors and 10 m² outdoors for one tortoise, with 20–25 °C by day, 15 °C at night and a 35 °C basking spot.

- **Indoors (RSPCA):** for example in a shed, on a loam and soft sand mix at least 50 mm deep. Gravel, wood shavings, alfalfa pellets, calci-sand and cat litter are unsuitable. Each tortoise needs its own basking spot under a lamp with a reflector at one end.
- **Outdoors:** 10 m² for one tortoise, escape-proof, with a barrier at least 45 cm high and buried 15 cm deep (RSPCA). The British Chelonia Group asks for at least 10 m² per tortoise and solid walls at least 50 cm high, buried 10 cm deep. Add shrubs for shade, a waterproof shelter in a sunny spot and no pesticides or weed killers.
- **Company:** the RSPCA says these tortoises are naturally solitary and territorial and are best kept alone unless the enclosure is very large, although Hermann's are more social than other species. Males can butt and bite females. The British Chelonia Group suggests groups of no more than five, of one species, with males and females kept apart.

## Does my tortoise need UVB light?

Yes. Tortoises need UVB to make vitamin D, so indoors they need a UVB lamp: the RSPCA says less than 30 cm from the tortoise.

- Glass filters out the sun's ultraviolet rays, so sunlight through a window or greenhouse glass is not enough (British Chelonia Group).
- Outside summer, the British Chelonia Group says supplementary UVB is needed, on every day to mimic the natural day length.
- UVB output falls with time and distance, so replace lamps frequently, even if they still seem to work (British Chelonia Group).
- The RSPCA suggests 12 hours of light in winter, 13 in spring and autumn and 14 in summer.

Too little UVB and calcium is a cause of soft or misshapen shells; see [metabolic bone disease in reptiles](/animals/reptile-metabolic-bone-disease).

## How do I know my tortoise is healthy?

A healthy tortoise has a hard, smooth shell, is alert with bright, shiny eyes and breathes easily, according to the RSPCA.

The RSPCA lists these signs of health:

- a hard shell with no deformities, weakness or damage, and a lower shell (plastron) that does not drag on the ground
- alert, with bright shiny eyes; it withdraws its head and legs if disturbed, unless used to handling
- skin free of ticks and mites, sores and fungal patches, and not hanging loosely in folds around the legs and neck (a sign of too much vitamin A)
- easy breathing, with no wheezing, nasal discharge or mucus around the mouth

Blood in the droppings may point to bladder stones, which can result from a diet too high in protein, and droppings should also be tested for parasites (RSPCA).

## When should a tortoise see a vet?

Take a tortoise that is not feeding or seems ill to a vet without delay, says the British Chelonia Group. A tortoise that has not started eating within a week of waking from hibernation needs veterinary help (Tortoise Protection Group).

See a vet with reptile experience if your tortoise:

- has stopped eating; the British Chelonia Group says to seek advice within a week at the latest, and sooner if it seems ill
- has a runny nose, wheezes, or has mucus around the mouth, or signs of mouth rot (runny nose, mouth rot and parasites are infectious and need isolation, British Chelonia Group)
- loses a lot of weight or passes urine during hibernation (British Chelonia Group)
- has a soft, misshapen or damaged shell (a healthy shell is hard, with no deformities, weakness or damage, RSPCA)
- has blood in its droppings (RSPCA)

Also plan a vet check before every hibernation (Bundestierärztekammer, RSPCA). More help: [my reptile is not eating](/animals/reptile-not-eating).

## Salmonella: is a tortoise safe with children?

Yes, with simple hygiene. Reptiles can carry salmonella even when they look healthy (CDC), so wash your hands before and after handling your tortoise or cleaning its enclosure (RSPCA).

- Wash with soap and running water after touching the tortoise, its droppings or its equipment (CDC).
- Keep the tortoise and its equipment out of the kitchen and away from food (CDC).
- Don't kiss the tortoise or hold it close to your face (CDC).
- Children under 5, adults over 65 and people with a weakened immune system are more likely to get ill (CDC). Germany's Robert Koch Institute advises not keeping reptiles in households with children under 2.

To pick up a tortoise, hold it at both sides of the shell and spread your fingers underneath to support the body, so it cannot fall (RSPCA).

## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

Hermann's tortoise is listed in Annex A of the EU wildlife trade regulation, so buying, selling and other commercial use are only allowed with a certificate from the authority. Germany and Italy add duties to report animals to the authorities.

**EU.** Testudo hermanni is listed in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97 (current annex: Regulation (EU) 2023/966). Article 8 of that regulation bans buying, offering to buy, acquiring for commercial purposes, displaying to the public for commercial purposes, selling and keeping, offering or transporting for sale. The authority of the Member State can grant an exemption case by case with a certificate, for example for captive-born and bred animals. Member States may also restrict keeping Annex A animals.

**Germany.** The Bundesamt für Naturschutz says the federal states' authorities handle possession, marketing and registration of protected live animals, including the "Anmeldung geschützter lebender Tiere". The Lower Saxony state agency NLWKN explains in its guide for keepers:

- Annex A animals may only be bought or sold with a valid marketing permit (Vermarktungsgenehmigung) from the authority.
- Vertebrates of specially protected species must be reported to the competent authority without delay once you start keeping them; changes such as breeding, buying, selling or death must be reported too. Species in Annex 5 of the Federal Species Protection Ordinance (BArtSchV) are exempt from this.
- Reptiles are marked by photo documentation, or optionally by transponder (tortoises from 500 g body weight), and the photo documentation of tortoises must be updated regularly.

Ask your local nature conservation authority which office is responsible where you live.

**Italy.** The Carabinieri CITES service provides the forms for Annex A animals: a birth in captivity must be reported within 10 days (form SCT1/A, art. 8-bis Law 150/92), a death is reported on form SCT5, a temporary change of where the animal is kept (for example for urgent vet treatment) on form SCT4, and a certificate for live Annex A animals is requested on form SCT2. Forms go to the territorial CITES service for your area.
