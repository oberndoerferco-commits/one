## How long do grey parrots live?

On average 30 to 50 years, according to the MSD Veterinary Manual, so a grey parrot is a commitment for decades.

There are two kinds of African grey (LafeberVet): the larger and more popular Congo grey from central Africa, with a black beak, a silver-grey body and a red tail, and the smaller Timneh grey from Ivory Coast and Sierra Leone, with a horn-coloured beak, a dark grey body and a maroon tail. The MSD Veterinary Manual calls African greys medium-sized to large and the most intelligent of the parrots, which is why they often have behaviour problems and need a lot of time and attention.

## Should grey parrots be kept in pairs?

Yes. The Leipzig University bird clinic says a pair, ideally a sexually mature male and female, is the acceptable minimum and a group the ideal; keeping a grey parrot alone for good is not appropriate.

- In the wild, greys live in small groups that often gather in larger flocks to roost (Leipzig).
- The Leipzig clinic names behaviour problems from keeping a grey alone as one of the most common problems it sees.
- The RSPCA says large, non-colony-breeding parrots such as African greys can live together only as a single pair: in separate cages in the same room or in a large aviary, but not both in one cage, as they will probably fight. A home aviary is unlikely to be big enough for more than one pair (RSPCA).
- In Germany, the federal expert opinion on keeping parrots (BMEL, 1995) says parrots live in pairs or groups and should in principle be kept that way. Where keeping a bird alone is justified, its need for social contact must be met by enough daily occupation with the bird.

## How big should a grey parrot's aviary be?

At least 3 × 1 × 2 m for one pair, with 50% more floor area for each further pair, according to the Leipzig bird clinic, and that does not replace daily free flight.

- The RSPCA bases cage size on wingspan: if your bird spends most of its time in the cage, height, width and depth should each be at least twice its wingspan.
- The RSPCA asks for at least six hours a day out of the cage, with room to fly freely and safely, and says African greys need UVB lighting from lamps made for birds.
- Use unzinced wire, natural branches of different thickness, a bath and, outdoors, a frost-free and draught-free shelter (Leipzig).
- The German expert opinion says parrots must not be chained or kept on a perch stand, and cages should stand at least 80 cm off the ground (BMEL, 1995).
- Be careful with toys and accessories with chains, rings, snap hooks or loose fibres, which can injure birds or be swallowed (Leipzig).

## What do grey parrots eat?

The RSPCA recommends about three-quarters pellets made for parrots and about one quarter washed fruit and vegetables, with seeds and nuts only in very small amounts as treats; the Leipzig bird clinic suggests 60% low-fat seeds and 40% vegetables, greens and fruit.

- The RSPCA warns that seed mixes let parrots pick out the fattiest seeds, and that purely seed-based diets can lead to obesity, fatty liver, and vitamin A and calcium deficiency.
- The Leipzig clinic says greys fed mostly seeds are prone to problems such as vitamin A deficiency and hardening of the blood vessels.
- Fresh water every day (Leipzig).
- Never give **avocado**: the RSPCA calls it highly poisonous to parrots. The Leipzig clinic also rules out alcohol, tobacco, salty or spiced food, caffeine, sugary or fatty foods such as chocolate, peanuts and raw cabbage.

The sources disagree on the share of seeds, so ask your bird vet.

## What temperature and humidity do grey parrots need?

The Leipzig bird clinic gives 15–35 °C and constantly high humidity, which should not fall below 60%.

The Leipzig clinic links low humidity, along with feeding nuts in their shells, to fungal infections of the airways, which it sees very often in greys. Keep the cage away from radiators, fires, stoves and draughts, and don't let your bird fly into the kitchen (RSPCA). The MSD Veterinary Manual for vets says poisoning by PTFE gas from overheated non-stick pans is common in pet birds kept in or near kitchens, and that often the only sign is sudden death.

## How do I keep a grey parrot busy?

Every day, with changing things to chew, climb on and forage for: the Leipzig clinic says a changing and wide range of occupation materials, such as branches, wood, cardboard and hidden food, is essential to prevent boredom and the behaviour disorders it can lead to.

- LafeberVet says feather-damaging behaviour is a common problem in grey parrots, especially when they get too little attention or their routine changes abruptly.
- The MSD Veterinary Manual says birds that are lonely or under-stimulated can start biting, screaming or pulling out their feathers. See [bird feather plucking](/animals/bird-feather-plucking).
- Greys are excellent mimics that potentially know the most words, but don't like to talk in front of strangers; they don't scream, but they do growl (MSD).
- Without special effort, greys can become one-person birds (LafeberVet).

## How do I know my grey parrot is healthy?

A healthy bird is bright and alert with clear eyes, active and not huddled or fluffed up; because birds hide illness, the MSD Veterinary Manual says any change in how your bird acts or looks means a vet visit right away.

The MSD Veterinary Manual lists these signs of illness:

- fluffed-up feathers, sleeping more than usual with closed eyes (see [bird fluffed up and sleepy](/animals/budgie-fluffed-up))
- not being active, talking less
- sitting low on the perch or at the bottom of the cage, weakness or losing balance
- wheezing or tail bobbing while breathing
- changes in droppings (see [bird droppings and diarrhoea](/animals/budgie-diarrhoea))
- eating or drinking more or less than usual, or throwing up food

The Leipzig clinic advises checking your birds daily and weighing them weekly if you can.

## When should a grey parrot see a vet?

Go now if your parrot is huddled, weak, unresponsive or lying on the cage floor, which the MSD Veterinary Manual calls an emergency; otherwise the Leipzig clinic recommends a check-up at least once a year.

Go to a vet who treats birds now, and call ahead, if your grey:

- is huddled, weak, unresponsive or lying on the cage floor (MSD)
- is bleeding and the bleeding doesn't stop (MSD)
- has difficulty breathing, such as wheezing or tail bobbing with each breath (MSD)
- is losing its balance (MSD)
- has breathed fumes from an overheated non-stick pan, smoke or sprays: birds' airways are very sensitive to fumes (MSD)
- is a hen that strains or sits on the floor with a swollen belly: see [egg binding](/animals/budgie-egg-bound)

On the way, think warmth, darkness and a secure carrier, with at least three sides covered by a towel (MSD). The Leipzig clinic recommends an examination by a specialised vet and a quarantine of several weeks for every new bird.

## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

Since 2017 the grey parrot is in Annex A of the EU wildlife trade regulation, the strictest level: buying, selling, offering for sale or using it commercially is banned unless the authority issues an EU certificate that grants an exemption.

**EU (applies in Italy and Germany):**

- CITES moved *Psittacus erithacus* from Appendix II to Appendix I, and Commission Regulation (EU) 2017/160 moved it from Annex B to Annex A of Council Regulation (EC) No 338/97. The amending regulation was published on 1 February 2017 and entered into force on the third day after publication.
- Article 8 of Regulation 338/97 bans the purchase, offer to purchase, acquisition for commercial purposes, display to the public for commercial purposes, sale, keeping for sale, offering for sale and transporting for sale of Annex A specimens.
- A management authority can grant an exemption case by case with a certificate, for example for birds that were bred in captivity or acquired before the rules applied.
- Member States may also prohibit keeping Annex A animals.

**Germany:**

- Selling or buying an Annex A animal without an EU marketing certificate (Vermarktungsbescheinigung) is a criminal offence under § 71(2) of the Federal Nature Conservation Act, says the City of Cologne. The certificate belongs to one individually marked bird and is passed on with it.
- Keepers of protected animals that must be reported (§ 7 of the Federal Species Protection Ordinance) must register them with the lower nature conservation authority (Untere Naturschutzbehörde) without delay, and report when they give an animal away or it dies (Cologne). For Annex A animals, the City of Nuremberg asks for a copy of the EU or CITES certificate with the registration.
- Landkreis Börde noted in 2018 that grey parrots that stay with their owner need no EU certificate; it is needed when they are sold.

**Italy:** the same EU rules apply. Before you buy, give away or sell a grey parrot, ask the competent authority what papers you need.
