## How long do cats live?

On average about 12 years: a 2024 VetCompass study of UK cats found a life expectancy at birth of 11.74 years, with female cats living about 1.3 years longer than males.

The same study linked being unneutered, being purebred and having a non-ideal body weight to a shorter life (Teng and colleagues, Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery).

## Do cats need company?

Not always. Cats usually enjoy company on their own terms, and many prefer to be the only cat, says the RSPCA; they can be social, but prefer to choose their own companions.

- **People matter too.** The RSPCA notes that cats don't really like to be left alone for long periods during the day, and need regular interaction.
- **More than one cat?** Provide multiple litter trays, hiding places, food and water so the cats don't have to share, and enough space to avoid each other at all times (RSPCA).
- **Not every pair will get along.** Some cats are never compatible, so plan for that before you add a second cat (RSPCA).

## What do cats eat?

Meat-based cat food and clean water. The RSPCA says meat is an important part of a cat's diet and that cats can't survive without the nutrients found in animal-based food.

Some foods and plants are dangerous to cats, lilies above all: see [foods and plants that are toxic to cats](/animals/cat-toxic-foods-plants). If your cat stops eating, see [cat not eating](/animals/cat-not-eating).

## What does a cat need at home?

A private litter tray, places to hide, room to be active, and regular time with people (RSPCA). With several cats, give each one access to its own resources.

The RSPCA also recommends regular grooming and a daily check of your cat, because cats often hide pain well.

## How do I know my cat is healthy?

Check your cat every day and watch for changes in behaviour or grooming, which can signal a problem (RSPCA). See the vet for a full check-up at least once a year, or twice a year or more for a senior cat (MSD Veterinary Manual).

- **Vaccinations** matter for indoor and outdoor cats alike; your vet will recommend the ones your cat needs based on where you live and its lifestyle (MSD Veterinary Manual). See [vaccinations for dogs and cats](/animals/vaccinations-dogs-cats).
- **Teeth:** kittens have 26 baby teeth, replaced by 30 adult teeth between 5 and 7 months of age. Cats need dental care throughout their lives (MSD Veterinary Manual).
- **Neutering:** the RSPCA recommends spaying female kittens at around four months, the age from which they start to attract tomcats. Neutering males can stop spraying indoors and reduce fighting and wandering.
- **Microchipping:** the RSPCA notes that microchipped cats are more likely to be reunited with their owners.
- **Medicines:** give only what your vet has prescribed. Human and dog medicines can be dangerous to cats (RSPCA).
- **Parasites:** see [fleas, ticks and worms](/animals/fleas-ticks-worms).

For the early months, see the [first year with a puppy or kitten](/animals/puppy-kitten-first-year); for older cats, the [senior dog and cat checklist](/animals/senior-dog-cat-checklist).

## When should a cat see a vet?

Immediately if your cat strains in the litter box and produces little or no urine: the Cornell Feline Health Center calls a blocked urethra an absolute emergency, and death can follow in less than 24 to 48 hours.

Male cats are at greater risk of a blockage, because their urethra is longer and narrower (Cornell). See [cat straining in the litter box](/animals/cat-straining-litter-box).

The PDSA also lists as emergencies a pet that:

- is not breathing or has difficulty breathing
- is unresponsive, or has collapsed and cannot get up
- is having a seizure
- may have eaten something toxic: see [poisoning first aid](/animals/poisoning-first-aid)
- has been vomiting or passing diarrhoea for more than 24 hours
- is having a severe allergic reaction, is choking or is bleeding heavily

If in doubt, contact your vet. Cats often hide pain well (RSPCA), so even small changes are worth a call.

## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

To travel with a cat between EU countries you need a microchip, a valid rabies vaccination and an EU pet passport. Since 22 April 2026 these rules come from Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/131, which took over from Regulation (EU) No 576/2013.

- **EU:** the regulation requires marking with an injectable transponder (microchip, Article 7), a complete primary rabies vaccination at least 21 days before the movement (Article 8), and the pet passport (Article 11). The same rules apply in Italy and Germany. Passports on the old model stay valid if they were issued before 1 January 2028 (Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/705, Article 6). Details: [travelling with a dog, cat or ferret inside the EU](/animals/travelling-eu-pets) and our [travel page](/travel).
- **Italy:** registering a cat depends on the region. In Lazio, cats and ferrets can be registered voluntarily, and registration is required for commercial purposes (Regione Lazio). In Friuli Venezia Giulia, every cat must be registered in SINAC, the national pet registry, from 1 July 2026; kittens within 60 days of birth, with fines of 150 to 900 euros for owners who don't (Regione Friuli Venezia Giulia).
