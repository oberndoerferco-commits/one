## How long do Cane Corsos live?

Probably shorter than most dogs, but the numbers are thin. A 2024 UK study found a median lifespan of 8.1 years for the Cane Corso, against 12.0 years for crossbred dogs, and listed the breed among those most at risk of early death (McMillan et al.). That estimate rests on only 202 Cane Corsos, 101 of which had died, so the true figure could lie anywhere between about 6.5 and 9.5 years. The PDSA gives an average lifespan of under 10 years.

## How big does a Cane Corso get?

Large and heavy. The FCI breed standard describes a medium to large, robust and sturdy dog with lean, powerful muscles, slightly longer than tall. Males stand 64 to 68 cm at the withers and weigh 45 to 50 kg; females stand 60 to 64 cm and weigh 40 to 45 kg, with a tolerance of 2 cm. The coat is short, shiny and very dense with a light undercoat.

## What is a Cane Corso like?

The breed descends from the old Roman molossian dogs and was kept in southern Italy, especially Apulia, to guard farms and handle cattle. The FCI standard describes a "guardian of property, family and livestock; extremely agile and responsive", once also used for herding cattle and hunting big game. It lists aggressive or overly shy dogs as disqualifying faults. That is the breeding goal; every dog is an individual.

The PDSA says Cane Corsos are large, strong dogs that should be trained with positive, reward-based methods from a young age and socialised with many people and experiences, and that they suit experienced owners best. It does not recommend the breed for homes with small children, who could be knocked over, and suggests adult-only homes or families with older children. Cane Corsos drool.

## How much exercise does a Cane Corso need?

The PDSA gives around two hours a day, including a couple of longer brisk walks, off-lead play somewhere secure and short training sessions. As a heavy breed it should not be expected to run for miles. Leave a gap between eating and exercise.

## What health problems do Cane Corsos have?

**Cherry eye:** in a VetCompass study of 905,543 dogs, the Cane Corso had 14.7 times the odds of a prolapsed third-eyelid gland ("cherry eye") compared with crossbred dogs, the third highest of all breeds after the Neapolitan Mastiff and the English Bulldog (O'Neill et al.). It is usually first diagnosed in young dogs (median age under a year) and shows as a pink swelling at the edge of the third eyelid. Left untreated, it can lead to dry eye and damage to the eye, so see your vet if you notice it.

**Joints, stomach and eyelids:** the PDSA says the breed is uncommon in the UK, so it is not yet clear which conditions are most frequent. It lists hip and elbow dysplasia, gastric dilatation-volvulus (GDV or "bloat", where the stomach twists, which is an emergency), and eyelid problems such as entropion and ectropion. For lameness see [limping in dogs](/animals/dog-limping).

## Rules in Germany

The FCI standard asks for un-cropped ears and a natural tail. German law bans amputating body parts of vertebrates; for dogs this means ear cropping and tail docking are banned unless a vet finds it necessary in the individual case, with a narrow exception for hunting dogs (§ 6 Tierschutzgesetz). Dogs whose ears or tail have been cropped in breach of animal welfare law may not be exhibited or entered in other events where dogs are judged (§ 10 Tierschutz-Hundeverordnung).

## Is a Cane Corso right for me?

Plan for a powerful 40 to 50 kg dog that needs about two hours of exercise and training a day, an experienced owner and careful socialisation from puppyhood. Because the breed is uncommon in the UK, health data are limited; ask for the parents' hip and elbow results.
