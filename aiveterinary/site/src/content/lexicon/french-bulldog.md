## How long do French Bulldogs live?

Shorter than most dogs. A 2024 UK study found a median lifespan of 9.8 years and put the French Bulldog among the breeds most at risk of early death (McMillan et al.). VetCompass life tables, built from UK vet records, gave French Bulldogs a life expectancy at birth of only 4.53 years, the shortest of the 18 breeds studied (Teng et al.).

The two figures measure different things, and the authors of the life tables add a caution: French Bulldog numbers rose steeply in the UK (Kennel Club registrations went from 2,771 in 2011 to 39,266 in 2020), so the dogs in their data were mostly young, which may make the life expectancy look lower than it is. Both studies still place the breed near the bottom.

## What health problems do French Bulldogs have?

Many of them come from the shape the breed is bred for: a short, flat face, a compact body and a screw tail. In a VetCompass study comparing 2,781 French Bulldogs with 21,850 other dogs, French Bulldogs had higher odds of 20 of 43 common disorders (O'Neill et al., 2021). The strongest were:

- **Narrowed nostrils** (stenotic nares): 42 times the odds of other dogs
- **BOAS**, brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome: 31 times the odds
- **Ear discharge**: 14 times
- **Skin fold dermatitis**: 11 times
- **Difficult births** (dystocia): 9 times

The authors propose breeding towards a more moderate body shape as the logical way to reduce these problems.

**Spine:** French Bulldogs often have malformed spinal bones (hemivertebrae). The UFAW genetic welfare database links them to selection for the screw tail; they can press on the spinal cord and cause pain, wobbliness, loss of hind-leg function and incontinence.

**Eyes and skin:** in an earlier VetCompass study of young French Bulldogs, the most common problems were ear infections (14.0%), diarrhoea (7.5%), conjunctivitis (3.2%), overlong nails (3.1%) and skin fold dermatitis (3.0%) (O'Neill et al., 2018).

## Why do French Bulldogs have breathing problems?

Because the skull is shortened but the soft tissue of the nose and throat is not, it is squeezed into a much smaller space. The UFAW database describes narrowed nostrils, an overlong soft palate and a narrow windpipe; breathing harder all the time then causes further narrowing, including collapse of the larynx. According to UFAW, to some extent all French Bulldogs may be affected because of their severe brachycephalic shape, although how badly varies.

BOAS is progressive and lifelong. It causes snoring, noisy and open-mouth breathing, fast breathing and struggling for breath, and can lead to collapse and death. Affected dogs cannot take even moderate exercise, are very prone to heatstroke and have constantly disrupted sleep (UFAW).

Signs the RSPCA lists include noisy breathing (wheezing, snorting), sleeping sitting up or with the head raised, being unable or unwilling to exercise, "reverse sneezing" and difficulty eating. To help a brachycephalic dog, the RSPCA advises:

- check its breathing every day, especially during and after exercise and in warm weather
- keep exercise steady and regular, and avoid the hottest part of the day
- use a harness instead of a collar, so there is no pressure on the neck
- keep it at a healthy weight, as extra weight makes breathing harder
- clean and dry the skin folds, especially around the face and tail, every day

Heatstroke is an emergency: see [heatstroke in dogs](/animals/heatstroke). A dog that is struggling to breathe needs a vet straight away.

## Is there a breathing test for French Bulldogs?

Yes. The Royal Kennel Club and the University of Cambridge run the Respiratory Function Grading Scheme. A trained vet listens to the dog's breathing at rest and after three minutes of brisk exercise and gives a grade from 0 (free of BOAS signs) to 3 (severe signs; not to be bred from). Dogs should be assessed from 12 months of age and every two years. If you are looking for a puppy, the scheme advises making sure both parents have been graded. Even then, two unaffected parents cannot guarantee a puppy free of BOAS.

## Is a French Bulldog right for me?

Think about the health of the breed first. The PDSA calls the health problems of flat-faced pets "a real concern", particularly with the growing popularity of French Bulldogs, and says many owners are not aware of the effect extreme body shapes can have on welfare. The RSPCA warns that caring for a brachycephalic dog is a big responsibility that can affect you financially and emotionally.

If you do choose one, plan for regular vet checks, a harness, careful exercise in cool weather, daily skin fold care, and ask for both parents' breathing grades. The FCI standard itself says the nose "must allow normal nasal breathing".

## Rules in Germany

German law bans breeding vertebrates when it is to be expected that the offspring will suffer pain, suffering or harm because inherited body parts or organs are missing, unsuitable or altered (§ 11b Tierschutzgesetz). Dogs with such features may also not be exhibited, nor take part in other events where dogs are compared or judged (§ 10 Tierschutz-Hundeverordnung). The law describes the harm and does not list breeds.
