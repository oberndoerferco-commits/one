## How long do chickens live?

Many backyard chickens live 6 to 10 years and bantams 8 to 12, according to LafeberVet; Texas A&M's veterinary college says chickens can live up to 8 years.

Hens lay far fewer eggs as they age. LafeberVet explains that layers produce the most eggs in their first year of sexual maturity, fewer in the second year, clearly fewer in years 3 and 4, and that most hens stop laying after year 5.

## How many chickens should I keep together?

More than one: Texas A&M says chickens are a social species and best kept in groups, and the Straubing-Bogen veterinary office calls four to six hens with a rooster ideal.

- Every flock has a pecking order with a dominant hen at the top, and roosters fight for the top spot (Texas A&M).
- Hens can be kept without a rooster, but a rooster tends to stabilise the pecking order (Straubing-Bogen). Texas A&M warns that roosters can be aggressive and loud, and many towns don't allow them, so check local rules first.
- Avoid overcrowding, which leads to stress and conflict (Texas A&M).
- Dogs, cats, ferrets and large reptiles may see chickens as prey, so keep contact limited and supervised (Texas A&M).

## How much space do backyard hens need?

LafeberVet gives at least 0.37 m² per large adult bird and 0.19 m² per bantam inside the housing; the Straubing-Bogen veterinary office recommends much more, including a run of about 20 m² per hen.

From the Straubing-Bogen information sheet for hobby keepers in Germany:

- a coop with at least 2.5 m² of freely usable floor; the TVT, Germany's veterinary association for animal welfare, recommends no more than two hens per m²
- about 8 m² for two hens if they have to stay inside for a while, for example during a bird flu housing order
- perches of at least 15 cm per bird, better 30 cm, one nest for every seven hens and loose litter at least 5 cm deep
- a covered dust-bathing area, structure from bushes or trees, and a fence of about 180 to 200 cm, ideally sunk about 20 cm into the ground

LafeberVet adds that the coop and pen must be covered and predator-proof, with fencing set at least 30 cm into the ground, that good ventilation keeps the air free of ammonia, and that roosting perches should sit above nest-box height.

## What do chickens eat?

A complete, balanced feed made for their life stage, says LafeberVet: chick feed first, then grower or pullet feed, and layer feed only once hens start laying, with grit and fresh water for all ages.

- **Never give layer feed to young birds**: LafeberVet warns that its high calcium damages their kidneys and can kill them.
- Free-ranging hens that eat worms, insects and plants still need a complete feed (LafeberVet).
- Kitchen scraps only occasionally, as they easily unbalance the diet; green vegetables are a good choice (LafeberVet). Never feed spoiled kitchen waste (Straubing-Bogen).
- Texas A&M says to avoid bread and other starches, chocolate, onions, caffeine, **avocado** and excess salt, and to check your garden for toxic plants.
- Keep feed in rodent-proof containers in a cool, dry place, and place drinkers at shoulder height so droppings don't get in (LafeberVet).

## What temperature do chickens need?

LafeberVet considers 10–24 °C inside the coop ideal, with humidity as low as possible; healthy adult birds that are not moulting rarely need extra heat except in extreme weather.

In cold weather the birds' combined body heat is often enough. If you add a heat lamp or heated mat, take care not to create a fire hazard or overheat the birds; overheated birds move away from the heat and stand near exits and walls (LafeberVet). Chicks are different: they cannot control their body temperature at hatching and need a heat source until they are feathered.

## How do I keep my hens healthy?

Keep other birds' germs out: LafeberVet says the single most important way disease enters a flock is through new birds, so quarantine newcomers for at least 4 weeks and have a vet check them first.

- House quarantined birds in a separate building and look after them last (LafeberVet).
- Don't let people or pets who have recently been around other birds visit your flock, and don't borrow or share equipment unless it is cleaned and disinfected (LafeberVet).
- Clean drinkers and feeders daily, spot-clean litter daily and clean nest boxes weekly (LafeberVet).
- Keep wild birds away from feed, water and litter, and never give poultry surface water that wild birds can reach (Schweinfurt veterinary office).
- Wash your hands after handling chickens: Texas A&M says their droppings can carry Salmonella, which can be on wings and feet too and is a particular risk for children.

## When should a chicken see a vet?

Call a vet straight away if several birds die within a day, if laying changes markedly or birds lose weight, or if a bird dies suddenly; in Germany, keepers must then have the cause found, and suspected bird flu must be reported to the vet office at once.

- The Schweinfurt veterinary office explains the German rule: if within 24 hours at least 3 birds die in a flock of up to 100 (more than 2 in 100 in larger flocks), or laying changes markedly, or the birds lose more than 5% of their weight, the owner must have a vet find the cause at once, always including tests for bird flu (influenza A subtypes H5 and H7).
- The Straubing-Bogen veterinary office says even the suspicion of bird flu must be reported to the vet office immediately.
- If a chicken dies suddenly, LafeberVet says it is very important for the rest of the flock to find out why, usually with a post-mortem examination.
- Texas A&M notes that most backyard chickens need little veterinary care and are usually treated when problems arise, but that good care avoids many problems.

## Do I need to register my chickens?

Yes in Germany and in Italy, even for a few hens kept as a hobby: in Germany with the veterinary office and the Tierseuchenkasse (animal disease fund), in Italy with the ASL veterinary service, which records the flock in the national database (BDN).

**Germany:**

- Every poultry keeper, commercial or hobby, must report keeping poultry to the veterinary office and the Tierseuchenkasse under § 26 of the Viehverkehrsverordnung (Straubing-Bogen). The Landkreis Kassel adds that keeping even a single bird must be reported.
- You give your name, address, species, number of birds, purpose and where they are kept, and you need a registration number, which in Bavaria comes from the local agriculture office (Straubing-Bogen, Schweinfurt).
- Tierseuchenkasse rules are set by each federal state: in Bavaria, for example, only keepers of chickens and turkeys must register with the Bavarian Tierseuchenkasse (Schweinfurt).
- Every keeper must keep a flock register of all birds coming and going, under § 2 of the Geflügelpest-Verordnung (Straubing-Bogen, Schweinfurt).
- Vaccination against Newcastle disease is compulsory for every chicken and turkey flock, however small (Straubing-Bogen, Landkreis Kassel).

**Italy:**

- A family flock ("allevamento familiare") is registered directly with the ASL veterinary service, while ordinary farms register through the town's SUAP office (ASL Sassari).
- A family flock means poultry kept for your own consumption or private domestic use, with no commercial activity, and up to 50 birds (ASL Viterbo, ASL Sassari).
- Each poultry species you keep gets its own registration number in the BDN, and a family flock and an ordinary flock cannot exist on the same site (ASL Viterbo).

## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

Beyond registration, three sets of rules matter for backyard hens: bird flu measures, food rules for your eggs, and medicine rules, because hens kept for eggs or meat count as food animals.

**Bird flu (avian influenza):**

- In Germany, the veterinary authority can order poultry to be kept indoors (Stallpflicht). The Straubing-Bogen office says that bird flu breaks out practically every year, leading to a housing order that it checks, so plan your coop for it. Keepers must be able to house their birds in closed buildings during an outbreak (Schweinfurt).
- In an outbreak, the affected flock is killed, and protection and surveillance zones of 3 km and 10 km are set up with movement restrictions (Straubing-Bogen).
- The Friedrich-Loeffler-Institut (FLI), Germany's national animal health institute, expected a further rise in H5 bird flu cases in poultry and wild birds for October 2026 in its risk assessment of 9 October 2026.
- In Italy, measures are set by the Ministry of Health and the regions. In Lombardy, for example, the high-risk period runs from 15 September to 15 March; in the risk zones A and B, ordinary farms must keep all their poultry indoors, family flocks must keep contact with wild birds to a minimum, and the measures apply until 15 March 2027 (Regione Lombardia).

**Eggs and meat from your own hens:**

- The EU food hygiene regulation (EC) No 852/2004 does not apply to primary production for private domestic use or to preparing food at home for your own consumption.
- In Germany, according to the Straubing-Bogen office, if you sell small quantities of your own eggs directly to consumers, you need no registered laying unit, no egg stamping and no grading by quality and weight.

**Medicines:**

- LafeberVet says all poultry are considered food animals and very few antibiotics are licensed for them, even when the eggs or meat are eaten only by the owner. Ask your vet before giving your hens any medicine.
- Under Article 108 of Regulation (EU) 2019/6, owners and keepers of food-producing animals must record the medicines they use: date, product name, quantity, supplier, which animals were treated, the prescribing vet, the withdrawal period and the length of treatment, and keep the records for at least five years.
- The withdrawal period is the minimum time between the last administration of a veterinary medicine to an animal and the production of food from that animal (Regulation 2019/6). If a vet uses a medicine outside its authorisation in laying hens, the vet sets the withdrawal period; for eggs it must be at least 10 days if the product is not authorised for egg-laying animals (Article 115).
- In Germany, keepers must also keep records of buying and using pharmacy-only medicines for food-producing animals for five years (Straubing-Bogen).
