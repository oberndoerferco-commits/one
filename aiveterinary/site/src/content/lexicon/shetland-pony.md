## How big is a Shetland pony?

Small. The Shetland Pony Stud-Book Society, which sets the breed standard worldwide, says registered ponies must not be taller than 42 inches (107 cm) at the withers. The breed has lived on the Shetland Isles for over 2,000 years. The standard describes a strong body, a double winter coat with guard hairs that shed the rain and keep the skin dry, a short silky summer coat and a long, profuse mane and tail. It calls the pony's air of vitality, stamina and robustness its most essential feature.

We found no published lifespan study for Shetland ponies.

## Why are Shetlands prone to laminitis?

Laminitis is a painful disease of the feet. Native pony breeds are at higher risk than other horses. In a British cohort study of 1,070 horses and ponies, native ponies, mainly Welsh, Connemara, New Forest and Shetland ponies and their crosses, developed laminitis at 1.9 times the rate of other breeds. Weight gain during the study was also linked with more laminitis (Pollard et al., 2019).

Much of the risk comes through weight and insulin:

- In 446 UK ponies aged seven or older, 17% of them Shetlands, 72.2% were overweight or obese. Within three years 9.9% had laminitis, and high insulin levels beforehand were among the risk factors (Menzies-Gow et al., 2017).
- In 167 Shetland and Welsh ponies in Australia, 61% had insulin dysregulation, the core of equine metabolic syndrome. Ponies with it had several times the odds of laminitis. Older age, being female and being judged obese by the owner were linked with it (Clark et al., 2024).
- A gene variant (HMGA2) that was first found to make Shetlands smaller is also linked with higher insulin. In a study of 236 ponies, 120 of them Shetlands, ponies with two copies were more than 10 cm shorter and had higher insulin (Clark et al., 2023).

Read about the signs and what to do in [laminitis in horses](/animals/horse-laminitis).

## What is hyperlipaemia?

A dangerous build-up of fat in the blood that can damage the liver and kidneys. In a UK series of 18 cases, 11 were Shetland ponies, 12 were obese, many mares were in foal or feeding a foal, and 67% died. In one pony it seems to have been triggered by undernutrition meant to prevent laminitis (Watson et al., 1992). This is an old and small case series, but it is a reason to plan any weight loss for a fat pony with your vet rather than cutting its food drastically, and to call a vet if a Shetland stops eating.

## Are there inherited problems?

The stud-book society strongly advises its breeders not to use silver ponies or carriers of the silver gene, because of abnormalities found in their pedigrees, and says research is ongoing. It recommends that colts go through a Voluntary Vetting Examination to check they are free from hereditary conditions, and since 1 January 2022 the sire of every registered foal must have a DNA profile.

In the much smaller Miniature Shetland pony, a dwarf foal with a malformed skull, a short upper jaw and a cleft palate that caused breathing difficulties carried two copies of a variant in the ACAN gene; its healthy parents carried one each (Metzger et al., 2017). This is a single case.

## Is a Shetland right for me?

A Shetland is small but not a toy: it is a horse with the same needs for company, forage and space as any other, as our [horse fact sheet](/animals/horse) explains. In the studies above its biggest health risks are weight, insulin and laminitis, so plan how you will control its weight before you take one on.
