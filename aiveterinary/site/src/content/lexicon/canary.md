## How long do canaries live?

Up to 15 years, according to the MSD Veterinary Manual.

Canaries come from the Canary Islands and Madeira (MSD). The RVC says monks are thought to have started breeding them in the 1400s, which led to the many colours and types seen today. The MSD Veterinary Manual describes three main groups: colour-bred, type (bred for shape and feathering) and song canaries, and says canaries suit most flats.

## Do canaries sing, and how do I tell males from females?

Males are generally the better singers, while females mostly chirp or whistle; for certain, canaries can only be sexed with a DNA test from a blood sample or feather (RVC).

The MSD Veterinary Manual also says males often have a lovely song, which is how they are told apart from females.

## Should canaries be kept alone or in pairs?

The sources differ: the MSD Veterinary Manual says canaries are territorial and prefer to be housed alone or in mated pairs, while the RSPCA lists them among colony-breeding species that can live in social groups.

If you keep more than one canary, the RSPCA says the birds must be compatible, have room to fly and be able to get away from each other, in a large aviary or a large cage suited to the species. Introduce new birds gradually, and give them separate living areas if they don't get along. The RVC adds that canaries make good pets because they are tidy, easy to care for and non-destructive, but they are not generally keen on being handled.

## How big should a canary cage be?

As big as you can manage: the RVC says canaries are happiest in a spacious aviary, indoors or outdoors, and an indoor cage must let the bird stretch its wings in every direction and ideally fly.

- The RSPCA bases cage size on wingspan: if the bird spends most of its time in the cage, height, width and depth should each be at least twice its wingspan.
- For a group of more than two small birds under 50 g, such as canaries, add 5% to the cage for every bird over the first two (RSPCA).
- Free flight is essential, says the RVC. Before letting your canary out, block off windows, doors and chimneys, turn off heaters and fans and remove poisonous house plants.
- Use natural tree branches as perches, not dowels or sandpaper covers, and make sure cages and toys are zinc-free (RVC).
- Outdoor aviaries need zinc-free wire mesh, a warm, sheltered sleeping area, extra heating in winter, shade and double doors against escapes and predators (RVC).

## What do canaries eat?

Seed mixes are common, but the RVC warns that many contain millet that is high in fat and low in vitamins and minerals; good complete pellets give a better balance, with fresh greens such as seeding grasses, dandelion, chickweed and groundsel.

- The RSPCA agrees that canaries are often fed seed-based diets but would benefit from good-quality pellets, plus fresh, washed greens.
- Pick greens from unpolluted places and wash them well (RVC).
- Give a **cuttlefish bone** for extra calcium and to wear down the beak. The RVC says grit can be offered but is not thought to be essential.
- Fresh water must always be available, both to drink and in a shallow dish for bathing (RVC).

The MSD Veterinary Manual lists **avocado** among household poisons for birds, so never offer it.

## What temperature do canaries need?

The MSD Veterinary Manual says birds usually do well at temperatures that are comfortable for people; keep the cage away from windows, air conditioning and draughts, and out of the kitchen.

The RVC says to place the cage out of direct sunlight and draughts and away from anything that gives off toxic fumes. It names tobacco smoke, Teflon from non-stick frying pans, plug-ins and deodorants, which can irritate a bird's airways and in some cases be fatal. In an outdoor aviary, the RVC advises a sheltered, warm sleeping area and extra heating in winter.

## How do I know my canary is healthy?

A healthy bird is bright and alert with clear eyes, active and not huddled or fluffed up, says the MSD Veterinary Manual; because birds hide illness, any change from normal is a reason to call the vet.

The MSD Veterinary Manual lists these signs of illness:

- fluffed-up feathers, sleeping more than usual with closed eyes (see [bird fluffed up and sleepy](/animals/budgie-fluffed-up))
- not being active, singing less
- sitting low on the perch or at the bottom of the cage, weakness or losing balance
- wheezing or tail bobbing while breathing
- changes in droppings (see [bird droppings and diarrhoea](/animals/budgie-diarrhoea))
- eating or drinking more or less than usual, or throwing up food

Feather loss outside the moult needs a vet: see [bird feather plucking](/animals/bird-feather-plucking).

## When should a canary see a vet?

Go now if your canary is huddled, weak, unresponsive or lying on the cage floor, which the MSD Veterinary Manual calls an emergency; otherwise have a health check every year, as the RVC recommends.

Go to a vet who treats birds now, and call ahead, if your canary:

- is huddled, weak, unresponsive or lying on the cage floor (MSD)
- is bleeding and the bleeding doesn't stop (MSD)
- has difficulty breathing, such as wheezing or tail bobbing with each breath (MSD)
- is losing its balance (MSD)
- has breathed fumes from an overheated non-stick pan, smoke or sprays: birds' airways are very sensitive to fumes (MSD)
- is a hen that strains or sits on the floor with a swollen belly: see [egg binding](/animals/budgie-egg-bound)

On the way, think warmth, darkness and a secure carrier, with at least three sides covered by a towel (MSD).

Have a new canary checked by a vet before you bring it home, ideally one specially trained to treat birds (MSD). Natural tree branches as perches help prevent overgrown nails (RVC).
