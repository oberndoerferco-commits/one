## How long do dogs live?

Dogs live about 8 to 16 years, depending on the breed, according to the MSD Veterinary Manual. In general, smaller dogs tend to live longer than larger ones.

Ask your vet or breeder about the typical lifespan and inherited health problems of your dog's breed or mix.

## How big do dogs get?

It depends entirely on the breed. The MSD Veterinary Manual gives a range from about 2.5–4.5 kg for the smallest breeds, such as the Toy Poodle and Chihuahua, to more than 90 kg for giant breeds such as the Mastiff and Great Dane.

## Do dogs need company?

Yes. The RSPCA describes dogs as sociable animals who enjoy company, and advises never to leave a dog alone long enough for it to become distressed.

Signs that a dog struggles when left alone include destructive behaviour, toileting indoors and barking, says the RSPCA. If you see them, read our article on [separation anxiety](/animals/dog-separation-anxiety) and talk to your vet.

German law is specific: the Tierschutz-Hundeverordnung requires enough contact with the person who looks after the dog, several times a day for a dog kept on its own, and at least four hours a day for puppies up to 20 weeks old (as summarised by the Vogelsbergkreis veterinary office).

## How much exercise does a dog need?

All dogs need exercise (RSPCA), and how much depends on the dog: German rules say exercise and social contact must be matched to the dog's breed, age and health.

In hot weather, the PDSA advises walking dogs in the cooler hours, such as early morning or later in the evening, and avoiding strenuous exercise like running and games of fetch on hot or humid days.

## What do dogs eat?

A well-balanced diet suited to the dog's age, lifestyle and health, with constant access to clean drinking water. The RSPCA warns that without water a dog can become seriously ill within hours.

- Follow the feeding guide on the food, and adjust to keep your dog at a healthy weight (RSPCA).
- Most human food does not meet a dog's needs, and some foods, such as chocolate and onions, are poisonous (RSPCA). See [foods that are toxic to dogs](/animals/dog-toxic-foods) and [my dog ate chocolate](/animals/dog-ate-chocolate).
- A change in eating or drinking can be a sign of illness, so contact your vet (RSPCA).

## How do I know my dog is healthy?

Know what is normal for your dog, and see the vet for a full check-up at least once a year, or twice a year or more once your dog is a senior (over 7–8 years), as the MSD Veterinary Manual recommends.

- **Vaccinations:** core vaccines protect against illnesses such as distemper, parvovirus and rabies (MSD Veterinary Manual). See [vaccinations for dogs and cats](/animals/vaccinations-dogs-cats).
- **Teeth:** puppies have 28 baby teeth, replaced by 42 adult teeth. Brushing your dog's teeth helps prevent plaque and tartar (MSD Veterinary Manual).
- **Temperature:** the average body temperature of a dog is 38.3–39.2 °C (MSD Veterinary Manual). See [fever in dogs](/animals/dog-fever).
- **Parasites:** see [fleas, ticks and worms](/animals/fleas-ticks-worms).

For the first months, the [first year with a puppy or kitten](/animals/puppy-kitten-first-year) has a month-by-month plan; for older dogs, see the [senior dog and cat checklist](/animals/senior-dog-cat-checklist).

## When should a dog see a vet?

Go to a vet as an emergency if your dog is not breathing or has difficulty breathing, has collapsed and cannot get up, is having a seizure or may have eaten something toxic, says the PDSA. If in doubt, call your vet.

The PDSA's emergency list also includes a dog that:

- is unresponsive
- may have broken bones
- has difficulty moving or coordinating its movements
- has been vomiting or passing diarrhoea for more than 24 hours
- is unable to pee
- is having a severe allergic reaction
- is choking
- is bleeding heavily

**Heatstroke** can be fatal, warns the PDSA. Signs include heavy panting or short breaths, bright red gums, shaking, confusion, fits and falling unconscious. Phone your vet and start cooling your dog straight away, and never leave a dog in a car, caravan, conservatory or shed, especially on hot days. See [heatstroke in dogs](/animals/heatstroke).

For a suspected poisoning, see [poisoning first aid](/animals/poisoning-first-aid). For everyday symptoms, our articles on [vomiting](/animals/dog-vomiting), [diarrhoea](/animals/dog-diarrhoea) and [not eating](/animals/dog-not-eating) explain when to call.

## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

To travel with a dog between EU countries you need a microchip, a valid rabies vaccination and an EU pet passport. Since 22 April 2026 these rules come from Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/131, which took over from Regulation (EU) No 576/2013.

- **EU:** the regulation requires marking with an injectable transponder (microchip, Article 7), a complete primary rabies vaccination at least 21 days before the movement (Article 8), and the pet passport (Article 11). Passports on the old model stay valid if they were issued before 1 January 2028 (Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/705, Article 6). Details, including extra rules of some countries: [travelling with a dog, cat or ferret inside the EU](/animals/travelling-eu-pets) and our [travel page](/travel).
- **Italy:** a dog must be identified with a microchip and registered in the dog registry (anagrafe canina). In Lazio, for example, this must happen within 60 days of birth, or within 10 days of taking on an older dog, at the local health authority (ASL) or an accredited vet (Regione Lazio). Deadlines can differ between regions.
- **Germany:** the Tierschutz-Hundeverordnung sets minimum rules for keeping dogs (as summarised by the Vogelsbergkreis veterinary office): enough exercise outdoors and enough contact with the carer, water available at all times, fresh air and a suitable temperature when a dog is left alone, especially in vehicles, and a puppy may only be separated from its mother when older than eight weeks. Since 1 January 2023, dogs may in principle no longer be kept tethered.
