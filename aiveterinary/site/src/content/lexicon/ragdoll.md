## How long do Ragdolls live?

In UK vet records of 2,025 Ragdolls in 2019, the median age at death was 12.85 years, and the most common causes of death were kidney disease (21.2%) and poor quality of life (12.9%) (O'Neill et al., 2025). That is more than a year shorter than the 14.0 years an earlier study found for cats overall, but in line with purebred cats in general, so the authors think the gap comes mostly from being purebred rather than from being a Ragdoll.

VetCompass life tables for cats that died in 2019 put a Ragdoll's life expectancy at birth at 10.31 years, against 11.89 years for crossbred cats (Teng et al.). The GCCF, the UK's main cat registry, gives 12 to 17 years.

## How big does a Ragdoll get?

Large. The GCCF calls the Ragdoll one of the largest cat breeds, with a long, substantial body and sturdy bones, and says it takes up to four years to reach full maturity. Fully grown males often weigh more than 6 kg, females 4 to 6 kg. Adult Ragdolls in UK vet care had a median bodyweight of 4.46 kg (O'Neill et al., 2025).

## What health problems do Ragdolls have?

In the VetCompass study, the most common problems were (O'Neill et al., 2025):

- **Dental (periodontal) disease**: 8.8%
- **Diarrhoea**: 7.1%
- **Obesity**: 6.9%

By group, dental (14.5%), gut (enteropathy, 13.5%) and skin problems (7.8%) led. The authors find the breed largely typical of purebred cats, though it appears predisposed to coat problems and enteropathy.

The GCCF lists two health issues for the breed. One is HCM (below). The other is blood group: a sub blood group B3 has been recognised, and mating cats with compatible blood groups is strongly recommended. The PDSA also lists **polycystic kidney disease (PKD)**, in which cysts in the kidneys grow until the kidneys fail; there is a genetic test. For kidney disease in older cats, see [kidney disease in cats](/animals/cat-kidney-disease).

## What is HCM in Ragdolls?

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is the most common heart disease in cats, and very common in Ragdolls (UFAW). The heart wall thickens until the heart cannot work normally. Many affected cats look healthy for a long time; a vet may hear a murmur or an abnormal rhythm, and an ultrasound scan of the heart is the key test. In severe cases fluid builds up in the lungs, which makes breathing very hard. A blood clot can also form in the heart and block a blood vessel, most often to the legs, causing sudden paralysis of one or both back legs with severe pain (International Cat Care).

According to UFAW, about 30% of Ragdolls in the UK carry a gene variant that causes HCM. Cats with one copy tend to have milder disease; cats with two copies are often severely affected and develop heart failure before two years of age.

A DNA test for the known Ragdoll variant has been available since 2007. Under GCCF rules, all Ragdolls registered for breeding since 24 February 2016 must be tested; the GCCF advises asking for copies of the parents' results. International Cat Care recommends screening with a heart scan (echocardiogram) as well as the DNA test: cats with HCM on the scan, or with two copies of the variant, should not be bred from, and breeding cats should have a heart scan every year.

A cat that breathes fast or with its mouth open at rest, or is suddenly lame or paralysed in the back legs, needs a vet straight away.

## Is a Ragdoll right for me?

The GCCF describes a gentle, playful cat, relaxed and easy to handle, with an "astonishingly equable character". It also corrects two myths: the floppiness that gave the breed its name is "pure hype", and the claim that Ragdolls have a low pain threshold is "positively dangerous", because they feel pain like any other cat. Their coat needs weekly grooming and more when moulting, and play keeps them fit. Ask for the parents' HCM test results.
