## How long do Russian Blues live?

The data are thin. VetCompass life tables for cats that died in the UK in 2019 give Russian cats (Russian Blue and Russian not further specified) a life expectancy at birth of 9.65 years, against 11.89 years for crossbred cats (Teng et al., 2024). That figure rests on only 19 deaths, so its margin of error is wide (7.20 to 12.10 years). The GCCF, the UK's main cat registry, gives 12 to 15 years and says Russians often live well into their late teens.

## How big does a Russian Blue get?

Medium to large, but always graceful. The GCCF describes an elegant cat of foreign type with a long body, long legs, small oval paws and medium-strong bone; a cobby or heavy build is undesirable. Fully grown Russians weigh 8 to 15 pounds (about 3.6 to 6.8 kg) on average, and males are usually larger than females.

## What is special about the coat?

Breeders and judges call the coat the breed's defining feature (GCCF): a short, thick but fine double coat with a very dense undercoat that stands away from the body and feels soft and silky. Russian Blues are an even medium blue with a silvery sheen and vivid green eyes. The GCCF also recognises black and white Russians; the large CFA (United States) and FIFe (Europe) registries accept only blue. Like most shorthairs, the coat needs little grooming, but many Russians enjoy a soft brush.

## What health problems do Russian Blues have?

The GCCF calls the Russian generally a very healthy breed and says no major genetic problems have been reported. As in other white cats, deafness is a concern in white Russians: those on the GCCF active register used for breeding must have had a BAER hearing test. Because Russian Whites have green eyes, deafness is usually low.

## Is a Russian Blue right for me?

The GCCF describes an intelligent cat that watches new people closely and can seem shy or aloof, but is extremely affectionate once it has decided you are worthy, and gets on well with children and other pets. It is content on its own while you are out and is home-loving: it copes with living indoors if there is plenty of play with its owner, but windows then need screens.

## Rules in Germany

§ 11b of the German Animal Welfare Act bans breeding when the offspring are expected to suffer because inherited body parts or organs are missing, unsuitable or altered; it names no breeds. A 1999 expert report on how to interpret § 11b, written for the federal agriculture ministry, names the Russian White among white breeds and recommends a breeding ban for white cats whose colour comes from the dominant W gene, because their offspring can be deaf or have eye defects. This concerns white cats, not Russian Blues.
