## How old do horses get?

On average 25 to 30 years. The MSD Veterinary Manual says a horse's lifespan depends on its health, care and size, and that miniature horses can live 40 years or more.

That means a horse can be part of your life for decades. The Defra code of practice advises owners to make plans for emergencies and to leave a record of their wishes in case someone else has to care for their horse, for example during a holiday or after the owner's death.

## Can a horse live alone?

No, not well. The Defra code of practice says horses are herd animals that should be able to socialise with their own species, and that isolating a horse from other horses can have a negative psychological impact.

- **Other horses are best.** Where that is not possible, other animals can keep a horse company, but the code says the company of other horses is by far the better option.
- **Pair bonds matter.** Horses may become distressed if they are separated from other horses, or from a horse they have bonded with.
- **Introduce new horses carefully.** When forming new groups, the code advises minimising fighting and stress, for example with more space, or by grazing the newcomer next to the group for a short time first.
- **A donkey is not the same as a horse companion.** Donkeys have their own social needs: see our [donkey](/animals/donkey) entry.

## How much do horses graze and move?

A lot. The Defra code says horses evolved to spend 16 to 20 hours a day grazing and browsing for forage, so they have a strong behavioural need to chew.

In the wild, horses travel long distances searching for resources. The code says all stabled horses, except those on box rest for veterinary reasons, benefit from daily turnout to graze and socialise with other horses, which may also lower the risk of habits such as crib-biting, weaving and box walking.

## What do horses eat and drink?

Mostly fibre: grass, hay, haylage or a hay replacement. The Defra code says horses are natural grazers that eat little and often, and should have almost constant access to forage when they are not exercising.

- **Forage first.** Horses fed diets too low in fibre may suffer physical or psychological health problems (Defra code).
- **Not too rich.** Grass mixes designed for cows and sheep are not suitable, as their higher energy content may lead to obesity and laminitis. The code says a very overweight horse is as much of a welfare concern as an underweight one.
- **Plenty of water.** An average adult horse needs about 25–50 litres of water a day, around 5 to 10 litres per 100 kg of body weight, and more in hot weather (Defra code).
- **Watch for changes.** A sudden change in appetite or weight can be a sign of ill health (Defra code).

## How much space does a horse need?

As a general rule, about 0.5 to 1.0 hectares of suitable grazing per horse if it gets no extra feed, according to the Defra code. A horse that lives mainly in a stable and goes out only for turnout may need less.

Some horses, particularly native breeds, can live outdoors all year if they can shelter from the prevailing winds, summer sun and flies, under trees or hedges or in a field shelter. The Defra code adds that living outdoors in a social group may suit some horses better than living inside, while less hardy, clipped, very young or old horses may need a stable, and that any horse may need stabling at short notice if it becomes sick or injured.

## How often do teeth and hooves need care?

Teeth at least once a year, hooves usually every 4 to 8 weeks. The Defra code says teeth should be checked by a vet or a qualified equine dental technician at least once a year, and hooves trimmed or re-shod as the farrier advises.

A horse's teeth grow constantly throughout its life (MSD Veterinary Manual). The code warns that a horse with worn or abnormal teeth cannot chew properly, and that older horses may need dental checks more than once a year.

## How should I handle a horse?

Calmly and with respect for its instincts. A horse is a classic flight animal: faced with danger, it instinctively runs, as the Swiss Federal Food Safety and Veterinary Office (BLV) puts it.

Learn your horse's normal behaviour: the Defra code says owners should be able to recognise it, because a change can be the first sign of illness.

## Which vaccinations does a horse need?

Tetanus at least. The Defra code strongly recommends vaccinating all horses against tetanus, because horses are very susceptible to this fatal disease.

Horses can also be vaccinated against equine herpes virus, strangles and equine influenza. Which vaccinations suit your horse depends on its age and use, so the code advises discussing them with your vet. Worm control should be planned with your vet and based on faecal egg counts (Defra code).

## When should a horse see a vet?

Straight away if your horse shows any sign of colic, such as pawing, rolling, sweating or fast breathing. The British Horse Society calls colic the most common emergency and cause of death in horses worldwide, and says it should be treated as an emergency because it can get worse quickly.

Call your vet straight away if your horse:

- shows signs of pain or discomfort, such as facial tension, reluctance to move, pawing at the ground, rolling, faster breathing or sweating (Defra code): these can be signs of [colic](/animals/horse-colic)
- is reluctant or unable to stand (Defra code)

The Defra code lists more signs that may mean poor health, and says to contact your vet if you think anything is wrong:

- any injury or lameness, including puncture wounds: lameness can be a sign of [laminitis](/animals/horse-laminitis)
- coughing or breathing difficulties, or discharge from the eyes, ears or nose: see [my horse is coughing](/animals/horse-coughing)
- a change in appetite, drinking, droppings, urine, behaviour or weight

The code also says veterinary advice should be sought if a horse seems ill or in pain and the cause is not clear, or if first aid does not work. Knowing your horse's normal temperature, pulse and breathing helps you and your vet: see [horse vital signs](/animals/horse-vital-signs).

## Rules in the EU, Italy and Germany

In every EU country, Italy and Germany included, a horse needs a single lifetime identification document, the equine passport, and it must always accompany the horse. The passport also records whether the horse may enter the food chain, which decides which medicines a vet can use.

- **Passport:** Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/963 recalls (recital 50) that, under Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/2035, operators must make sure equine animals are accompanied by their single lifetime identification document at all times. The Defra code adds a practical point: without the passport at hand, some treatments may not be available.
- **Food-chain status:** under Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/963, horses are deemed to be intended for slaughter for human consumption unless they are irreversibly excluded by a signed entry in the passport (Article 38). Before certain treatments, the vet must check this status in the passport and, where they have access, in the database (Article 39).
- **Medicines:** under Regulation (EU) 2019/6, the rules that let a vet use medicines outside their authorisation in animals not kept for food apply to a horse only if its passport declares it not intended for slaughter for human consumption (Article 112(4)).
- **Donkeys too:** Regulation (EU) 2021/963 defines equine animals as including horses, asses and zebras, so the same rules apply to [donkeys](/animals/donkey).

Keep the passport where you can find it quickly, and ask your vet about your horse's status before you need it in a hurry.
