## How long do Netherland Dwarfs live?

There is no study of this breed on its own. In UK vet records from 2013, the 370 rabbits of all breeds that died had a median age at death of 4.3 years, with some reaching 14 (O'Neill et al., 2020). The authors note that about a third of pet rabbits are not registered with a vet, so the figure may not reflect all rabbits. For rabbits in general, see our [rabbit fact sheet](/animals/rabbit).

## How big does a Netherland Dwarf get?

Very small. In the same records, 672 Netherland Dwarfs had a median adult weight of 1.4 kg, the lowest of the common breed types; the median for all rabbits was 2.1 kg (O'Neill et al., 2020). The RSPCA lists dwarf and mini varieties, bred for their small size, among the rabbits that commonly have inherited health problems.

## Do Netherland Dwarfs have more teeth problems?

Welfare groups think so, but the studies do not agree.

**What welfare groups say.** The Rabbit Welfare Association & Fund (RWAF) counts every dwarf breed as brachycephalic (short-faced). It explains that a flattened head crowds the cheek teeth and can push the front teeth out of line. Teeth that do not meet do not wear down, grow too long and form painful spurs that cut the cheeks and tongue. The German vets' animal welfare association TVT writes that breeding for a cute look, such as mini dwarfs under 1,000 g with a very round head and short ears, often leads to misaligned teeth that only a vet can treat.

**What the large studies found.** In UK vet records of 161,979 rabbits in 2019, 15.36% had dental disease in a single year. Lop ears and a short face were not linked with higher odds, but the odds of dental disease fell as body weight rose, so lighter rabbits were affected more often (Jackson et al., 2024). In a 2025 study, vets examined the mouths of 435 pedigree show rabbits, 55 of them Netherland Dwarfs. Small breed size was not linked with dental abnormalities, and the authors conclude that diet and housing matter more than body shape. They add that show rabbits are young and well kept, so the results may not apply to pets (Jackson et al., 2025).

**One clearly inherited problem.** A shortened upper jaw (maxillary brachygnathism) means the front teeth cannot meet. It is passed on as a recessive trait, and rabbits with it should not be bred (Jackson et al., 2025).

Whatever the breed, hay keeps teeth healthy. Read more in [overgrown teeth in rabbits](/animals/rabbit-overgrown-teeth).

## What else should I watch for?

**Tear ducts.** In short-faced rabbits the duct that drains tears into the nose is twisted and blocks more easily (RWAF). In UK vet records from 2019, short-headed rabbits had 3.5 times the risk of tear duct disease of medium-headed rabbits (O'Neill et al., 2024).

**Dirty bottom.** The same study found 2.6 times the risk of a dirty bottom. A soiled bottom attracts flies: see [flystrike in rabbits](/animals/rabbit-flystrike), which is an emergency.

The RWAF suggests checking a short-faced rabbit regularly for:

- weepy eyes
- lumps at the base of the ears
- drool around the mouth
- overgrown front teeth
- a dirty, matted bottom
- head tilt or head shaking
- a change in diet, such as avoiding hay or hard food

A rabbit that eats less or stops passing droppings needs a vet straight away: see [my rabbit isn't eating](/animals/rabbit-not-eating).

## Rules in Germany

German law bans breeding vertebrates when the offspring can be expected to suffer because inherited body parts are missing, unsuitable or altered (§ 11b Tierschutzgesetz). The law names no breeds. An expert report written for the federal ministry to interpret § 11b recommends a breeding ban for adult dwarf rabbits under 1.0 kg, because of high losses among the young, and for rabbits with a shortened jaw or an extremely round head.

## Is a Netherland Dwarf right for me?

The RSPCA encourages adopting a rabbit rather than buying one and does not recommend rabbits with extreme features. The RWAF says there are many dwarf and other short-faced rabbits in rescue centres; it is a good idea to adopt one as long as you know the risks and see a rabbit-savvy vet. However small it is, a Netherland Dwarf has the same basic needs as any rabbit: company, space and a diet that is mostly hay, as our [rabbit fact sheet](/animals/rabbit) explains.
