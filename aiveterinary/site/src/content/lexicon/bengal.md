## How long do Bengals live?

Shorter than most cats, going by UK vet records. VetCompass life tables for cats that died in 2019 put a Bengal's life expectancy at birth at 8.51 years, based on 73 deaths, against 11.89 years for crossbred cats (Teng et al., 2024). Only the Sphynx was lower among the 12 breeds analysed, and the authors name the Bengal as a breed with a particularly short life expectancy. The median age at death in that study was 8.40 years.

An earlier VetCompass study of cats in England (2009–2012) also found the Bengal the shortest-lived breed, with a median of 7.3 years, but that figure rests on only 15 cats (O'Neill et al., 2015). The GCCF, the UK's main cat registry, gives 12 to 16 years.

## How big does a Bengal get?

Medium to large. The GCCF describes a cat of foreign type with a strong, muscular, athletic body and slightly longer hind legs. Females weigh 3.6 to 5.4 kg, males 4.5 to 6.8 kg.

## Where does the Bengal come from?

The breed began in the United States in the early 1960s as a cross between the Asian Leopard Cat, a small wild cat, and domestic cats. According to the GCCF, males from the first generation are sterile, first-generation cats could be problematic as pets, and by the fourth generation the cat can be considered domestic rather than a hybrid. The GCCF stopped registering first-generation (F1) Bengals on 1 June 2002, and third-generation (F3) Bengals from 1 June 2008.

## What health problems do Bengals have?

The GCCF calls the Bengal generally healthy but names several problems buyers should know about:

- **HCM** (hypertrophic cardiomyopathy), a thickening of the heart muscle, occurs in some Bengal lines. There is no commercial DNA test in the breed, so breeding cats should be screened with a heart scan.
- **Pyruvate kinase deficiency**, which causes long-term anaemia, and **progressive retinal atrophy**, an eye disease that leads to blindness. DNA tests exist for both.
- **Slipping kneecaps** (patellar luxation) were seen in early imports and are now less common.
- **Flat-chested kitten syndrome**: International Cat Care says the chest bone and rib cartilage do not form correctly, and the condition is seen more often in Bengals, Burmese and Orientals. Mild cases often grow out of it; severe cases have a poor outlook.

The PDSA also lists hip dysplasia and FIP (feline infectious peritonitis), a usually fatal infectious disease that Bengals are more likely to develop than non-pedigree cats. The GCCF adds that gut parasites are not uncommon in kittens from larger breeding establishments: a cat with diarrhoea that does not settle should be tested for Giardia and Tritrichomonas foetus. See [diarrhoea in cats](/animals/cat-diarrhoea).

## Is a Bengal right for me?

The GCCF describes a confident, active, curious, athletic and energetic cat that is very sociable, gets on well with children and cat-friendly dogs, and is very vocal. It needs toys, activity centres, tunnels and high shelves; the GCCF advises against letting Bengals roam free, but says they can be trained to walk on a harness. The PDSA adds that they need plenty of mental stimulation. The short coat needs only a light brush once a week. Ask whether the parents have had heart scans and the PK deficiency and PRA DNA tests.
