## How long do Oriental Shorthairs live?

The GCCF, the UK's main cat registry, gives a long life expectancy of 12 to 15 years. There is no figure from vet records: the UK VetCompass life tables for cats that died in 2019 analysed only the 12 breeds with 15 or more deaths, and the Oriental was not among them (Teng et al., 2024).

## What is an Oriental Shorthair?

A Siamese in body, without the Siamese colour pattern. The GCCF explains that the Oriental was developed in the UK from the 1950s from Siamese crosses, starting with the chocolate Havana, and that all members of the Siamese and Oriental group share the same physical standard; what sets the Oriental apart is its wide range of colours and patterns, most with green eyes. For the related breed, see [Siamese](/animals/siamese).

## How big does an Oriental get?

Medium-sized but long and tall. The GCCF describes a long, svelte, firm and well-muscled body on long, slim legs, with a wedge-shaped head, large ears and a long tapering tail, and gives an average weight of 8 to 14 pounds (about 3.6 to 6.4 kg). The PDSA gives an average of 4.6 kg. The very short, fine, glossy coat needs little grooming, though many enjoy being brushed.

## What health problems do Orientals have?

The GCCF says several disorders seem to be associated with the Oriental family, much as in the closely related Siamese:

- **Progressive retinal atrophy (PRA):** an inherited eye disease that causes progressive blindness. Reliable DNA tests are available; ask whether the parents have been tested (GCCF, PDSA).
- **Flat-chested kitten syndrome:** International Cat Care says the chest bone and rib cartilage do not form correctly. It is seen in many breeds but more often in Oriental Shorthairs and Longhairs, Burmese and Bengals. Mild cases may need no treatment; severe cases may need surgery and have a poor outlook.
- **Familial amyloidosis:** a build-up of abnormal protein (amyloid) in the liver, described in certain breeds including the Oriental (GCCF).

## Is an Oriental right for me?

The GCCF describes lively, intelligent, sociable cats that stay playful and kitten-like all their lives, often fetch, jump high and get into every cupboard. Many are very talkative. They do well with children, other cats, dogs and lots of activity, but in general an Oriental does not do well as an only cat, so plan on company. These are not cats for a quiet life.

## Rules in Germany

§ 11b of the German Animal Welfare Act bans breeding when the offspring are expected to suffer because inherited body parts or organs are missing, unsuitable or altered; it names no breeds. The overview table of a 1999 expert report on how to interpret § 11b, written for the federal agriculture ministry, lists the Oriental Shorthair among breeds with white or mostly white cats. For these it recommends a breeding ban for cats whose white coat comes from the dominant W gene, which can cause deafness and sight defects in their offspring, and for cats with hearing or sight problems. This concerns white Orientals only.
