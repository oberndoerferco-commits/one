## How long do Havanese live?

Probably longer than most dogs, but the evidence is limited. A 2024 UK study found a median lifespan of 14.5 years for the Havanese, against 12.0 years for crossbred dogs (McMillan et al., supplementary data). That figure rests on only 381 Havanese, of which 56 had died, so it is uncertain. The Royal Kennel Club gives the breed's lifespan as over 12 years.

## How big does a Havanese get?

Small. The FCI breed standard describes a sturdy little dog, low on its legs, with a body longer than it is tall, and a height at the withers of 23 to 27 cm (tolerance 21 to 29 cm). It gives no weight; the PDSA gives a typical weight of 3 to 6 kg.

## How do I care for a Havanese coat?

The coat is long and needs work. The FCI standard describes a very long topcoat of 12 to 18 cm in adults, soft, flat or wavy and sometimes forming curly strands, with a woolly undercoat that is poorly developed or absent. For the show ring it forbids scissoring and trimming, except tidying the feet and keeping hair out of the eyes. The Royal Kennel Club lists grooming every day and says the coat does not shed.

## What is a Havanese like?

The breed belongs to the Bichon family and, according to the FCI standard, came from the western Mediterranean to Cuba with Italian ship captains. The standard describes an exceptionally bright dog that is easy to train as an alarm dog: affectionate, happy, amiable, a charmer, playful and "even a bit of a clown", that loves children and plays with them endlessly. That is the breeding goal, not a promise for every dog. The PDSA adds that Havanese are small but not delicate and like to be at the centre of family life.

## How much exercise does a Havanese need?

Not much. The Royal Kennel Club gives up to 30 minutes a day and the PDSA around 30 minutes, and the Royal Kennel Club says a flat or apartment is big enough.

## What health problems do Havanese have?

**Eyelashes:** distichiasis means extra eyelashes growing from the edge of the eyelid, where they can rub on the eye. In 1,156 eye-screened Havanese registered in Norway, 14.5% were affected, most of them mildly; the condition was highly heritable (Bellamy et al., 2021). The authors recommend eye screening of all Havanese before breeding. Some affected dogs have pain and damage to the eye, so see your vet if an eye looks sore.

**Legs:** some Havanese have shortened and bowed forelegs, which can be severe. Almost all Havanese tested (355 dogs) carried a gene variant, the FGF4 retrogene, that is known to cause chondrodystrophy (short legs) in dogs; the rare dogs without two copies were on average taller at the shoulder, 31.3 cm against 26.4 cm. The researchers suggest the variant may contribute to the foreleg problems and call for preserving the rarer, healthier gene form (Bellamy et al., 2020). For lameness see [limping in dogs](/animals/dog-limping).

**Other problems** listed by the PDSA: slipping kneecaps, progressive retinal atrophy (a gradual loss of sight) and portosystemic shunt, in which blood bypasses the liver.

## Which health tests matter for a Havanese puppy?

The Royal Kennel Club lists eye testing under the BVA/KC/ISDS Eye Scheme as good practice before breeding and elbow scoring under the BVA/KC Elbow Dysplasia Scheme as best practice; the breed also has a breed club health scheme. Ask to see the parents' eye results.

## Is a Havanese right for me?

Plan for a cheerful, people-loving small dog that needs only modest exercise but daily coat care. Ask for the parents' eye test results and whether either has had foreleg problems.
