## How long do bearded dragons live?

Usually 10 to 15 years or more, according to the RSPCA; VCA gives about 7 to 12 years with proper care. Either way, it is a long commitment.

Bearded dragons come from dry scrubland and woodland in Australia (RSPCA) and grow to around 45 cm including the long tail (RSPCA); VCA gives 45 to 56 cm from nose to tail tip for healthy adults.

## What do bearded dragons eat?

Bearded dragons are omnivores: they eat live insects and plants, and the share of greens grows with age. The RSPCA suggests about 65% livefood and 35% greens for young dragons, and about 40% livefood and 60% greens once they are over 30 cm.

- **Greens:** watercress, rocket, chicory, cress and grated butternut squash are safe (RSPCA). VCA says adult dragons should get most of their nutrition from leafy greens.
- **Avoid:** spinach, which stops calcium being absorbed, and too much cabbage or kale, which can affect hormone production (RSPCA).
- **Livefood:** no bigger than the dragon's mouth, fed a formulated diet for 24–48 hours beforehand (gut-loading), and dusted lightly with a vitamin and mineral powder (RSPCA).
- **How often:** babies twice a day with the greens chopped small; juveniles and adults once a day, in the morning (RSPCA).
- **Water:** a large, shallow dish of clean water at the cool end at all times, changed at least daily (RSPCA).

## What temperature does a bearded dragon need?

A bearded dragon needs a temperature gradient: the RSPCA gives 38–42 °C at the hot, bright end, 22–26 °C at the cool, shaded end, and no lower than 20–22 °C at night.

- VCA gives 32–38 °C at the warm basking end and 24–27 °C at the cool end, and says extra heat at night may not be needed if the cool end stays between 18 and 27 °C.
- Keep the dragon's back at least 25–30 cm from the basking lamp (RSPCA), and use thermostats so heaters cannot burn it (VCA).
- Use a thermometer at each end (VCA). Never use hot rocks: they can become hot enough to burn (VCA).
- Humidity at the cool end should normally be around 30–40% (RSPCA).
- The RSPCA gives at least 120 cm long × 60 cm high × 60 cm wide for one adult. VCA says the enclosure should be at least three times as long as the lizard.

## Does my bearded dragon need UVB light?

Yes. Bearded dragons need UVB to make vitamin D3 in their skin (VCA). The RSPCA recommends a high-output 10–12% UVB tube, giving a UV index of 3.0–5.0 in the basking zone and zero in the shade.

- Albino and similar pale morphs need a maximum UVI of 3.0 in the basking zone (RSPCA).
- A bulb labelled "full spectrum" does not necessarily give enough UVB (VCA).
- UVB must reach the dragon without glass or plastic in between; mesh screens also reduce it (VCA).
- Replace the lamp when the manufacturer recommends (RSPCA); VCA says every six months or as the manufacturer directs, because output falls even while the bulb still lights.
- Lights on for 12 hours and off for 12 hours (RSPCA).

Too little UVB and calcium causes metabolic bone disease, one of the most common problems in captive reptiles (RSPCA). Read more about [metabolic bone disease](/animals/reptile-metabolic-bone-disease).

## Is my bearded dragon brumating or ill?

Bearded dragons naturally slow down, sleep more and eat less in cooler seasons; this is called brumation (RSPCA). But a dragon kept at normal indoor temperatures that is lethargic or not eating may be sick, says VCA, so check with a reptile vet rather than assume brumation.

- Contact your vet if your dragon is losing weight during this time (RSPCA).
- VCA says a dragon should be checked by a reptile vet before brumation, because an unhealthy dragon may wake up critically ill.
- During brumation dragons are usually lethargic, refuse food and probably do not pass droppings, and their skin may look darker (VCA).
- After their growth phase ends at about 10–12 months, dragons eat only every 48–72 hours, so eating less alone is not proof of illness (VCA).

## How do I know my bearded dragon is healthy?

A healthy bearded dragon is aware, active and alert, with clear, bright eyes (VCA, RSPCA).

- The tail near the back legs is round and full, not sunken and bony (VCA).
- The vent is clean, without wetness or droppings stuck to it (VCA).
- Droppings are long and quite firm (RSPCA).

## When should a bearded dragon see a vet?

VCA says any change from normal should be checked by your vet straight away. Go immediately if your dragon sneezes, has discharge from its eyes or nose, bubbles from the mouth or nose, or breathes fast, shallowly or with its mouth open (VCA).

Also see a reptile vet soon if your dragon:

- has a swollen lower jaw or soft jaw ("rubber jaw"), muscle twitching, swollen legs or seizures, signs of metabolic bone disease (VCA, RSPCA)
- has constipation or diarrhoea together with weight loss, which can be due to internal parasites (RSPCA), or foul-smelling droppings (VCA)
- is weak and not eating; atadenovirus, very common in young dragons, can cause sudden death (VCA)
- has old skin stuck on its feet, which can cut off the blood supply and lead to the loss of toes (RSPCA); see [stuck shed](/animals/reptile-stuck-shed)
- is a female that cannot lay her eggs: being egg-bound is a serious condition (RSPCA)
- has pinpoint bleeding on the gums or cheesy material in the mouth, signs of mouth rot (VCA)

VCA also advises an examination by a reptile vet soon after you bring a new dragon home.

## How should I handle my bearded dragon?

Gently scoop it up with both hands so that all four legs are supported, and keep sessions to about 10–15 minutes, depending on the air temperature (RSPCA).

Never surprise or grab your dragon, as this causes stress, and leave a new dragon to settle for the first week before handling it (RSPCA). VCA describes bearded dragons as often curious, docile and responsive to their owners, and says a dragon is usually best housed alone: dragons kept together may compete for food and basking space and fight.

## Salmonella: hygiene with a bearded dragon

Reptiles can carry salmonella, so wash your hands before and after handling your dragon or cleaning its vivarium (RSPCA). That simple habit, plus a few rules, keeps the risk low.

- Use soap and running water, and keep the dragon and its equipment out of the kitchen (CDC).
- Don't kiss the dragon or hold it close to your face (CDC).
- Children under 5, adults over 65 and people with a weakened immune system are more likely to get ill (CDC). Germany's Robert Koch Institute advises not keeping reptiles in households with children under 2.
- Clean the vivarium once a month with a reptile-safe disinfectant (RSPCA).
