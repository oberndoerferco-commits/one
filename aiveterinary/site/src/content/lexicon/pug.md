## How long do Pugs live?

Shorter than the average dog. A 2024 UK study of 12,808 Pugs found a median lifespan of 11.6 years, against 12.0 years for crossbred dogs (McMillan et al.). VetCompass life tables, built from UK vet records, gave Pugs a life expectancy at birth of 7.65 years, among the shortest of the 18 breeds studied, and Pugs were one of the breeds with a much higher chance of dying before adulthood than dogs overall (Teng et al.). The Royal Kennel Club gives the breed's lifespan as over 10 years.

## How big does a Pug get?

The FCI breed standard describes a square, cobby dog, "multum in parvo" (a lot of dog in a small frame), with an ideal weight of 6.3 to 8.1 kg. It says substance "must not be confused with obesity which is unacceptable". Overweight is still common: in VetCompass records it was the most common problem in Pugs (13.2%) (O'Neill et al., 2016), and extra weight makes breathing harder (RSPCA).

## Why do Pugs have breathing problems?

Because of the flat face. The skull and nose are shortened, but the soft tissue inside is not, so it is squeezed into a much smaller space. The UFAW genetic welfare database describes narrowed nostrils and a long, thick soft palate; breathing harder all the time then causes further narrowing and can lead to collapse of the larynx. This is brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome (BOAS).

BOAS starts early in life, lasts for life and gets worse over time. It causes snoring, noisy and open-mouth breathing, fast breathing and struggling for breath, and can lead to collapse and death. Affected dogs cannot take even moderate exercise, are very prone to heatstroke and sleep badly. According to UFAW, to some extent all Pugs may be affected because of their severe flat face, although how badly varies. Dogs with BOAS also have a much higher risk of dying under anaesthesia.

The VetCompass numbers are stark. Comparing 4,308 Pugs with 21,835 other dogs, Pugs had 54 times the odds of a BOAS diagnosis and 51 times the odds of narrowed nostrils (O'Neill et al., 2022). The authors think the true rate of BOAS is much higher than recorded. They conclude that the Pug has diverged so far from other breeds that it "can no longer be considered as a typical dog from a health perspective".

Signs the RSPCA lists include noisy breathing (wheezing, snorting), sleeping sitting up or with the head raised, sleeping with the tongue out, being unable or unwilling to exercise, "reverse sneezing" and difficulty eating. Snorts, snores and loud breathing are not "normal Pug noises": the PDSA says to talk to a vet if you hear them. To help a flat-faced dog, the RSPCA advises:

- check its breathing every day, especially during and after exercise and in warm weather
- keep exercise steady and regular, and walk early or late on warm days
- use a harness instead of a collar
- keep it at a healthy weight
- clean and dry the skin folds every day

Heatstroke is an emergency: see [heatstroke in dogs](/animals/heatstroke). A dog that is struggling to breathe needs a vet straight away.

## What other health problems do Pugs have?

**Eyes:** in the 2022 VetCompass study, Pugs had 13 times the odds of corneal ulcers. The UFAW database describes the cause: shallow eye sockets and large, bulging eyes, eyelids that may not close fully, a nose fold and lashes that rub on the eye, and poor tears. This can lead to constant pain and loss of sight; probably all Pugs have it to some degree. Because so little of the eye sits inside the skull, a minor knock can push the eye out of its socket, which is an emergency.

**Skin folds:** the wrinkled skin of the face, and the folds around the screw tail, trap moisture and secretions and become irritated and infected. UFAW calls skin fold dermatitis common in puppies and many adults.

**Spine:** malformed, wedge-shaped spinal bones (hemivertebrae) are linked to the screw tail. They can press on the spinal cord and cause pain, wobbliness, loss of hind-leg function and incontinence (UFAW).

**Other problems** in VetCompass records were corneal disorders (8.7%) and ear infections (7.5%) (O'Neill et al., 2016). The PDSA also lists dry eye, eyelid problems, slipping kneecaps, elbow and hip dysplasia, and Pug dog encephalitis, a brain inflammation that causes seizures, blindness and difficulty walking and can be fatal.

## Is there a breathing test for Pugs?

Yes. Under the Respiratory Function Grading Scheme of the Royal Kennel Club and the University of Cambridge, a trained vet listens to the dog's breathing at rest and after three minutes of brisk exercise and gives a grade from 0 (free of BOAS signs) to 3 (severe signs; not to be bred from). Dogs are assessed from 12 months of age and every two years. Pugs entering Crufts 2026 need a valid grade, and dogs graded 2 or 3 may not attend. The Royal Kennel Club lists this breathing test and an eye test as good practice before breeding, and a DNA test for Pug dog encephalitis and hip scoring as best practice.

## Is a Pug right for me?

Think about the breed's health first. The PDSA says that, because of how severe the Pug's health problems can be and their effect on quality of life, it always recommends thinking carefully before buying or rescuing one. The RSPCA calls caring for a flat-faced dog a big responsibility that can affect you financially and emotionally.

If you still choose a Pug, the PDSA advises looking for a dog with a longer nose and fewer skin folds on the face, whose parents have had the relevant health tests. The FCI standard itself asks for "fairly large well opened nostrils" and calls pinched nostrils unacceptable. The standard describes great charm, dignity and intelligence and an even-tempered, happy and lively dog; that only tells you what breeders aim for. Plan for a harness, careful exercise in cool weather, daily eye and skin fold checks, weekly grooming of a shedding coat, and ask for both parents' breathing grades.

## Rules in Germany

German law bans breeding vertebrates when it is to be expected that the offspring will suffer pain, suffering or harm because inherited body parts or organs are missing, unsuitable or altered (§ 11b Tierschutzgesetz). Dogs with such features may also not be exhibited, nor take part in other events where dogs are compared or judged (§ 10 Tierschutz-Hundeverordnung). The law describes the harm and does not list breeds.
