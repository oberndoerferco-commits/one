Where AI belongs in a house that works by hand

The first edition: what the tools can actually do now, what the other houses learned the expensive way, and the one line this house should not cross.

3 September 2026 Edition 01 Next brief: Monday

What matters this week

Three findings

01

Hold one line: AI may stage, animate, translate and organise — it may never depict the hand, the artisan, or the grain of the leather. Gucci (Milan, February 2026) and Valentino (December 2025) both put generative imagery in front-facing campaigns and were told by their own audiences that it looked cheap and lazy. For a house whose entire claim is made by hand around Milan, the downside is asymmetric.

02

From 2 August 2026, EU AI Act Article 50 requires disclosure of AI-generated content — and you sell into the EU. Labelling costs nothing in reach: no major platform demotes a post for carrying the AI label. An unlabelled AI image that gets caught by C2PA detection does cost trust.

03

The cheapest real win is room-staging for the furniture. A DTC sofa brand replaced €4,000–6,000-per-product location shoots with AI room scenes and measured conversion within 5% of professional photography. The sofas, poufs and trunk-tables are exactly this case: one real photograph of the piece, many rooms built around it.

The rule

Where the line sits

The question worth answering is not which tool, but which side of the object it stands on. Everything below is sorted by risk to the house's central claim.

It misrepresents the material you sell, and it is the one thing the buyer inspects on arrival.
Disclosure, in practice. Where a published image contains generated pixels, say so plainly — "room rendered digitally; the piece is photographed" costs nothing and reads as confidence. Most tools now embed C2PA credentials by default, so leaving the label intact is easier than stripping it out.

The other houses

Who is using it, and who got burned

Hermès — refuses it, loudly

Commissioned the artist Sarah Martinon to illustrate its website over several months rather than generate the visuals in seconds, and refuses to put customer service on chatbots.

The refusal is itself the marketing. You are small enough to make the same promise credibly — and to say it out loud.

LVMH — everywhere except the object

An internal "AI Factory" serves Dior, Louis Vuitton and Tiffany with forecasting, personalisation and creative production. Louis Vuitton's design team generates hundreds of pattern and colour variations; creative directors select; artisans then make the real thing by hand.

The working model: AI upstream of the hand, never in place of it.

Gucci and Valentino — punished for it

Gucci's AI-generated imagery for its PRIMAVERA show at Milan Fashion Week (February 2026) drew comments calling it cheap, lazy and out of step with a brand built on Italian craftsmanship. Valentino got the same reaction for a clearly labelled AI video for the DeVain bag in December 2025.

Disclosure protects you legally, not reputationally. The content itself still has to be defensible.

The research finding behind all of it

Consumers tie luxury authenticity to perceived effort. When a luxury brand discloses AI in an advertisement, the advertisement is judged less authentic — a penalty specific to this category.

Which is the argument for keeping AI in staging and logistics, where no claim about effort is being made in the first place.

Furniture — the volume end is already moving

One furniture brand reported a 47% conversion lift and $180,000 saved annually after switching to AI product photography; the DTC sofa brand above measured AI room scenes within 5% of professional shots. No public AI configurator or AR programme could be verified at Poltrona Frau, Minotti or B&B Italia.

Your differentiation is not refusing AI for the room. It is refusing it for the object.
