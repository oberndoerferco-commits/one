II

The break is at the last step, not the first

People find you, they add pieces to a cart, they reach the checkout — and then, for twelve months, essentially none of them buy. That single fact outranks every idea in this document.

III

Two checks worth more than any AI project

Both take under an hour. Until they're done, every other number here is being read through a dirty lens.

01

Put a real order through the checkout yourself

Twenty-nine people started checkout in the last three months and none finished. There are two honest explanations and they lead to opposite actions. Either the checkout is failing — a payment method that isn't live for the card types your buyers hold, a duty or address step that rejects them — or those twenty-nine were bots, which is what the pattern suggests: all of them arrived as “direct”, while 421 real Instagram visitors and 233 from Google produced not a single checkout start between them.

Test it the way a customer would: a real card, on a phone, from outside Germany, on a piece over €1,000. If it goes through, you have a traffic-quality problem. If it doesn't, you have found the reason for the last twelve months in twenty minutes.

02

Stop counting yourself as a customer

157 of your GA4 sessions in three months came in via admin.shopify.com — that's you, previewing your own store. It is 11% of everything you measure, and it is concentrated on exactly the pages you're trying to judge. Add an internal-traffic filter in GA4 before you use any of this to make a decision.

V

Where AI actually pays, in order

Ranked by return on your time, given what the data above shows. The first one is worth more than the other four together.

01
Highest return

Rebuild the product data in German and Italian, driven by your own search data

You rank on page three to five for commercial queries describing products you actually make, in the two languages where your demand is strongest. The traffic is being offered to you and the listings aren't good enough to take it.

The proof that this works is already in your own account. Your free Google Shopping listings earned roughly 115 clicks in three months, and the best-performing entries are the ones with proper German and Italian titles — Uhrenbox, Krokodil Geldbörse, Portafoglio coccodrillo — not the English product names. Where you speak the customer's language, you get the click. Most of the catalogue still doesn't.

This is the exact shape of work AI is genuinely better at than either a freelancer or a translation tool: 267 products across three languages, each needing a title, a description, a meta title, a meta description, image alt text and structured data — roughly five thousand pieces of copy. A model can be driven directly from your Search Console export, so each one leads with the phrase people actually type, in your established voice, rather than a guess. And it is repeatable: pull the query data, regenerate, write back through the Shopify API, measure six weeks later.

One specific, cheap fix inside this: your image alt text currently reads “Oberndorfer 3p green ivory”. You sell visual objects to people who search visually. That's a wasted slot on every photograph in the catalogue.

02
Unglamorous, high leverage

Stop your own pages competing with each other

The same product is indexed under as many as nine locale paths, and Google is being asked to choose a winner among your own URLs. It is choosing none of them.

Search Console shows your catalogue split across /products/, /de/, /de-de/, /de-it/, /en-de/, /en-it/, /it/, /it-it/ and /it-de/. One belt appears at /it-de/products/classic-leather-belt in position 44 and at /it/products/classic-leather-belt in position 43 — together 206 impressions and no clicks, each version holding the other down.

AI's role here is narrow and real: auditing hreflang tags and canonical URLs across 267 products is exactly the kind of tedious pattern-matching it does reliably and you shouldn't do by hand. The judgement call — which markets you genuinely serve and ship to — stays yours, and it has to be made before play 01 is worth doing, or you'll write beautiful copy nine times over.

03
New channel, already live

Be the answer an assistant gives, not just a link it might cite

41 visits came from ChatGPT last quarter, plus a trickle from Perplexity — with no effort from you. For a house whose own name draws about 70 searches a quarter, that is a more realistic route to a buyer than outranking the maisons.

What gets you cited is machine-readable specificity: materials, dimensions, lead times, what can be customised, price, provenance — as structured product data and plain answers, not atmosphere. Your watch-box copy already does this well: wood core, floccato velvet lining, memory-foam pillows resizable on request, solid brass closure, made in Milan. That paragraph is exactly what an assistant can use to recommend you. Almost nothing else in the catalogue reads like it.

The work is extending that level of concrete detail across the live products and exposing it as structured data — a job that is mechanical enough for AI and specific enough that only you know the facts to feed it.

04
Fits how you actually sell

Answer enquiries like a Milanese showroom — in three languages, within the hour

Your one genuine abandoned checkout was $974 for BAG MODEL 017, from a customer in the United States. Nobody puts €13,170 on a card at two in the morning without speaking to a person first.

With 129 live products held at an inventory of one each, and prices from €125 to €34,250, your transaction is a conversation, not a cart. Yet every piece over €2,000 currently offers a buyer nothing but “add to cart”. Add a visible way to enquire about a specific piece, and let AI do the part that's genuinely hard for one person across three languages: fast, correct, personal first drafts written against your real product facts and stock, for you to read and send.

Note the shape of that: a drafting assistant behind your inbox, not a chatbot on the storefront. At this price point, a customer who realises they're talking to a bot is a customer you've lost.

05
With a hard limit

Use generative tools for the campaign, never for the object

421 Instagram sessions and zero checkouts means you have attention and no path leading away from it. Production is cheap now; the missing piece is what the attention is meant to do next.

Atmosphere, motion, campaign framing, layout, the fifteen variations of a format you'd never pay a studio to shoot — these are worth doing with the generative tools you already have connected, at a fraction of studio cost.

The limit is absolute and it is commercial, not squeamish: never generate an image of the product itself. You sell handmade objects whose entire value proposition is that they are real and singular. A rendered bag that differs from the one that ships is a trust problem with your highest-value customers and, under EU consumer law, a compliance one. The atmosphere can be synthetic. The object is photographed.

VI

What not to point AI at

Each of these is a plausible-sounding use that would cost you time, or actively damage something that's working.

  • ✕More brand copyThe voice on the site is good and it is distinctly yours. It is also finished. More of it dilutes the asset and moves nothing, because copy quality is not what's stopping the sale.
  • ✕Blog posts as an SEO strategyYou already have them. “Why real leather is more sustainable than eco leather” sits at position 79 with 157 impressions and no clicks. Volume won't rescue that; the product pages are where your demand actually is.
  • ✕A storefront chatbotNot before check 01 is done. A conversational layer on a checkout that may not complete simply gets you closer to a customer at the moment you fail them.
  • ✕Generated product photographySee play 05. This is the one hard line.
  • ✕Paid advertising, for nowYou have no ad accounts connected and that is the right call today. Paying to send more people into a funnel that converts at 0.7% of checkouts would only make the loss faster.
VII

The order to do it in

Sequenced deliberately: each week's work depends on the one before it being settled. This is a real dependency chain, not a schedule.

Week 1

Clear the fog

  • Place a real order through your own checkout, from a phone, outside Germany, over €1,000.
  • Add an internal-traffic filter in GA4 so your admin visits stop counting.
  • Decide which markets you genuinely ship to and want to rank in.
→ The market decision gates everything after this.
Week 2

Consolidate the URLs

  • One canonical URL per product per market; correct hreflang across the set.
  • Retire the locale paths for markets you decided against.
→ Do this before writing copy, or you write it nine times.
Week 3

Rewrite the product data — the earning 30, not all 267

  • German and Italian titles, descriptions and meta for the products already drawing impressions.
  • Real alt text on every image of those products.
  • Start with uhrenbox: position 1, 111 impressions, zero clicks. The listing is the only thing wrong.
Week 4

Open the conversation

  • An enquiry path on every piece over €2,000, alongside the cart.
  • Multilingual reply drafting set up against your real product facts and stock.
Week 10

Measure the right thing

  • In Search Console, check impressions and average position on the German and Italian commercial queries.
  • Not sessions, and not revenue yet — position moves first, and it moves before money does.

What I couldn't determine from the data

Three things change the plan meaningfully, and only you can answer them.

Were the 29 checkout starts people or bots?The pattern points to bots — every one arrived as “direct” while your real Instagram and Google visitors started none. But I can't see the sessions themselves, and the answer decides whether week 1 is a repair job or a traffic-quality job.
Is the €10 order yours?Order #1002 was paid and fulfilled on 11 August. If it was a genuine customer, the checkout works and everything shifts toward traffic. If it was your test, it tells us only that the plumbing was connected that day.
What are the 138 draft products?More than half the catalogue is unpublished, including a fine-jewellery line priced from €9,600 to €27,630 and a full apparel range. Whether those are arriving, abandoned, or deliberately held back changes what the store is supposed to be — and therefore what any of this should be optimised for.
