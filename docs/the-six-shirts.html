The Six Shirts

The T-shirt copy is the best writing in the store, and it is already close to how Loro Piana, Hermès and Brunello Cucinelli write. Four things stand between it and them, and two of the six names are the largest of them.

Six models, twelve listings, read as published

Four things between this and the houses

Finding one / naming

Three of the six names describe the same word

Gothic, Serigraph and Milano are all shirts whose mark is the word MILANO, set three ways. The names do not tell them apart, the typography does, and only one of the three names refers to what is actually printed. Gothic names a class of typeface. Serigraph names a printing process. A customer reading a collection page has to open all three to find out which is which.

Neither of the two houses that name products does this. Loro Piana names from its own world, the roads and the walks. Hermès names a scarf after its subject, Brides de Gala, never after the silk screen it was printed on. Crest, Wordmark and Star already follow that rule: each names the thing on the shirt. Gothic and Serigraph name the method.

Finding two / material

"100% cotton" is the weakest line in the lane

It closes all twelve descriptions, and it is the one sentence none of the three houses would let stand. Not one of them leaves a material as a category.

Sandal in Epsom calfskin

Hermès, Oran

The refined, iconic yarn of the Brunello Cucinelli collections elevates the essential feel of this new cashmere sweater.

Brunello Cucinelli, cashmere sweater

Epsom is a leather. Feather yarn and Diamond yarn are Cucinelli's own. The Gift of Kings is Loro Piana's. A T-shirt's version of that is the cloth: its weight, whether the cotton is combed, what the jersey is. A 240g combed cotton jersey does work that 100% cotton cannot, and it is the only line in the lane where the garment itself, rather than the graphic, gets to be the subject.

Needs a real number

The weights and yarn below are written as placeholders in amber. They have to come from the maker before any of this is published, because a wrong gram weight is worse than no gram weight.

Finding three / structure

Two of every five words are repeated across all twelve listings

Shaded text below appears verbatim in every shirt in the lane:

A cotton T-shirt in black, with the house crest embroidered at the centre of the chest, the ornament above OBERNDÖRFER MILANO, stitched in ivory thread. A regular cut with a ribbed collar. The woven Oberndörfer label is sewn inside the collar, and a small lockup sits at the back hem. The crest is the same four-pointed ornament that appears on the house's leather goods and hardware. 100% cotton. Made in Italy.

Shaded: identical in all twelve listings

Hermès never repeats a shared fact in prose. The cut, the collar, the label, the composition and the origin are the same on every shirt, so they belong in a labelled block underneath, where they can be scanned and compared. What is left in the sentences is only what differs, which is the mark. The prose gets shorter and the lane gets easier to keep true.

Finding four / catalogue

Six shirts are listed as twelve products

Black and white are separate listings with separate handles. Loro Piana does this too, so it is defensible, but Loro Piana is choosing between Tapioca, Stone Dove and Pine Bark, where this lane is choosing between black and white. The cost is real: twelve descriptions to keep consistent instead of six, reviews and search relevance split across pairs, and a collection page that shows the same six shirts twice.

The rewrite below is written so it works either way. If the pairs are merged, the colour sentence becomes a variant line and everything else is unchanged.

The six, rewritten

What changed. "Painted, not drawn" was the best line in the lane and it stops one clause too early. Saying what the brush actually leaves behind is the Loro Piana construction sentence.

A T-shirt in 240g combed cotton jersey, in black. MILANO runs across the shoulders in blackletter, printed in ivory, with the house mark at the chest as a diamond badge. A small lockup sits at the back hem. Blackletter is the lettering of the cathedral the word names, cut in stone five centuries before it was cut in type.

Cloth240g combed cotton jersey, 100% cotton
CutRegular, ribbed collar
MarkPrint, ivory, across the shoulders

Why the name. Gothic named the typeface. Duomo names why the typeface is on the shirt: Milan's cathedral is the city's Gothic monument, and it is the one building the word MILANO in blackletter can be pointing at. It is also the Loro Piana move exactly, a name taken from the house's own geography rather than from its production methods, and it frees the word "Milano" for the shirt that actually is one.

A T-shirt in 240g combed cotton jersey, in black. Across the back, OBERNDÖRFER is printed over MILANO in blackletter, the two marks laid through each other so that neither reads first. The house badge sits at the chest.

Why the name. Serigraph is the process, and it is also the process used on four of the six shirts, so it never distinguished this one. Overprint names what you see, which is one mark laid over another. It also removes the second shirt whose name pointed at blackletter, so Duomo now owns that register alone.

A T-shirt in 240g combed cotton jersey, in black. MILANO runs across the back in amber serif capitals with OBERNDÖRFER set above it, and the house badge sits at the chest. The amber is mixed to the tone of the brass the house uses for its clasps and buckles, so the shirt and the hardware read as one colour.

What changed. The amber sentence was already the best link in the lane and it was stated passively. Mixed to the tone of the brass is a claim about a decision someone made, which is the kind the houses make.

What not to touch

  • Black and white as colour names. Loro Piana would call them something, but Loro Piana is separating six greys. Two colours, named plainly, is honest, and inventing Ivory and Onyx here would be the one move in this document that reads as pretending. Hermès sells Noir and Blanc.
  • The definite article. The Crest T-Shirt is doing real work, it turns a description into a name, and it is the reason this lane already reads as a house and the leather lane does not.
  • The four kept names. Crest, Wordmark, Star and Milano each name the mark on the shirt. That is the rule, and they already follow it.
  • The house-link sentence. Crest and Milano both end by tying the shirt to the leather goods. That is the single most valuable sentence in the lane, because it is the only one that says these are not merchandise.

Order of work

  1. Get the cloth spec from the maker. Weight in grams, combed or carded, jersey type. Everything else here is writing, this is the only thing that needs an answer from outside.
  2. Decide the two renames. Gothic to Duomo and Serigraph to Overprint change product handles, so they need redirects from the old URLs. Two products, four listings.
  3. Decide whether black and white merge. This is a bigger change than the copy and it can wait, but it is cheaper to decide before rewriting twelve descriptions than after.
  4. Write the facts block into the theme, not into the description field, so the cut, collar, label, composition and origin are one string the whole lane shares.
  5. Then the six descriptions, which by that point are three sentences each.

Standing

All of this is staging-theme work. Nothing here changes a price, and the renames are the only part that touches anything a customer has bookmarked.
